\section{Expected Shortfall Computation and Heuristic Correction}
\label{app:es}
%============================================================

This appendix reports the Expected Shortfall (ES) computations used for
supplementary diagnostics. The conformal framework in the main text is
developed for Value-at-Risk (VaR). ES is reported at $\alpha=0.025$,
following the Basel~III/FRTB convention \citep{bcbs2019frtb}, while the
conformal correction is calibrated on the 1\% VaR threshold. ES results are
therefore interpreted as auxiliary diagnostics; they do not carry conformal
validity guarantees.

\subsection{Expected Shortfall Computation and Heuristic Adjustment}
\label{app:es_raw}

For each base forecaster, raw ES is computed from the same predictive object
that produces the VaR forecast. For sample-based forecasters
(Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini, Moirai~1.1, and Lag-Llama), raw ES is the
empirical conditional tail mean,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_raw_es_sample}
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}(0.025)
	=
	-\frac{1}{\lfloor 0.025 N\rfloor}
	\sum_{i:\,\hat r_t^{(i)}\le \hat q_t(0.025)}
	\hat r_t^{(i)}.
\end{equation}
With $N=1{,}000$, this average is computed from the 25 lowest predictive
draws.

For quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0), raw ES is
computed from the same fitted Student-$t$ distribution used for VaR
tail-completion. Let $(\hat\nu_t,\hat\mu_t,\hat\sigma_t)$ denote the fitted
parameters. The lower-tail conditional expectation is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_raw_es_student}
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}(0.025)
	=
	-\Biggl(
	\hat\mu_t
	-
	\hat\sigma_t
	\frac{
		f_{\hat\nu_t}\bigl(t^{-1}_{\hat\nu_t}(0.025)\bigr)
		\left[
		\hat\nu_t+
		\bigl(t^{-1}_{\hat\nu_t}(0.025)\bigr)^2
		\right]
	}{
		(\hat\nu_t-1)\cdot 0.025
	}
	\Biggr),
\end{equation}
where $f_\nu(\cdot)$ and $t_\nu^{-1}(\cdot)$ denote the density and quantile
functions of the standard Student-$t$ distribution. The restriction
$\hat\nu_t>2$ ensures a well-defined scale.
For classical benchmarks, ES is computed from the model's native conditional
distribution: skewed-$t$ ES for GJR-GARCH
\citep{hansen1994autoregressive}, Gaussian ES for GARCH-Normal and EWMA,
and the empirical tail mean of the 250-day rolling window for Historical
Simulation.

The conformal correction shifts the predicted lower quantile by $\qVstat$, so as a supplementary diagnostic we apply the same location shift to ES:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_es_cp_shift}
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{cp}}
	=
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}+\qVstat,
	\qquad
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{roll}}
	=
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}+\qVroll.
\end{equation}
Under the positive-loss convention, $\qVstat>0$ indicates that the base
model underestimates tail risk, so both VaR and ES increase.
This adjustment preserves the location-shift interpretation of the VaR
correction but does not address tail-shape misspecification.

\subsection{Why Joint VaR--ES Conformal Recalibration Is Outside Scope}
\label{app:es_obstacles}

A formal conformal treatment of ES is more delicate than the VaR correction
studied in the main text. ES is not elicitable as a standalone functional
\citep{gneiting2011making}. Although $(\VaR,\ES)$ is jointly elicitable
\citep{fissler2016higher}, a joint conformal construction would require a
nonconformity score that accounts simultaneously for violation frequency and
tail-loss magnitude, introducing additional modelling choices.

Joint VaR--ES recalibration also creates a conditioning mismatch: raw ES
errors are evaluated on days defined by raw VaR violations, whereas corrected
ES would be evaluated on days defined by corrected VaR violations. Because
the conformal shift changes the violation set, the relevant tail-loss samples
need not coincide. ES is also more sensitive to tail sparsity than VaR,
since it depends on the average severity beyond the threshold rather than
the threshold alone; under temporal dependence, ES residuals therefore
inherit a more complex dependence structure than the one-sided VaR score.
For these reasons, joint VaR--ES conformal validity under temporal
dependence is left for future work.

\subsection{Empirical Diagnostic}
\label{app:es_empirical}

ES backtesting uses the Acerbi--Sz\'{e}kely $Z_2$ statistic. Let
$V_t=\mathbf{1}\{r_t<-\widehat{\VaR}_t\}$ denote the VaR violation
indicator. The statistic is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_z2}
	Z_2
	=
	\frac{1}{T\alpha}
	\sum_{t=1}^{T}
	\frac{V_t r_t}{\widehat{\ES}_t}
	+1 .
\end{equation}
with $\widehat{\ES}_t$ entered as a \emph{positive magnitude}. Under correct
joint tail specification $Z_2$ is close to zero, and the test is one-sided:
it rejects for $Z_2$ sufficiently negative, meaning realised losses on violation
days exceed the forecast ES.

\paragraph{The sign convention, and why it matters here}
Equation~\eqref{eq:app_z2} requires $\widehat{\ES}_t$ to enter as a positive
magnitude. Everything in this subsection is $Z_2$ as
equation~\eqref{eq:app_z2} defines it, with the forecast ES inside the sum, and
not the time-averaged variant $Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}$ of
equation~\eqref{eq:app_z2_mod} that the pipeline implements; the argument here is
about the published definition, so it is computed from the published definition.
The forecast files store a lower-tail quantity, which is negative, so
dividing by the stored column directly maps $Z_2$ to $2 - Z_2$, returning a large
positive statistic --- $+\nSupZTwoFlippedSmall$ and $+\nSupZTwoFlippedMini$ on the
two Chronos series sampled at $\texttt{top\_k} = 50$. A one-sided lower-tail test
cannot reject such a value, so the routine would report \nSupZTwoAssets{} of
\nSupZTwoAssets{} assets passing on series whose violation rate at the same level
is \nSupZTwoPiSmall{} and \nSupZTwoPiMini{}.

Entered as a magnitude, the median $Z_2$ on those series is
$\nSupZTwoSmall$ and $\nSupZTwoMini$ and the test rejects on all
\nMainAssets{} assets. These are not the figures in
Table~\ref{tab:es_correction}, which reports $Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}$ and gives
$\nSupZTwoModMeanSmall$ and $\nSupZTwoModMeanMini$; the difference is the
denominator and not the aggregation, since taking medians of
$Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}$ returns $\nSupZTwoModMedianSmall$ and
$\nSupZTwoModMedianMini$ and closes none of it. The two statistics coincide only
if $\widehat{\ES}_t$ is constant, and on Chronos-Mini they differ by a factor of
$\nSupZTwoRatioMini$ because its per-day forecast ES is not. Both are computed in
\texttt{analysis/provenance/verify\_z2.py}, and every figure in this subsection
is $Z_2$. This is the same sign ambiguity
that the validation gate of Supplement~\ref{app:validation_gate} checks for in the
forecast series, arising here in an evaluation routine instead, and it is worth
stating explicitly for two reasons. First, a structural check on the forecast
series would not have caught it: the series are well-formed and it is the
statistic that is misassembled. Second, it is verified by an independent second
implementation written from the published definition rather than by re-reading
the first (\texttt{analysis/provenance/verify\_z2.py}), which is the only way a
sign convention of this kind is reliably established.

Read on the corrected panel, $Z_2$ does discriminate at this level. It rejects
the two truncated Chronos series on all \nMainAssets{} assets raw and on
\nMainZTwoRejCorrTrunc{} of
\nMainAssets{} after the heuristic ES correction, while the remaining forecasters
pass on between \nMainZTwoPassRawMin{} and \nMainAssets{} assets raw and on
\nMainZTwoPassCorrMin{} or \nMainAssets{}
corrected. The heuristic correction reduces $|Z_2|$ for every forecaster, sharply
so for the truncated series, whose raw values of $-130$ and $-163$ reflect
realised losses on violation days far in excess of a predictive distribution
whose standard deviation is \nDispDefaultSmall{} of realised volatility. Two limitations should be read
alongside this. ES over-prediction inflates the denominator and can mask
under-coverage, so a pass is weaker evidence than a rejection; and because the ES
correction is heuristic, $Z_2$ is interpreted here only as a diagnostic of
whether reported ES forecasts are broadly consistent with realised tail losses.

conditional calibration tests, are left for future work.

(Table~\ref{tab:fz_scores}) produces rankings consistent with the Quantile
Score results: parametric benchmarks dominate the recalibrated TSFMs, with
Lag-Llama the closest competitor on the joint score.

\subsection{Baseline Specifications}
\label{app:baseline-specs}

\paragraph*{Single-shot post-hoc corrections}
Five baselines apply a single calibration map fitted on the
calibration split.

\textit{Historical quantile replacement} discards the base forecast
and returns the empirical $\alpha$-quantile of the calibration-window
returns,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:hq}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{HQ}}
	=
	-\,Q_{\alpha}\!\left(\{r_\tau\}_{\tau\in\mathcal C}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal C$ is the calibration set. Zero free parameters; the
base model is ignored entirely.

\textit{Scale correction} multiplies the raw forecast by a scalar
$c\in\mathbb R_{+}$ chosen so that the rescaled violation rate matches
$\alpha$ over $\mathcal C$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:sc}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{SC}} = c\,\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{raw}},
	\qquad
	c=\arg\min_{c>0}
	\left|
	\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal C}
	\mathbf 1\{r_\tau<-c\,\widehat{\VaR}_\tau^{\mathrm{raw}}\}-\alpha
	\right|.
\end{equation}
One parameter; preserves forecast dynamics but cannot correct level
bias of the wrong sign.

\textit{Linear quantile regression} fits an affine map at level
$\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qr_baseline}
	Q_\alpha(r_t\mid \hat q_t^{lo})
	= a + b\,\hat q_t^{lo},
	\qquad
	(\hat a,\hat b)
	=\arg\min_{a,b}
	\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal C}
	\rho_\alpha\!\left(r_\tau-a-b\,\hat q_\tau^{lo}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\alpha(u)=u(\alpha-\mathbf 1\{u<0\})$ is the pinball loss.
Two parameters: an intercept matching the conformal shift and a slope
that the conformal shift fixes at one.

\textit{Isotonic regression} replaces the linear map with a monotone
nonparametric mapping $\widehat g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, fitted by
pool-adjacent-violators on the calibration scores
$\{(\hat q_\tau^{lo},r_\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal C}$ with $\widehat g$
non-decreasing. Effectively $|\mathcal C|-1$ free parameters; flexible
in principle but unstable at the 1\% tail, where the effective sample
is of order $\alpha T$.

\textit{Adaptive Conformal Inference} (ACI) is sequential: it replaces
the fixed miscoverage level $\alpha$ with an online update
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:aci}
	\alpha_t=\alpha_{t-1}+\gamma\!\left(\alpha-\mathbf 1\{V_t\}\right),
	\qquad \gamma>0,
\end{equation}
where $V_t$ is the realised violation indicator
\citep{gibbs2021adaptive}. One free parameter $\gamma$; adapts the
conformal threshold without distributional assumptions.

\paragraph*{Distributional and nonlinear post-hoc corrections}
Two baselines move beyond scalar or monotone maps.

\textit{GAMLSS-SST} \citep{rigby2005generalized} models the conditional
return distribution as a skewed Student-$t$ density
$\mathcal{SST}(\mu_t,\sigma_t,\nu,\tau)$ with location driven by
$\hat q_t^{lo}$ and log-scale by lagged squared returns:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:gamlss}
	\mu_t=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\hat q_t^{lo},
	\quad
	\log\sigma_t = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\,r_{t-1}^2,
	\quad
	r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{SST}(\mu_t,\sigma_t,\nu,\tau).
\end{equation}
Four parameters $(\beta_1,\gamma_1,\nu,\tau)$ per asset; tests whether
a full conditional density improves on the one-parameter shift.

\textit{Gradient-boosted quantile regression} (GBM-QR) fits a nonlinear
quantile function via boosted regression trees minimising the pinball
loss,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:gbm_qr}
	\widehat f
	=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal F_{\mathrm{GBM}}}
	\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal C}
	\rho_\alpha\!\left(r_\tau-f(x_\tau)\right),
\end{equation}
with features $x_\tau$ comprising $\hat q_\tau^{lo}$ and lagged
realised volatility. The most flexible baseline; tests whether
nonlinearity extracts tail signal that linear methods miss.

\paragraph*{Dedicated VaR models}
Two baselines bypass the post-hoc framing and replace the base
forecast.

\textit{Extreme Value Theory with Peaks-Over-Threshold} (EVT-POT) fits
a generalised Pareto distribution $\mathrm{GPD}(\xi,\beta)$ to
threshold exceedances of GARCH-filtered residuals $\hat z_t$
\citep{mcneil2000estimation}:
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:evt_pot_gpd}
	\Pr(\hat z_t-u>y\mid \hat z_t>u)
	&=\left(1+\xi\,y/\beta\right)^{-1/\xi}, \\
	\label{eq:evt_pot_var}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{POT}}
	&=\hat\sigma_t\!\left[
	u+\tfrac{\beta}{\xi}\!\left((\tfrac{n}{n_u}\alpha)^{-\xi}-1\right)
	\right],
\end{align}
with $u$ the 90th percentile of $|\hat z_t|$, $n_u$ the exceedance
count, and $n$ the window size. Two parameters $(\xi,\beta)$ per
window; the only baseline with an explicit extreme-value tail model.

\textit{Filtered Historical Simulation} (FHS) standardises returns by
GARCH volatility and takes the empirical $\alpha$-quantile of the
standardised residuals \citep{baroneadesi1999var}:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:fhs}
	\hat z_\tau=\hat\varepsilon_\tau/\hat\sigma_\tau,
	\qquad
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{FHS}}
	=\hat\sigma_t\,Q_\alpha\!\left(\{\hat z_\tau\}_{\tau\in\mathcal C}\right).
\end{equation}
Combines GARCH dynamics with a nonparametric tail; inherits GARCH
estimation instability in short windows.

\section{Backtesting Implementation Details}
\label{app:backtesting}
%============================================================

The Kupiec proportion of failures (POF) likelihood ratio statistic
tests $H_0:\hat\pi=\alpha$ against two-sided alternatives
\citep{kupiec1995}. For the boundary case $x=0$, the likelihood
ratio is evaluated using the continuous-limit convention
$(x/T)^x=1$; the $p$-value is computed from $\chi^2_1$.

The Christoffersen conditional coverage test \citep{christoffersen1998}
augments the unconditional coverage test with an independence component.
The joint statistic is $\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{CC}}=
\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}}+\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{IND}}\sim\chi^2_2$.

The $Z_2$ statistic (Eq.~\ref{eq:app_z2}) evaluates Expected Shortfall
consistency. In our implementation, we replace the per-day ES
denominator with the time-averaged ES,
$\overline{\ES}=T^{-1}\sum_{t}\widehat{\ES}_t^\alpha$, giving
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_z2_mod}
	Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}
	=
	\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_t r_t}
	{T\alpha\,\overline{\ES}}
	+1.
\end{equation}
This modification avoids division by near-zero daily ES values. The two
formulations are compared per model in
\texttt{analysis/provenance/z2\_verification.csv}; we report the comparison
qualitatively because a per-pair difference is not stored, and a summary
statistic we cannot recompute is not one we state.


\subsection{Diagnostic Regression Specification}
\label{app:diag_regression_spec}

The diagnostic regression of the Quantile Score improvement on the
conformal threshold and the raw violation rate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dqs_regression}
\Delta\mathrm{QS}_{m,a}
= \beta_0
+ \beta_1\,(\qVstat)_{m,a}
+ \beta_2\,\hat\pi^{\mathrm{raw}}_{m,a}
+ \varepsilon_{m,a},
\end{equation}
estimated across $n = 312$ model--asset pairs $(m,a)$. Standard errors
are computed under one-way clustering by asset (24 clusters) and,
separately, by model (13 clusters). The partial $R^{2}$ for $\qVstat$
is computed by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell residualisation on
$\hat\pi^{\mathrm{raw}}$. Coefficients are reported in Supplement~\ref{app:diag_regression},
Table~\ref{tab:diag_regression}.

\subsection{Diebold--Mariano with Panel HAC}
\label{app:dm_panel}

Quantile Score differences between forecasters $j$ and $k$ are tested
with the \citet{diebold1995comparing} statistic
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dm_stat}
\mathrm{DM}_{jk}
= \frac{\bar d_{jk}}
{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\bar d_{jk})}},
\qquad
d_{jk,t} = \mathrm{QS}^{j}_{t}-\mathrm{QS}^{k}_{t},
\end{equation}
with the \citet{harvey1997testing} small-sample correction. To
accommodate cross-asset dependence, the variance is the
\citet{driscoll1998consistent} panel HAC estimator with a Bartlett
kernel and \citet{newey1994automatic} automatic bandwidth.

\subsection{Panel-Pooled Kupiec}
\label{app:panel_kupiec}

The panel-pooled Kupiec statistic pools the violation series across
the 24 assets, giving approximately $36{,}600$ test observations per
model. Under the null $H_0\colon\hat\pi_{m,a}=\alpha$ for all $a$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:panel_kupiec}
\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}}^{\mathrm{panel}}
=
-2\log\!\left\{
\frac{(1-\alpha)^{N-X}\alpha^{X}}
{(1-X/N)^{N-X}(X/N)^{X}}
\right\},
\quad
X=\sum_{a,t}V_{a,t},
\quad
N=\sum_a T_a,
\end{equation}
distributed asymptotically as $\chi^{2}_1$. Cluster-robust inference
at the asset level is obtained from the same Driscoll--Kraay panel
HAC estimator used for the Diebold--Mariano statistic, with violations
clustered by asset; the implied number of independent clusters is 24.
Wild-cluster bootstrap finite-sample $p$-values are reported in
Supplement~\ref{app:panel_robustness}
(Table~\ref{tab:wildcluster_kupiec}).

\subsection{Monte Carlo Design}
\label{app:mc_design}

Section~5 of the manuscript reports the Monte Carlo study. It uses five
data-generating processes: (i) Normal innovations, corresponding to correct
specification; (ii) Student-$t(5)$; (iii) Student-$t(3)$; (iv) skewed-$t(3,
-0.5)$ \citep{hansen1994autoregressive}; and (v) a mixture of Normals,
$0.95\,\mathcal N(0,1)+0.05\,\mathcal N(0,25)$, all standardised to unit
variance. In every case the forecaster is given the true GARCH parameters and
assumes Normal innovations, so the progression from (i) to (v) introduces
increasing tail misspecification and nothing else. The grid runs
$T\in\{500,1000,2000,5000,10000\}$ at 500 replications per cell, 12{,}500 in
total.

\paragraph{The two smallest calibration samples} At $T = 500$ the calibration
block holds $n = 350$ observations, and at $\alpha = 0.01$ the two quantile
conventions of Section~4.2 of the manuscript are biased in opposite directions
there. The conformal index sits at $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n =
\nSupConfIndex$ rather than $0.99$, an overshoot of $\nSupConfOvershoot$ that
inflates $\qVstat$; the
interpolated empirical quantile at exactly $1-\alpha$ has its own small-sample
downward bias in a right-skewed tail. Measured against a forecaster given the
correct innovation law, so that the population shift is zero, the interpolated
quantile returns a mean of $-\nSupCtlExactSmallTFive$ ($t_5$) and
$-\nSupCtlExactSmallTThree$ ($t_3$) at
$T = 500$, both within or marginally outside a three-standard-error band, and
falls monotonically to $-\nSupCtlExactLargeTFive$ and
$-\nSupCtlExactLargeTThree$ at $T = 10{,}000$. The
conformal convention returns $+\nSupCtlConfSmallTFive$ and
$+\nSupCtlConfSmallTThree$ at $T = 500$, falling to
$+\nSupCtlConfLargeTFive$ and $+\nSupCtlConfLargeTThree$. From $T = 1{,}000$
upward the interpolated quantile is inside the three-standard-error band on
every cell. The $T = 500$ column of
Table~1 of the manuscript is reported with this qualification rather than
dropped.

\paragraph{Reproduction} The published two-sample-size study reproduces exactly
from its 5{,}000 per-replication rows. The grid above was produced by a fresh
implementation, gated on reproducing that study's ten cells on all six reported
quantities before any new sample size was reported; the comparison tolerance is
three standard errors of the \emph{difference} between two independent Monte
Carlo estimates, with the new run at 2{,}000 replications so that the published
run's error dominates it. A systematic bias survives the gate only if it is
inside the band in all \nSupReproCells{} cells, so the smallest
discrepancy the tolerance admits is $\nSupReproTol$ on mean $\qVstat$, at the
loosest cell $\nSupReproTolMax$, and the check establishes
agreement to Monte Carlo resolution and not exact agreement.

\subsection{Gap Parameter Ablation}
\label{app:gap_ablation}

Theorem~4.5 of the manuscript requires a gap of
$g_n \to \infty$ unused observations between calibration and
evaluation, ensuring that the test point is far enough from the
calibration sample for the $\beta$-mixing bound to be tight. The
empirical implementation uses adjacent splits ($g_n = 0$): the
calibration sample ends at time $n$ and the evaluation begins at
$n+1$, with no observations discarded in between. This is the
operational standard in conformal prediction, but is not covered
directly by the theorem. To check whether the gap requirement
matters in practice, we compare two implementations on four
representative model--asset pairs: $g_n = 0$ (the operational
choice) and $g_n = \lceil c \log n \rceil$ (the minimal gap
satisfying the theorem), where $c = 1/|\log \hat\rho|$ and
$\hat\rho$ is the AR(1) coefficient of the calibration scores.

Table~\ref{tab:gap_ablation} shows that the choice of $g_n$ has
negligible empirical impact. The absolute difference in corrected
violation rates is at most \nGapAblFullPP{} percentage points over the full test
window and \nGapAblCovidPP{} in the crisis sub-windows, and Basel Traffic
Light classifications are identical for all four pairs. The
theoretical gap requirement is therefore empirically non-binding
for the \nGapAblPairs{} pairs tested, which span the full-period
score-persistence range $\hat\rho \in [\nGapAblRhoLo, \nGapAblRhoHi]$.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}
%============================================================

Section~4 of the manuscript states the assumption, the two lemmas, the coverage
theorem, the GARCH corollary and the two rolling-estimator bounds, together with
their scope. This appendix carries the proofs, and each is labelled by the
numbering of the statement it proves. No statement is repeated here in a second
form.

For reference while reading them, Assumption~4.1 of the manuscript requires
(A1) that the score process $S_t = \hat q_t^{lo} - r_t$ be strictly stationary
and geometrically $\beta$-mixing, $\beta(k)\le C\rho^k$; (A2) that the marginal
law $F_S$ have a continuous density bounded away from zero near
$q_{1-\alpha}$; and (A3) that the evaluated score be separated from the
calibration block by a gap $g_n\to\infty$ with $g_n/n\to0$.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~4.2 (inheritance of weak dependence)}
\label{app:lem_score_mixing}

\begin{remark}[Why the context must be finite]
	\label{rem:finite_context}
	The finiteness of $m$ is not a convenience. Allowing
	$\hat q_t^{lo}=g(r_{t-1},r_{t-2},\ldots)$ with an infinite context does not
	yield $\beta_S(k)\le\beta_r(k-1)$: the proof requires
	$\sigma(S_s : s\ge t+k)\subseteq\sigma(r_u : u\ge t+k-1)$, and with an
	infinite context $S_s$ depends on returns arbitrarily far before $t+k-1$,
	so the inclusion fails and no separation of the two $\sigma$-fields is
	obtained. Restricting $g$ to a window of length $m$ is what makes the
	blocking argument available, and for all but one forecaster here the
	restriction holds by construction: $m = 512$ for the foundation models and
	$m = 250$ for the GARCH-family and historical-simulation benchmarks
	(Section~3.3.3 of the manuscript).

	EWMA is the exception, and it is stated rather than rounded off. Its
	conditional variance is the RiskMetrics recursion, whose memory is infinite,
	so the lemma does not apply to it as literally implemented; the manuscript
	reconciles this wherever the 250-observation convention is quoted. What can be
	said is quantitative: at $\lambda = 0.94$ the weight beyond lag $k$ is
	$\lambda^k$, which is $1.9\times10^{-7}$ at $k = 250$ and falls below
	double-precision machine epsilon at $k = 583$. The lemma applies to the filter
	truncated at $m = 600$, which is indistinguishable from the recursion in the
	arithmetic that produced the series. That is an approximation argument rather
	than an exemption, and it is the only one in the paper.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
	By assumption, $\{r_t\}$ is strictly stationary. Since $\hat q_t^{lo}$ is
	a time-invariant measurable functional of the past, the joint process
	$(\hat q_t^{lo},r_t)$ is also strictly stationary. Therefore,
	$S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ inherits strict stationarity.
	
	Let $\mathcal S_{-\infty}^t=\sigma(S_s:s\le t)$ and
	$\mathcal S_{t+k}^{\infty}=\sigma(S_s:s\ge t+k)$. By
	equation~(10) of the manuscript, $S_t$ is a measurable function of
	$(r_t,r_{t-1},\ldots,r_{t-m})$, so
	\[
	\mathcal S_{-\infty}^t
	\subseteq
	\mathcal R_{-\infty}^t:=\sigma(r_s:s\le t),
	\]
	and, crucially, the forward field reaches back only $m$ steps:
	\[
	\mathcal S_{t+k}^{\infty}
	\subseteq
	\mathcal R_{t+k-m}^{\infty}:=\sigma(r_s:s\ge t+k-m).
	\]
	It is this second inclusion that fails without a finite context
	(Remark~S.9.1).
	By monotonicity of $\beta$-mixing coefficients under measurable
	transformations,
	\[
	\beta_S(k)
	\le
	\beta(\mathcal R_{-\infty}^t,\mathcal R_{t+k-m}^{\infty})
	=
	\beta_r(k-m),
	\qquad k>m,
	\]
	which is equation~(11) of the manuscript.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~4.4 (empirical quantile deviation)}
\label{app:lem_quantile}

\begin{proof}
	Under geometric $\beta$-mixing as in Assumption~4.1(A1) of the manuscript,
	the empirical CDF satisfies the uniform concentration bound
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_ecdf_bound}
		\sup_{s\in\mathbb R}|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|
		=
		\mathcal O_p\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right);
	\end{equation}
	see \citet[Theorem 2.1]{yu1994rates} and
	\citet[Theorem 7.2]{rio2017asymptotic}.
	 Since $\qVstat$ is the
	empirical $(1-\alpha)$-quantile,
	\[
	\hat F_n(\qVstat)=1-\alpha+\mathcal O(1/n).
	\]
	Thus,
	\[
	|F_S(\qVstat)-(1-\alpha)|
	\le
	\sup_s|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|+\mathcal O(1/n).
	\]
	By the density condition in Assumption~4.1 of the manuscript,
	$f_S(q_{1-\alpha})$ is bounded away from zero in a neighbourhood of
	$q_{1-\alpha}$. A first-order expansion around $q_{1-\alpha}$ gives
	\[
	|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
	\le
	\frac{1}{f_S(q_{1-\alpha})}
	\left[
	\sup_s|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|+\mathcal O(1/n)
	\right],
	\]
	which yields the stated rate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~4.5 (coverage under $\beta$-mixing)}
\label{app:thm_coverage}

\begin{proof}
	The proof proceeds in three steps.
	
	\textit{Step 1: Reduction to score control.}
	By definition of the nonconformity score
	$S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:proof_score_event}
		S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat
		\Longleftrightarrow
		r_{n+g_n+1}\ge \hat q_{n+g_n+1}^{lo}-\qVstat.
	\end{equation}
	It therefore suffices to show
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:proof_target_coverage}
		\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\Delta_n.
	\end{equation}
	
\textit{Step 2: Quantile approximation.}
Let $q_{1-\alpha}=F_S^{-1}(1-\alpha)$. By
Lemma~4.4 of the manuscript,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_qv_rate}
	|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
	=
	\mathcal O_p\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
\end{equation}
By the density condition,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_fs_qv_bound}
	F_S(\qVstat)
	\ge
	1-\alpha
	-
	C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
\end{equation}
for a finite constant $C>0$.

\textit{Step 3: Gap-separated dependence control.}
By $\beta$-mixing, conditional on the calibration sample
$\mathcal D_n=(S_1,\ldots,S_n)$,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_conditional_gap}
	\bigl|
	\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat\mid\mathcal D_n)
	-
	F_S(\qVstat)
	\bigr|
	\le
	\beta(g_n).
\end{equation}
Taking expectations over $\mathcal D_n$ and combining with
\eqref{eq:proof_fs_qv_bound} gives
\[
\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat)
\ge
\mathbb E\!\left[F_S(\qVstat)\right]-\beta(g_n)
\ge
1-\alpha
-
C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
-\beta(g_n).
\]
Setting
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_delta_n}
	\Delta_n
	=
	C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\beta(g_n)
\end{equation}
completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~4.6 (GARCH data-generating processes)}
\label{app:cor_garch}

\begin{proof}
	Under $\omega>0$, $\alpha_1,\beta_1\ge0$, and
	$\mathbb E[\log(\alpha_1\varepsilon_t^2+\beta_1)]<0$, the GARCH(1,1)
	process admits a unique strictly stationary solution
	\citep[Theorem~2.10]{francq2019garch}. Under standard regularity
	conditions, it is geometrically $\beta$-mixing
	\citep{francq2019garch,lindner2009stationarity}:
	\[
	\beta_r(k)\le a\rho^k,
	\qquad \rho\in(0,1).
	\]
	By Lemma~4.2 of the manuscript, the score process is also strictly
	stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing. The density condition holds
	under the assumed continuous innovation density near the target quantile.
	
	Choosing $g_n=c\log n$ with $c>1/|\log\rho|$ gives
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_garch_gap_rate}
		\beta(g_n)\le a\rho^{c\log n}
		=
		\mathcal O(n^{-c|\log\rho|})
		=
		o\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
	\end{equation}
	Theorem~4.5 of the manuscript therefore applies with
	\[
	\Delta_n
	=
	\mathcal O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
	\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~4.7 (online quantile tracking)}
\label{app:ogd_regret}

Proposition~4.7 of the manuscript is deterministic: it holds for every score
sequence, with no probabilistic assumption whatsoever. Its scope relative to the
estimator actually used in the empirical sections is stated after the proof.


\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~4.7 of the manuscript]
	\emph{Step 1: the iterates stay bounded, without projection.} We claim
	$q_t\in[-R-\eta\alpha,\;R+\eta(1-\alpha)]$ for every $t\ge1$. The claim
	holds at $t=1$ by assumption. Suppose it holds at $t$. For the upper end,
	either $q_t\le R$, in which case
	$q_{t+1}\le q_t+\eta(1-\alpha)\le R+\eta(1-\alpha)$; or $q_t>R\ge s_t$, in
	which case $\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}=0$, the update is $-\eta\alpha<0$, and
	$q_{t+1}<q_t$. For the lower end, either $q_t\ge -R$, so
	$q_{t+1}\ge q_t-\eta\alpha\ge -R-\eta\alpha$; or $q_t<-R\le s_t$, so
	$\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}=1$, the update is $+\eta(1-\alpha)>0$, and
	$q_{t+1}>q_t$. In both directions the iterate is pulled back as soon as it
	leaves $[-R,R]$, so no projection step is needed and the range has width at
	most $2R+\eta$.

	\emph{Step 2: telescoping.} Summing the second form of
	equation~(15) of the manuscript over $t=1,\ldots,T$,
	\[
	\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha\right]
	=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(q_{t+1}-q_t\right)
	=q_{T+1}-q_1 .
	\]
	Dividing by $\eta T$ and applying Step 1,
	\[
	\left|\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha\right|
	=\frac{|q_{T+1}-q_1|}{\eta T}
	\le\frac{2R+\eta}{\eta T},
	\]
	which is equation~(16) of the manuscript. Substituting $\eta=R/\sqrt T$ gives
	$\eta T=R\sqrt T$ and the stated rate.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What this argument replaces, and what it does not deliver]
	\label{rem:ogd_scope}
	A bound of this form is often obtained by invoking the
	online-convex-optimisation regret guarantee of \citet{zinkevich2003online}
	and converting average regret on the pinball loss into average miscoverage.
	That conversion requires a local Lipschitz argument in the indicator
	parametrisation and does not follow from the regret bound alone. The proof
	above needs none of it: the update telescopes exactly, and the only work is
	showing the iterates cannot escape a bounded interval. The constant is
	explicit and the statement is pathwise.

	Three limitations should be read alongside it. It controls the
	\emph{time-average} of the coverage indicator, not conditional coverage and
	not coverage over any subwindow: a sequence can satisfy
	equation~(16) of the manuscript while violating badly in one regime and
	over-covering in another, which is exactly the behaviour
	Section~6 of the manuscript warns about. It requires bounded scores, which
	rules out nothing empirically but is an assumption. And, most importantly
	for this paper, it is a statement about the \emph{online update}
	equation~(15) of the manuscript, whereas the rolling estimator used throughout the
	empirical sections is a trailing-window empirical quantile, which is not
	literal online gradient descent. In stationary regimes the two target the
	same population quantile; under drift the window trades bias for variance
	near transitions. We therefore cite this bound for what it is --- a
	dependence-free guarantee for one estimator in the family --- and not as
	cover for the estimator we run.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~4.8 (finite-window coverage under drift)}
\label{app:rolling_drift}

Proposition~4.8 of the manuscript is a supporting bound. Its conclusion holds
under the total-variation drift measure $\delta_w(t)$ of equation~(17) of the
manuscript, which is not estimable from a single path without further
assumptions, and the constant it produces is too loose to be informative at
$\alpha = 0.01$. It is retained because it makes precise which quantity has to be
small for a trailing window to work; $\hat\delta_w(t)$ below is the operational
monitoring diagnostic that replaces it in practice.


\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~4.8 of the manuscript]
	Let $S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ and let $\qVroll$ be the trailing-window
	conformal shift of equation~(9) of the manuscript, the
	$\lceil (w+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th order statistic of
	$\{S_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}$. It differs from the plain empirical quantile
	$Q_{1-\alpha}$ of the same window by one order statistic, an
	$\mathcal O(1/w)$ gap that is absorbed into $\Delta_w$ below without changing
	its rate.
	The coverage event satisfies
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_score_event}
		r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll
		\Longleftrightarrow
		S_t\le \qVroll.
	\end{equation}
	
	Following the weighted conformal framework of
	\citet{barber2023conformal}, the coverage deviation under
	non-exchangeability is bounded by the average total variation distance
	between calibration-window score distributions and the test distribution:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_tv_bound}
		\left|
		\mathbb P(S_t\le\qVroll)-(1-\alpha)
		\right|
		\le
		\frac{1}{w}
		\sum_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}
		d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)\right).
	\end{equation}
	By definition,
	\[
	\frac{1}{w}
	\sum_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}
	d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)\right)
	\le
	\delta_w(t).
	\]
	The empirical quantile estimated from a dependent window of size $w$
	adds the finite-sample remainder
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_delta_w}
		\Delta_w\le C\sqrt{\frac{\log w}{w}}.
	\end{equation}
	Combining the drift and estimation terms yields
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_score_bound}
		\mathbb P(S_t\le\qVroll)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w.
	\end{equation}
	Returning to the score representation gives
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_bound}
		\mathbb P\!\left(
		r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll
		\right)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w.
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}

The bound separates distributional drift, captured by $\delta_w(t)$, from
finite-sample estimation error under dependence, captured by $\Delta_w$.
In the empirical implementation, $\delta_w(t)$ is proxied by the empirical
total variation distance between the first and second halves of the rolling
window. Using $\sqrt w$ histogram bins, define
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_delta_hat}
	\hat\delta_w(t)
	=
	\frac{1}{2}\sum_b |\hat p_{1,b}-\hat p_{2,b}|,
\end{equation}
where $\hat p_{1,b}$ and $\hat p_{2,b}$ are empirical bin frequencies over
the first and second halves of the window. When $\hat\delta_w(t)$ exceeds a
practitioner-chosen threshold, the bound weakens and a shorter window or
temporary deployment pause may be warranted.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~7.1 of the manuscript}
\label{app:identification}

Recall $u_t = F_t(\hat q^{lo}_t)$ and $V_t = \mathbf 1\{r_t < \hat q^{lo}_t\}$,
with $\hat q^{lo}_t$ measurable with respect to $\mathcal G_{t-1}$.

\begin{proof}
	Since $\hat q^{lo}_t$ is $\mathcal G_{t-1}$-measurable and $F_t$ is the
	conditional law of $r_t$ given $\mathcal G_{t-1}$,
	\[
	\P\!\left(V_t = 1 \mid \mathcal G_{t-1}\right)
	= \P\!\left(r_t < \hat q^{lo}_t \mid \mathcal G_{t-1}\right) = u_t ,
	\]
	and $u_t$ is $\mathcal G_{t-1}$-measurable. For $s \le t-1$ the indicator
	$V_s$ is a measurable function of $(r_s, \hat q^{lo}_s)$ and hence
	$\mathcal G_{t-1}$-measurable. Conditioning on $\mathcal G_{T-1}$,
	\[
	\P\!\left(V_{1:T}=v_{1:T}\right)
	= \E\!\left[\mathbf 1\{V_{1:T-1}=v_{1:T-1}\}\,
	u_T^{\,v_T}(1-u_T)^{1-v_T}\right],
	\]
	because the first factor is $\mathcal G_{T-1}$-measurable and the conditional
	law of $V_T$ is Bernoulli$(u_T)$. Iterating the same step down to $t=1$ gives
	the path likelihood stated as equation (12) of the manuscript.

	The right-hand side is a functional of the joint law of $(u_1,\dots,u_T)$
	alone. Hence if $P$ and $Q$ induce the same law for $\{u_t\}$ they induce the
	same law for $V_{1:T}$, so $\E_P[\phi(V)] = \E_Q[\phi(V)]$ for every
	measurable $\phi$, and in particular the rejection probability of any test in
	that class is the same under both: its power equals its size.

	For the moment-restriction case, $\E[V_t \mid \mathcal G_{t-1}] = u_t$, so
	$u_t \equiv \alpha$ implies $\E[(V_t-\alpha)Z_{t-1}] = 0$ for every
	predictable $Z_{t-1}$ with finite second moment. Two models both satisfying
	$u_t\equiv\alpha$ therefore satisfy every such restriction exactly, and a test
	of one of them rejects each with probability equal to its nominal size. The
	dynamic quantile regression of \citet{engle2004caviar} is of this form, with
	$Z_{t-1}$ collecting lagged exceedance indicators and the reported quantile
	itself.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the proposition does not cover]
	\label{rem:identification_scope}
	Two exclusions are worth stating. First, $\sigma(V_t, \hat q^{lo}_t)$ is
	strictly larger than $\sigma(V_t)$, and the two models constructed below do
	\emph{not} have the same marginal law for $\hat q^{lo}_t$. A diagnostic that
	compared the level of the reported quantile against a scale reference would
	separate them --- but that reference is not in $\sigma(V,\hat q^{lo})$, and no
	test in standard use within that class exploits the difference. This is why
	the proposition is stated for the moment-restriction family rather than for
	$\sigma(V,\hat q^{lo})$ in full. Second, statistics reading the
	\emph{magnitude} of an exceedance rather than its occurrence lie outside the
	class entirely; Supplement~\ref{app:es} treats them.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot_depth}: the construction, and the depth of the blind spot}
\label{app:blindspot_depth}

Let $G$ be the standardised Student-$t_5$ law of the correctly specified model,
with $\alpha$-quantile $q_{\mathrm{true}} = \nGapQTrueStd$ in units of
conditional scale. A forecaster that truncates its predictive support, discarding
mass $\delta$ from each tail and renormalising, reports
\[
q_\delta \;=\; G^{-1}\!\left(\delta + \alpha(1-2\delta)\right),
\]
which is closer to zero than $q_{\mathrm{true}}$ and is what
$\texttt{top\_k}$ truncation does to a categorical predictive law.

For the two models to induce the same $u$-process it suffices that the
alternative return law $H$ place exactly $\alpha$ mass strictly below $q_\delta$,
since then $u_t \equiv \alpha$ under both. Requiring in addition that $H$ be
symmetric with unit variance and the same mean absolute deviation as $G$, and
that its density be non-increasing in $|x|$, gives a feasibility problem that is
\emph{linear} in the atoms of a discrete symmetric law on a fixed grid:
\begin{align*}
	\textstyle\sum_i p_i = 1, \qquad
	\textstyle\sum_i p_i x_i^2 = 1, \qquad
	\textstyle\sum_i p_i |x_i| = \E_G|X|, \\
	\textstyle\sum_{i:\,x_i < q_\delta} p_i = \alpha, \qquad
	p_{i+1} \le p_i \ \text{ for } x_i > 0, \qquad p_i \ge 0 .
\end{align*}
Feasibility is therefore decided by a linear programme rather than argued.
Bisection on $\delta$ gives the largest feasible truncation depth
$\delta^\star = \nGapDeltaUni$, at which the reported quantile is
$\nSupQClassUni$ and the Value-at-Risk is understated by \nGapUndUni\%.

The resulting pair is matched to machine precision on coverage
($u \equiv 0.01$), variance, mean absolute deviation, median and predictive
standard deviation, and to three decimals on point-forecast mean absolute error
and root mean squared error. Both laws place exactly $\alpha$ mass strictly below
their own reported threshold, so the exceedance indicator is Bernoulli($\alpha$)
and serially independent under each, and every test of the exceedance path has
the same null distribution on the two. Simulated at $T = \nSupPairT$ the realised
rates are $\nSupPairPiHonest$ and $\nSupPairPiAlt$, both paths are Basel Green,
and neither rejects at any conventional level on Kupiec
($p = \nSupPairKupHonest$ and $\nSupPairKupAlt$), on Christoffersen independence
($\nSupPairCCHonest$ and $\nSupPairCCAlt$), or on a dynamic quantile test with
\nSupPairDQLags{} lagged exceedance indicators and the reported quantile as
instruments ($\nSupPairDQHonest$ and $\nSupPairDQAlt$). The simulation checks
that the sampled law reproduces the constraints the linear programme imposed;
the indistinguishability itself is the equality above. The reported thresholds
are $\nPairVaRHonest$ and $\nPairVaRAlt$ in units of conditional scale.

Two further properties are worth recording. Truncation is not repairable by the
sampling parameters an analyst would vary: restricting the support to its $k$
most probable atoms bounds every quantile read from any reweighting of that
support below by $\min S_k$, so no temperature and no nucleus threshold recovers
a quantile the truncation has removed. And matching \emph{mean} expected
shortfall as a fifth linear constraint is also feasible, but it does not make
magnitude-reading statistics blind: the law of $Z_2$ is not pinned by its mean,
and at $T = \nSupPowerT$ a size-$5\%$ one-sided test calibrated on the honest
model still rejects the alternative with probability $\nSupPowerRej$ over
\nSupPowerReps{} replications, a $95\%$ interval of $[\nSupPowerLo,
\nSupPowerHi]$ that excludes the nominal size. Necessary, not
sufficient.

\section{Tail Completion Determines the Tail}
\label{app:tail_closure}

TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 do not report a 1\% quantile. Their output is a
nine-decile grid whose lowest point is the 10\% quantile
(Section~3.1.1 of the manuscript), so the threshold that is backtested comes from an
external completion step. This appendix asks how much that step matters, and the
answer is: more than the model does.

Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended} compares the Student-$t$ closure used
throughout the paper against a Gaussian closure and a linear extrapolation from
the two lowest deciles, on three assets. The same grids, on the same days, give
$\bar R$ ranging from $\nLitClosureRMin$ to $\nLitClosureRMax$. Within a single
model--asset pair the closure rule moves $\bar R$ by a factor of
\nSupClosureFactorLo{} (Moirai~2.0 on BTC) to \nSupClosureFactorHi{}
(Moirai~2.0 on NATGAS). On BTC the choice moves both models out of the Basel
yellow zone into the red one after correction, so it changes the regulatory
verdict and not only a diagnostic magnitude.

The practical consequence is the one the body of the paper draws in a different
setting. A quantile-grid forecaster is not a forecaster until a completion rule
is specified, and the rule is part of the forecaster in the same sense that
$\texttt{top\_k}$ is part of a sampled one. Reporting ``TimesFM~2.5 at the 1\%
level'' without naming the closure describes an object that does not exist.

\paragraph{A refit on the inner deciles cannot fail, and is reported for that
reason} It is natural to treat a refit on $u \in \{0.2, \ldots, 0.8\}$ ---
dropping the outermost deciles that anchor the tail --- as the more demanding
test of the closure, and it does recover $\qVstat$ and the ordering exactly, to
six decimal places on all six model--asset combinations
(Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended}). That agreement is an artefact of the
construction rather than evidence about the model. The deciles are not native
model output; they are reconstructed from the stored Student-$t$ parameters
$(\nu, \mu, \sigma)$, so fitting a Student-$t$ to any subset of them recovers
those parameters exactly and the check cannot fail. We report it because a check
that cannot fail is easily mistaken for a check that passed.

The Gaussian and linear closures are informative precisely because they impose a
different functional form, and they are the ones that move the answer.

\input{Quantlets/CO_robustness_inner7/tab_tail_closure_extended}

The magnitude of the closure effect is not uniform across rules, and no rule
yields $\bar R > 1$ on SP500 or NATGAS. The position of a grid forecaster in the
ordering by $\bar R$ is therefore not invariant to the completion layer, which is
the reason the manuscript treats the closure as part of the forecaster rather
than as a robustness dimension.



\section{Series Validation Gate}
\label{app:validation_gate}

\paragraph{A note on sign, before anything else} Equation~(1) of the manuscript
defines $\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}$, a \emph{positive} threshold.
The forecast files, and therefore the gate, store and check the \emph{lower
quantile} $\hat q^{lo}_\alpha$, which is negative. The two conventions differ by
a sign, the checks below are written in the second, and the stored column is
nonetheless named \texttt{VaR\_}$\alpha$.

That naming is not incidental. A column named for a risk measure while holding a
quantile of the opposite sign invites exactly one error, and it is the ambiguity
that the first failure mode of Table~1 of the manuscript exploits. The gate
checks the sign explicitly because the name does not.

\bigskip

Every forecast series used in this paper is run through a scripted validation
gate before it is admitted to the analysis. The gate runs in continuous
integration, emits the table below, and blocks promotion of any series that
fails; a blocked series receives a written diagnosis and no tolerance is widened
to accommodate it. The implementation is \texttt{scripts/promotion\_gate.py}.

\paragraph{What kind of thing each check is} Two of the ten are invariants: they
follow from what a quantile forecast is, and have no tunable constant. One is an
invariant tested by a proxy with a threshold. The remaining seven are
\emph{plausibility bands} --- statements about what a Value-at-Risk series for
daily financial returns should look like, with cutoffs we chose and state rather
than derive. We separate them because the distinction is the difference between
a check that cannot be argued with and a check that can, and a reader deciding
whether to adopt this gate is entitled to know which is which. The bands are
deliberately wide: every one of them admits a range within which a forecaster may
be poor without carrying an implementation failure.

\paragraph{Two of them are not free of a backtest, and we say so}
$\alpha$-responsiveness and coverage plausibility both need realised returns over
an evaluation window, so the gate is not entirely upstream of the data a backtest
uses. What separates them from a backtest is not the input but the question. A
backtest asks whether a coverage rate is statistically distinguishable from
nominal, calibrates its threshold to sample size, and at $\alpha = 0.01$ rejects
most of a defensible panel. These two ask whether the series responds to the
level it was asked for at all, and whether its exceedance rate is within a factor
of five of nominal --- questions that a series carrying an implementation failure
fails by orders of magnitude and an ordinary one passes comfortably. The truncated Chronos series fail
$\alpha$-responsiveness with a ratio of \nLitAlphaResp{} against a threshold of 3, and
coverage with $\hat\pi/\alpha = 39$ against a bound of 5. No plausible tightening
of a Kupiec test produces that separation, because Kupiec is not asking that
question.

\paragraph{Scoping is declared, not inferred} Support cardinality and tail reach
apply only to sample-based forecasters, and monotonicity is weak for
order-statistic estimators. Historical Simulation returns an order statistic of a
250-observation window: it takes few distinct values by construction and may tie
across adjacent $\alpha$. That is a property of the estimator, not a failure, and
the gate declares the check inapplicable from the estimator class recorded in the
series manifest rather than returning a verdict it cannot support.

Two further invariants are enforced over the data directories rather than over
individual series: every file present must be named in the series manifest, and
every name in the manifest must exist on disk. Both directions are required. The
manifest records, for each series, the model, asset, path, version and estimator
class.

\subsection{Static versus rolling recalibration, in detail}

\section{A Controlled Case: Support Truncation in a Sampled Predictive Law}
\label{app:mechanism}

This section establishes, by controlled experiment, that a single documented
default in a checkpoint's packaged inference routine accounts for a realised
exceedance rate of \nPiSmallDefaultOne{} against a nominal $\alpha = 0.01$ for
Chronos-Small, and \nPiMiniDefaultOne{} for Chronos-Mini, and then removes the
sampler from the estimation path entirely. The case is developed on Chronos-T5
\citep{ansari2024chronos} because it is independently reproducible: the
checkpoint is public, the sampling routine ships with it, and the experiment
below requires neither the forecasts nor the returns used elsewhere in this
paper. A reader can run it and obtain the same mechanism.

\subsection{What the default does}
\label{app:mech_default}

Chronos-T5 does not emit a parametric predictive law. It quantises the scaled
context into 4093 value bins and treats forecasting as next-token prediction over
that vocabulary, so its one-step predictive distribution is categorical with 4093
atoms. Predictions are drawn from it by the same sampling machinery used for
text, and that machinery has a $\texttt{top\_k}$ parameter, defaulted to 50:
before a single path is drawn, the support is restricted to the 50 most probable
bins and the remaining 4043 are assigned probability zero.

Two properties of this restriction matter here. It removes \nLitSupportPct\% of the support,
and it removes it \emph{from the tails inward}, because the discarded bins are by
construction the least probable ones. A quantile read from the surviving support
is therefore a quantile of a different, radically narrower distribution --- while
the centre of that distribution, and hence any point forecast, is barely moved.

\subsection{Design}
\label{app:mech_design}

The experiment varies one thing. For each of the two checkpoints
\texttt{chronos-t5-small} and \texttt{chronos-t5-mini}, four assets of unlike
character (SP500, GOLD, BTC, EURUSD) and 200 evenly spaced dates with a full
512-observation context, we draw 1000 samples at horizon one under eight
sampling configurations: $\texttt{top\_k} \in \{50, 200, 1000, 4094\}$,\footnote{The
vocabulary holds 4096 tokens, of which two are special, leaving 4093 value bins
plus the pad token. Setting $\texttt{top\_k} = 4094$ therefore disables the
truncation with one place to spare; it is not an off-by-one against the 4093 bins
quoted elsewhere in this paper.} then
$\texttt{top\_p} \in \{0.9, 0.99\}$ and temperature $\in \{0.5, 2.0\}$ with
$\texttt{top\_k}$ held at its default. Context, seed and weights are identical
across configurations, so the only thing that differs between cells is the
sampling rule.

Three readings were fixed before the experiment was run. If the truncation is the
mechanism, then (i) predictive dispersion recovers monotonically in
$\texttt{top\_k}$ and reaches its target only at full vocabulary, (ii) dispersion
is unmoved by temperature and by nucleus sampling, and (iii) the number of
distinct values among 1000 draws under the default equals $\texttt{top\_k}$
exactly, on every asset and both checkpoints.

\subsection{Result}
\label{app:mech_result}

All three readings hold (Table~S.26). Dispersion --- the ratio of predictive
standard deviation to the asset's 250-day realised volatility, for which 1.0 is
the target --- recovers monotonically in $\texttt{top\_k}$: \nDispDefaultSmall,
\nDispTwoHundredSmall, \nDispThousandSmall{} and \nDispFullSmall{} for
Chronos-Small across $\texttt{top\_k} \in \{50, 200, 1000, 4094\}$, and
\nDispDefaultMini{} to \nDispFullMini{} for Chronos-Mini. It reaches its target
only when the vocabulary is not truncated at all. One thousand draws under the
default contain exactly 50 distinct values in all \nDoseCells{}
model--asset--date cells, without exception; this is a signature of the mechanism
rather than evidence about the model, and it is the reading that makes the
diagnosis arithmetic rather than inferential.

The remaining rows are the reason such a configuration is durable. A fourfold
change in temperature moves dispersion by \nLitTempGap{} (\nDispTempLowSmall{} against
\nDispTempHighSmall) and nucleus sampling moves it by \nLitNucleusGap. These are the
parameters that are documented, discussed in the literature and exposed in every
tutorial; an analyst who conducts a careful sensitivity analysis over them
obtains a flat response and correctly reports the model as robust. The one
parameter that governs the tail is the one such a sweep does not vary.

\subsection{Reading the distribution instead of sampling it}
\label{app:mech_analytic}

At a one-step horizon the sampler is unnecessary. The predictive law is
categorical and exactly available, so its quantiles can be read in closed form:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item apply the softmax to the logits, giving probabilities over token
	identifiers;
	\item map each identifier to its bin centre, offset by the special-token
	count;
	\item multiply by the tokenizer's own scale, the mean absolute value of the
	context;
	\item sort the support and take the cumulative sum of the probabilities;
	\item read $\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$ as the smallest value whose cumulative
	probability reaches $\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}

This estimator has no $\texttt{num\_samples}$, no $\texttt{top\_k}$, no
$\texttt{top\_p}$, no temperature and no seed. There is no configuration to get
wrong, and it costs one forward pass.

A reconstruction of a model's support is a claim about that model and is checked
against it before anything is concluded from it. Over 40 dates on SP500 we
compare the analytic quantiles against sampling at full vocabulary with 4000
draws: predictive standard deviations agree to within \nLitAnalyticSdPct\%, and
the violation rates at all four levels agree to four decimal places.

\paragraph{A third validation that could not have failed, and this one is
arithmetic} The corrected panels can be obtained two ways: by re-running the
estimator from the weights under the corrected token-to-bin map, or by the
closed-form rule that subtracts one bin width scaled to the context. The rule
was checked against \nRuleValidationDates{} re-run dates and agreed on all of
them. Panel-wide it is not exact: over \nRuleRows{} dates per checkpoint it
holds on \nRuleExactPct\% and fails on \nRuleFailures{} of them, where the bin
the cumulative distribution crosses moves index by one and the offset is not one
bin. The \nRuleValidationDates{}-date block contains none of those
\nRuleFailures{} dates, and that is what a block of that size should be expected
to do: drawing \nRuleValidationDates{} dates independently from a panel failing
on \nRuleFailPct\% of them returns no failure about \nRuleMissProb\% of the
time. The agreement observed is therefore consistent with the rule being exact
and equally consistent with it failing as often as it does, which is the same
form as the two agreements above --- a check run at a resolution the defect can
sit inside. The corrected panels in this paper come from the re-run, not the
rule, and the reason is this measurement rather than a preference.

Both agreements are reported with the defect they cannot see. A one-bin
translation of the support leaves the predictive standard deviation
\emph{exactly} invariant, so the first is a tolerance on a quantity the failure
mode does not move; and on \nLitAnalyticDates{} dates the realised rate lives on
a grid of \nLitAnalyticGrid{}, so ``agreement to four decimal places'' there
means the two routes returned the same multiple of that grid. Neither could have
detected a uniform translation of the support, and no choice of tolerance would
have repaired either. The sampled default, by contrast, is separated from
both by a quantity that does move: its predictive dispersion is
\nDispDefaultSmall{} against \nDispFullSmall{} at full vocabulary.

Cost is not a consideration either. Across 24 assets and both checkpoints the
analytic route required one forward pass per date --- 121{,}923 dates per
checkpoint --- and completed in roughly ten minutes per checkpoint on a laptop
GPU, against 1000 sampled paths per date for the sampling route. At horizon one,
reading the distribution is both more accurate than sampling it and cheaper.

\subsection{What this does to the forecasts}
\label{app:mech_consequence}

Table~S.27 reports the raw forecasts on the full panel, at four nominal levels
and with no recalibration of any kind. Two things in it deserve separate
statements.

\paragraph{The default produces a series that is not a quantile forecast}
Between the 1\% and the 10\% level the nominal exceedance probability changes
tenfold; under the default configuration the realised rate changes by a factor of \nLitAlphaResp, from \nPiSmallDefaultOne{} at $\alpha = 0.01$ to \nPiSmallDefaultTen{} at $\alpha = 0.10$, both for Chronos-Small. A distribution truncated
to its fifty most probable bins has so little spread that its $\alpha$-quantiles
are nearly coincident, and the series built from them responds to the level it
was asked for almost not at all. That property is diagnostic on its own: it needs
no benchmark, no model of comparison and no significance calculation, and it is
the $\alpha$-response check of Section~8 of the manuscript.

\paragraph{The residual is a tail phenomenon, and it shrinks} Removing the
sampler does not make the model well calibrated at the 1\% level. Read through
its own predictive law, Chronos-Small delivers $\hat\pi = \nPiSmallAnalyticOne$
against a nominal $0.0100$ --- \nRatioSmallAnalyticOne{} times nominal --- and
unconditional coverage rejects on \nMainKupRejRawSmallAnalyticOne{} of
\nMainAssets{} assets. The residual is
not a level shift, however. The ratio $\hat\pi/\alpha$ falls monotonically ---
\nRatioSmallAnalyticOne, \nLitRatioSmallTwoFive, \nLitRatioSmallFive, \nRatioSmallAnalyticTen{} for Chronos-Small
and \nRatioMiniAnalyticOne, \nLitRatioMiniTwoFive, \nLitRatioMiniFive, \nRatioMiniAnalyticTen{} for Chronos-Mini
--- so that at $\alpha = 0.10$ the analytic forecasts are essentially calibrated,
passing Kupiec on \nKupPassSmallAnalyticTen{} and \nKupPassMiniAnalyticTen{} of
\nMainAssets{} assets. What survives the removal of the sampler is a specifically
\emph{tail} deficiency, and that is a statement about the model and its
quantisation grid rather than about the interface through which it is read.

\section{How Large the Blind Spot Is}
\label{app:blindspot_pair}

Proposition~7.1 of the manuscript is only useful if the set of
indistinguishable alternatives contains objects a risk manager would care to
separate. It does. Let $\delta$ denote the \emph{truncation depth}: the
probability mass discarded from each tail of a predictive law before its
$\alpha$-quantile is read. It is distinct from the drift diagnostic
$\hat\delta_w(t)$ of Supplement~S.9.6, which shares no more than the letter.

\begin{proposition}[The depth of the blind spot]
	\label{prop:blindspot_depth}
	Fix $\alpha = 0.01$ and a correctly specified forecaster whose returns are
	standardised Student-$t_5$, so that its reported quantile is
	$\nGapQTrueStd$ in units of conditional scale. If the alternative model's
	return law is required only to be symmetric and unimodal with the same
	variance and the same mean absolute deviation, then for every
	$\delta \le \delta^\star = \nGapDeltaUni$ there exists a truncated
	forecaster and a return law under which the exceedance sequence has exactly
	the same law as under the correctly specified pair, while the reported
	Value-at-Risk is understated by up to \nGapUndUni\%. For
	$\delta > \delta^\star$ no such unimodal law exists.
\end{proposition}

The construction is explicit and is given in Supplement~S.9.8 as a linear
programme: the constraints --- coverage, variance, mean absolute deviation, and
the reported quantile --- are all linear in the probabilities of a discrete
symmetric law, so feasibility is decided by the programme. The resulting pair
is matched on the predictive standard deviation, on point-forecast accuracy in
both mean absolute error and root mean squared error, and on the median; it
differs only in the reported threshold and in the shape of the predictive law
away from it.

$\delta^\star$ belongs to the restriction placed on the return law and is not a
constant. Dropping unimodality raises it to $\nGapDeltaFree$, an understatement
of \nGapUndFree\%; requiring in addition a fourth moment no larger than the
honest model's lowers it to $\nGapDeltaMoment$; confining the alternative to a
GARCH class with standardised Student-$t$ innovations leaves only
\nGapUndGarchT\%. The discretisation
converges from above, so each figure is an upper bound and the bias runs toward
overstating the blind spot. Supplement~S.9.8 gives the full table.

Two consequences run through the rest of the paper. Section~5.4 of the manuscript
measures how much of this depth structural checks remove. And the Chronos
configuration of Supplement~\ref{app:mechanism} sits \emph{outside} the blind
region: the tail probability it cuts off shows up as a realised exceedance rate
of $\nPiSmallDefaultOne$, well beyond $\nGapDeltaFree$, which is why
unconditional coverage rejects it on every asset
--- while rejecting \nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\% of the panel besides. Detection is not
diagnosis, and the two separate exactly where Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot_depth}
says they should.

\section{Structural Validation: Why It Precedes Correction, and What It Removes}
\label{app:precede}

The ordering is forced by two facts already established. The correction restores
marginal coverage whatever the forecaster (Section~8 of the manuscript), so a
pipeline that validates after correcting has no test of the forecaster; and a
coverage test identifies only the realised tail probability
$u_t = F_t(\hat q^{lo}_t)$ (Proposition~7.1 of the manuscript), so validating
before correcting does not help either, if the validation is itself a coverage
test. What is left is a check that reads the series rather than its exceedances,
and it has to run first.

\paragraph{What is new here, and what is not} That exceedance-based backtests are
weak at $\alpha = 0.01$ is established, and this paper claims no priority over
it. \citet{escanciano2012pitfalls} prove the unconditional test inconsistent
against Historical Simulation and Filtered Historical Simulation, and their
Lemma~1 then exhibits a weighted backtest that is consistent --- a result
recovering inside the exceedance class the power the unconditional test loses.
\citet{gordy2020spectral} and \citet{kratz2018multinomial} treat the poverty of
the single-level indicator as a premise to design around, the first by reading
the probability integral transform and the second by reading exceptions at four
levels, and Gordy and McNeil state the requirement plainly: what is essential is
that the regulator observes more than the exception indicator at one level.

Two things separate the present argument from that literature and neither is the
observation of weakness.

The first is the characterisation, and it is what lets the two results stand
together. \citet{escanciano2012pitfalls}'s alternatives agree with the null on
the \emph{mean} of $u_t$; the alternatives of
Proposition~7.1 of the manuscript agree with it on the whole process
$\{u_t\}$. Against the first, a weighted backtest recovers power, and their
Lemma~1 constructs one. Against the second, \emph{no} statistic measurable with
respect to the exceedance sequence separates them, that weighted backtest
included. The two propositions are about different alternative sets and neither
contradicts the other; what the second adds is where the exceedance class ends.

The second is the information set, and it is not a weaker claim than theirs but a
claim about a different one. \citet{gordy2020spectral} and
\citet{kratz2018multinomial} establish what a supervisor can do who holds the
predictive distribution: read the probability integral transform, or take
exceptions at four levels, and recover power the single-level indicator cannot
carry. That is correct and it is not available here. A firm consuming a
third-party quantile series --- a vendor model, a pretrained checkpoint, a
counterparty's reported thresholds --- holds the thresholds and the realised
returns and nothing else, and cannot obtain a PIT value from a forecaster that
never published a distribution. The structural checks are built for that
position: they read the series and the returns, which is what such a user has.
The two literatures are answering the same question on two information sets, and
the second is the one most consumers of a forecast are in.

This section measures how much of the blind spot of
Proposition~S.16.1 a structural check removes. Measured on
the understatement it permits, which is the quantity
Table~\ref{tab:gate_residual} reports, the answer is a little over a third.

A forecast series can be checked against properties it must have \emph{to be a
Value-at-Risk series at all}, before it is calibrated, evaluated or compared with
anything. The checks are stated on the lower quantile $\hat q^{lo}_\alpha$, which
is negative and is what the forecast files store, and not on the positive
threshold $\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}$ of
equation~(1) of the manuscript; the two conventions differ by a sign, and a column
named for a risk measure while holding a quantile is precisely the ambiguity
that the first failure mode of Table~S.31 exploits.

Ten such checks are stated in Supplement~S.11 and implemented in
\texttt{scripts/promotion\_gate.py}: a lower quantile whose median is not
negative, quantiles that are not ordered across $\alpha$, an exceedance rate that
does not respond to the nominal level, a predictive dispersion far from realised
volatility, a support of a handful of distinct values.

The ten are not of equal standing. Two are invariants that follow from what a
quantile forecast is and carry no tunable constant; one is an invariant tested
through a proxy with a threshold; the remaining seven are plausibility bands
with chosen cutoffs, stated in Table~\ref{tab:gate_compact}. Seven of the ten need nothing but the forecast
series and the returns. Tail reach needs the model's sampled paths and is
inapplicable to grid and parametric forecasters; $\alpha$-responsiveness and
coverage plausibility need an evaluation window, so the gate is not entirely
upstream of the data a backtest consumes. What separates them from a backtest is the
question they ask: not whether a coverage rate is statistically distinguishable
from nominal, but whether the series responds to the level it was asked for at
all. No $p$-value is computed, no benchmark is required, and none of the ten
needs access to the hardware that produced the forecasts.

does not close the identification gap, so the checks are not a complete
complement of the test class. Against that, the size of their contribution is

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Where the band edge can sit, and how much margin it has}{Where the band edge can sit, and how much margin it has}}
\label{app:gate_band}

The scale band --- $\operatorname{median}(\hat q^{lo}_{0.01}/\hat\sigma_t)$
within $[\nGapBandLower, \nGapBandNow]$ --- is the check that binds, and its
edges were chosen by hand. On these data the choice does not matter, and the
separation that makes it not matter is the quantity worth reporting.

Evaluated on all \nGapCellsTotal{} series--asset cells, the two populations are
disjoint by a wide interval. The worst well-specified cell sits at
$\nGapCellGoodWorst$ and the best truncated cell at $\nGapCellTruncBest$, a gap
of $\nGapGapWidth$ in units of estimated scale. \emph{Any} edge strictly inside
that interval blocks all \nGapCellsTrunc{} truncated cells and no well-specified
one, so the gate's verdict on this panel is invariant to where the edge is placed
within the gap. The verdict therefore does not rest on the choice at all.

The safety margin is a different quantity from the gap, and the smaller of the
two. It is the distance from the edge to the nearest well-specified cell, and at
$\nGapBandNow$ it is $\nGapMarginUpper$ --- an order of magnitude smaller than
the gap. Tightening the edge to $\nTightBand$ reduces the residual understatement from
\nGapUndGateNow\% to \nTightUnd\%, and leaves a margin of $\nTightMargin$: essentially none. At the current edge of $\nGapBandNow$ the
gap exceeds the margin by a factor of eleven; at $\nTightBand$ it exceeds it
by more than two hundred. Robustness read off the gap rather than the margin
therefore overstates the check by one order of magnitude at the edge as
specified, and by two at the tightened one.

The margin measures the check against the panel that produced it, and on that
panel the two populations are far apart: eleven series whose reported quantile
sits between two and three estimated standard deviations below zero, and two
whose truncation has driven it to a fifth of that, with nothing between. A
separation established on a sample containing only gross failures and correct
specifications is a weak thing to report, because the discriminating power is
in-sample by construction. The band therefore has one test on this evidence
that is not: a family it was never shown.

The band edges were fixed before that family was run --- the declaration is dated
\nMLBandDeclared{} and the estimator panel \nMLPanelRun{} --- so what follows is
out of sample rather than a re-reading of the cells the edges were drawn around.
Gradient-boosted quantile regression and a quantile random forest were run on
\nMLAssets{} assets across \nMLLeafSettings{} leaf resolutions, \nMLCells{} cells
at \nMLDates{} dates each, and they land on opposite sides of the question. The
forest places every cell inside the band, between $\nMLQrfRatioLo$ and
$\nMLQrfRatioHi$. The boosted estimator places \nMLGbmBlocked{} of its
\nMLGbmCells{} cells between $\nGapCellGoodWorst$ and $\nGapCellTruncBest$ ---
the interval the sequence panel leaves empty --- and the check blocks all
\nMLGbmBlocked. Of those, \nMLBlockedUnder{} are on cells exceeding at
\nMLUnderThreshold{} times nominal or more, and no cell the check passes exceeds
at that rate; the remaining \nMLBlockedNotUnder{} return three violations where
two were expected, which \nMLDates{} dates at $\alpha = 0.01$ cannot separate
from correct coverage.

So the intermediate range is no longer empty and no longer untested. On the one
family available that occupies it, the check discriminates rather than misfiring:
it blocks under-covering cells, passes the rest, and misses none. That is the
strongest statement this paper makes about the gate, and it is the only one
measured on series the gate was not built around.

The edge's false-positive region is then bounded in closed form rather than left
open. A correctly specified forecaster is blocked at $\nGapBandNow$ only if its
standardised innovation law puts its own $1\%$ quantile above that point, which
requires a tail lighter than a triangular law's $\nMLQuantTriangular$: Normal
innovations sit at $\nMLQuantNormal$ and standardised $t_5$ at $\nMLQuantTFive$,
and only a bounded law as light as the uniform, at $\nMLQuantUniform$, is caught.
The illustrative forecaster reporting $-\nMLWorkedExampleSigma\hat\sigma_t$ is
not in that class. It is under-conservative by a quarter measured on the
threshold and by a factor of \nMLWorkedNormal{} measured on coverage --- it
exceeds at \nMLWorkedNormal{} times nominal under Normal innovations,
\nMLWorkedTFive{} under standardised $t_5$ and \nMLWorkedTThree{} under $t_3$ ---
so blocking it is a correct verdict. The two units differ by a factor of nearly
three, and the limitation is the coverage one.

\paragraph{One of our own checks cannot fail, and that is the paper's argument
turned on itself} The lower edge $\nGapBandLower$ blocks
\nGapCellsLowerBlocks{} of \nGapCellsTotal{} cells. Nothing in the panel comes
near it: the most negative cell anywhere is $\nGapCellGoodBest$, a margin of
$\nGapMarginLower$, five times the margin at the upper edge. \emph{A check that
blocks nothing on any series it has been shown cannot fail informatively, so a
series passing it has not been tested --- which is precisely what
Section~7.2 of the manuscript says about a Christoffersen statistic computed on
a degenerate transition table, and it is here in one of our own ten checks.} The
two cases have the same structure: an absence of evidence returned in the form
that reads as a pass, and a pass rate reported as though it were an outcome.

Adjusting the edge downward until it binds would manufacture the outcome, so the
band stands as specified and its consequence is recorded instead. On this panel
the scale check is one-sided; the band is asymmetric in risk; and whether a lower
edge is needed at all cannot be determined from these data, because nothing in
them is over-conservative enough to test one. The block count is nine checks
exercised and one that was not. The \nMLCells{} cells of the second estimator
family do not settle it either: the most negative of them reaches
$\nMLRatioMostNegative$, a margin of \nMLLowerEdgeMargin{} from the edge, and
\nMLBelowLowerEdge{} of them fall below it. That family is measured on
\nMLAssets{} assets rather than \nMainAssets, so it can only have shown the edge
binding and cannot show that it does not.

\paragraph{What the gate cannot see} The checks operate on a stored forecast
series and the returns. They can establish that a series has the properties a
Value-at-Risk series must have, and they cannot establish that the estimator
which produced it is the estimator the paper says produced it. A mismatch between
a declared estimator and an implemented one leaves no trace in the series: both
the plain empirical quantile and the conformal order statistic of
equation~(8) of the manuscript yield a well-formed series that passes all ten checks, and
only reading the code distinguishes them. Structural validation and code
provenance are complements, and neither is inferable from the other.

\paragraph{Nor can it validate prose} The same limit applies one level up.
Converting this manuscript's numeric claims to generated macros surfaced two
assertions that no artefact produces: a pair of figures comparing a modified and
a canonical test statistic, whose verification script computes neither of them,
and a pair of raw violation rates whose producer emits only corrected ones. Both
were in the version submitted for review and survived it. Seventy-nine numeric claims sat outside the reach of the script that
checks the manuscript's arithmetic, and a literal that is never emitted cannot be
checked by a tool that checks emitted values --- the structure of
Section~7.2 of the manuscript, one level up from the forecasts. A gate that
validates series does not validate the sentences written about them, and the
argument of this paper applies to the paper.

\paragraph{Two checks are out of scope for order-statistic estimators} Support cardinality and tail reach
are inapplicable to order-statistic estimators, which may legitimately take few
distinct values and tie across adjacent $\alpha$ as a window slides; a discrete
predictive support may tie for the same reason. The gate records the estimator class of each
series in a manifest and declares the check inapplicable rather than returning a
verdict on it.

That scoping has a consequence worth stating rather than leaving to the
manifest: on this panel tail reach returns \emph{inapplicable} for all
\nGateSeries{} series, because none of the shipped forecast files stores sampled
paths. It contributes no verdict to any of the blocks reported below, and the
gate that operates here is nine checks wide, not ten. A check that cannot return
a verdict on the population it is run against carries no evidence when that
population passes --- the same reading this paper applies to the lower band edge
in Section~\ref{app:gate_band}, and it is recorded for the same reason.

\paragraph{Result on this panel} The gate blocks \nGateBlocked{} of the
\nGateSeries{} series submitted to it. Two are the Chronos series sampled at the
checkpoint default, which fail monotonicity, scale, $\alpha$-response, coverage
and dispersion on all \nMainAssets{} assets, and the extremes band on
\nGateExtremesSmall{} and \nGateExtremesMini{} of them. Two are their analytic
counterparts, which fail the coverage-plausibility band on one asset each, and,
for Chronos-Mini-A, $\alpha$-responsiveness on one asset as well --- the
residual tail deficiency of
Supplement~S.14.5, recorded as a block requiring a written
diagnosis rather than silently admitted. Two of the four blocks are therefore
marginal, and the band that produces them is unchanged.

\paragraph{What this evidence is worth} The ten checks were specified after the
failure modes of Section~6.1 of the manuscript had been identified, and several
exist because a failure of that class had already passed whatever checks were in
place at the time. Their performance on those failure modes is in-sample by
construction and is not evidence of sensitivity to failures of a kind not
represented here. The argument for them does not rest on that performance. It
rests on Table~\ref{tab:gate_residual}: the checks reduce a gap that the
coverage-based tests of Section~7 of the manuscript cannot reduce at all, and both
the reduction and the remainder are computed.

\section{The Monte Carlo Harness, and What Its Gate Cannot See}
\label{app:mc_harness}

A simulation study is an instrument, and this paper's subject is what instruments
miss. The design above was therefore gated on reproducing an existing
two-sample-size study before any new sample size was reported, and the gate is
stated here with what it can and cannot see.

The published study reproduces from its 5{,}000 per-replication rows on every
printed value. A fresh implementation of the recursions and the estimator
reproduces all ten of its cells on all six reported quantities, at a tolerance of
three standard errors of the \emph{difference} between two independent Monte Carlo
estimates, with the new run at 2{,}000 replications so that the published run's
$500$-replication error dominates the comparison. A systematic bias survives the
gate only if it is inside the band in all \nSupReproCells{} cells,
so the smallest discrepancy the tolerance admits is $\nSupReproTol$ on mean
$\qVstat$: the gate establishes agreement to Monte Carlo resolution, and not
exact agreement.

Two defects surfaced, both in the harness rather than in what it was measuring,
and neither in a direction the results would have revealed.

The first was a tolerance. The standard error of a standard deviation was taken as
$\mathrm{sd}/\sqrt{2N}$, which assumes a normal replication distribution;
$\qVstat$ is a $1\%$ order statistic of heavy-tailed scores and its replication
distribution has skewness up to $+\nMCSkewMax$, so the declared tolerance understated the
true sampling variability by up to $\nMCSEUnderstate$ times, worst in exactly the cell where
the skew is largest. The second was a seed. Replications were keyed on Python's
string \texttt{hash}, which is salted per interpreter process, so two runs of the
same script drew different samples and the set of cells the gate flagged changed
between them --- non-reproducibility introduced by the code written to check for
it. Both were found because the gate failed, not because the results looked
wrong; under a correctly specified tolerance and a deterministic seed all ten
cells reproduce.

Neither defect is in a forecasting model, and that is the reason for reporting
them. This paper's argument is that the code converting a model output into a
number is an object of study, and the same argument applies to the code that
checks it. Section~10 of the manuscript returns to the point with the second
instance, which is in the estimator this paper offers as a remedy.

One control is reported rather than repaired. Run on a forecaster given the
correct innovation law, so that the population shift is zero, the harness returns
zero at every calibration sample of 700 or more and a small negative value at 350
--- the interpolated quantile's own downward bias in a right-skewed tail, running
opposite to the conformal convention's upward one. At $\alpha = 0.01$ and
$n = 350$ neither convention is unbiased. The $T = 500$ column of
Table~1 of the manuscript carries that and is reported with it.
