\begin{abstract}

	We report the first systematic 1\%-tail audit of zero-shot time-series foundation models (TSFMs) for Value-at-Risk, across 13 forecasters and 24 assets. The central finding is that the \emph{predictive interface} --- whether a model emits Monte Carlo samples or a coarse quantile grid --- rather than its architecture or pretraining corpus, drives extreme-tail calibration failure. Within the Moirai family, whose two versions share an architecture and a closely related pretraining design, the sample-based 1.1 release violates on 1.5\% of days at the 1\% level while the quantile-grid 2.0 release violates on 98.8\%. Quantile-grid outputs cannot deliver a backtestable 1\% VaR without external tail completion, and the closure step, not the model, then determines tail accuracy.
	
	The instrument for the audit is a one-parameter conformal shift applied to the predicted lower quantile, retained as a signed, continuous statistic $R$ measuring the correction's size relative to the reported VaR. Across the panel $R$ spans four orders of magnitude, from $0.001$ for CAViaR --- already calibrated, and unchanged by correction --- to $23.5$ for Chronos-Mini, whose tail estimate the correction replaces outright. We report $R$ as a continuous quantity: the data contain several gaps and no natural cutoff, and the largest is not where a threshold would fall.
	
	Recalibration is an intervention with an indication, not a universal improvement. It is close to free where it is not needed and harmful where the base forecast is already calibrated: under the rolling estimator, 93\% of pairs passing Kupiec on the raw forecast are degraded, and 60\% of those degradations buy no Basel zone change at all. Gating the correction on a failed raw backtest removes half the damage while retaining every zone upgrade. We claim marginal coverage and diagnosis only: the scalar shift addresses a median 35\% of total miscalibration, does not restore conditional violation independence, and is not a substitute for a correctly specified dynamic tail model.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accurate calibration of tail risk forecasts remains a central challenge in
financial forecasting under temporal dependence. Even state-of-the-art
models, from parametric volatility specifications to time series foundation
models (TSFMs), can systematically misestimate extreme quantiles, leading
to violations of nominal coverage and unreliable risk assessment
\citep{christoffersen1998,nolde2017elicitability,goel2024tsfmvar,pele2025llmvar}.
The problem is acute in the lower tail, where Value-at-Risk (VaR) forecasts
are subject to formal regulatory backtesting \citep{basel1996}. While
conformal prediction provides a natural framework for post-hoc recalibration,
the key question is what level of statistical complexity is identifiable from
tail data.

At tail probability level $\alpha$ with $T$ observations, the expected
number of tail events is only $\mathcal{O}(\alpha T)$. For $\alpha=0.01$
and roughly 1{,}500 out-of-sample observations, the extreme tail is informed
by few exceedances. Flexible recalibration is therefore statistically
fragile, because variance inflation can dominate bias reduction. This
tail-sparsity constraint motivates the low-dimensional design
used in this paper.

This paper makes three contributions. The audit operates on the predicted lower quantile and treats the upstream forecaster as a black box; this base-method-agnostic design is what allows the contributions below to range across both classical parametric and foundation-model forecasters within a single evaluation. 

First, the audit locates extreme-tail calibration failure in the predictive interface rather than in the architecture or the pretraining corpus. Moirai~1.1 and Moirai~2.0 share an architecture and a closely related pretraining design and differ in output format; at the 1\% level the sample-based release violates on 1.5\% of days and the quantile-grid release on 98.8\%. The same split holds across the wider set: every quantile-grid forecaster requires external tail completion to produce a backtestable 1\% VaR, and the closure rule then governs tail accuracy. This is a statement about how forecasts are exposed, not about how well they are trained, and it is invariant to the closure rule used (Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended}). 

Second, we develop an asymptotic coverage guarantee for the single-split estimator under $\beta$-mixing dependence with explicit gap-separation conditions, complemented by finite-window bounds for the rolling variant under distributional drift. 

Third, at the 1\% tail, local adaptation appears more valuable than additional distributional flexibility. Across two methodologically distant classes --- per-asset GARCH-type benchmarks and zero-shot foundation models --- a rolling one-parameter shift dominates richer alternatives, including GAMLSS-SST and gradient-boosted quantile regression, in coverage while remaining competitive in Quantile Score. This is consistent with the tail-sparsity argument that additional parameters add variance faster than they reduce bias at $\alpha = 0.01$.

Expected Shortfall is reported as a supplementary
diagnostic; Appendix~\ref{app:es} documents the obstacles to a formal
joint VaR--ES conformal treatment.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:lit} situates the contribution in the literature on tail miscalibration, regulatory backtesting, and post-hoc calibration.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} develops the two-layer audit, with
asymptotic coverage results under $\beta$-mixing dependence,
and
describes the asset universe, the ten forecasters, and the
experimental design. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the
empirical audit, the ordering by $\bar R$, and the
static-versus-rolling comparison.
Section~\ref{sec:discuss} discusses calibration--sharpness trade-offs,
deployment considerations, and the FRTB capital arithmetic.
Section~\ref{sec:conclude} concludes. Proofs and supplementary tables
are in the appendices.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}

This paper relates to three strands: tail miscalibration in probabilistic forecasters, regulatory backtesting of tail-risk forecasts, and post-hoc calibration under temporal dependence.

Tail miscalibration is well-documented across forecaster classes. Parametric volatility models can systematically under- or over-estimate extreme quantiles under heavy-tailed innovations or regime shifts \citep{christoffersen1998,mcneil2000estimation,nolde2017elicitability}. Recent time series foundation models (TSFMs) --- including Chronos \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}, TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, Moirai \citep{woo2024moirai,liu2025moirai}, and Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2024lagllama} --- achieve competitive zero-shot forecasting performance but exhibit a similar pattern: \citet{goel2024tsfmvar} show that pre-trained quantiles require fine-tuning for acceptable Value-at-Risk (VaR) coverage, and \citet{pele2025llmvar} identify tail compression in instruction-tuned models, with similar miscalibration documented for volatility forecasting \citep{goel2025volatility,rahimikia2025revisiting}. These findings, spanning parametric and foundation-model regimes alike, motivate post-hoc calibration but do not address the identification constraint imposed by extreme-tail sparsity, and they stop short of a taxonomy of when post-hoc correction adjusts an informative base versus when it acts as an empirical fallback.

Regulatory backtesting procedures \citep{kupiec1995,christoffersen1998,basel1996,acerbi2014back,fissler2016higher,nolde2017elicitability} detect violations of nominal coverage, exceedance dependence, and tail-loss inconsistency, but do not prescribe forecast corrections.

Post-hoc calibration adjusts the output of a fixed forecaster without retraining \citep{gneiting2013combining,taillardat2016calibrated}. These procedures sit within the broader probabilistic-forecasting literature on calibration--sharpness trade-offs \citep{gneiting2007probabilistic,gneiting2014probabilistic}, where calibration restoration is balanced against sharpness loss. Model-based alternatives, such as CAViaR \citep{engle2004caviar} and conditional-density frameworks such as the Generalised Additive Model for Location, Scale and Shape \citep[GAMLSS;][]{rigby2005generalized}, estimate tail behaviour directly but impose structure on the conditional distribution. Conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic}, applied to quantile regression by \citet{romano2019conformalized} as conformalized quantile regression (CQR), provides a model-free alternative. Standard guarantees rely on exchangeability, which is violated in financial time series by volatility clustering, serial dependence, and structural change; extensions to dependent or non-exchangeable data include weighted conformal methods \citep{barber2023conformal}, blocking schemes \citep{chernozhukov2018exact}, and adaptive procedures \citep{zaffran2022conformal,xu2024conformal,angelopoulos2024pid}.

The TSFM literature receives closer attention above because this class is recent and has not yet been the subject of a systematic 1\% tail audit; the parametric volatility literature is mature and enters this paper through standard benchmarks rather than fresh review.

Building on these strands, this paper treats the magnitude of a conformal correction as an audit statistic --- a signed, continuous measure of the tail information content retained by the base forecast --- rather than as a calibration parameter to be minimised. The closest prior work targets adjacent objectives: \citet{goel2024tsfmvar} and \citet{goel2025volatility} focus on reducing TSFM tail miscalibration through fine-tuning; \citet{gibbs2021adaptive} deliver adaptive coverage under nonstationarity through online updates of the miscoverage rate, treating the conformal threshold as a tracking target; and \citet{tamingtailrisk2026} extend conformal risk control to nonstationary portfolio VaR through regime-similarity weighting, targeting risk control rather than diagnosis. None of these works uses correction magnitude as an audit instrument; the order-of-magnitude separation we document across 240 model--asset pairs is invisible to their formulations.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:framework}

The audit has two layers: a base model $M$ producing one-step-ahead probabilistic forecasts (Section~\ref{sec:layer1}), and a one-parameter conformal shift that recalibrates the predicted lower quantile (Section~\ref{sec:layer2}). Two implementations of the second layer are developed in Section~\ref{sec:layer2}: a single-split estimator, which admits asymptotic coverage under $\beta$-mixing dependence, and a rolling variant.

\paragraph*{Notation}
Let $r_t$ denote the daily log return and $\hat q_t^{lo} = (\widehat F_t^M)^{-1}(\alpha)$ the predicted lower $\alpha$-quantile from base model~$M$; we fix $\alpha = 0.01$. VaR is reported as a positive loss threshold:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_raw}
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}.
\end{equation}
The nonconformity score is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:score}
	S_t = \hat q_t^{lo} - r_t,
\end{equation}
and the conformal shift is denoted $q_V$, with single-split and rolling variants
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_cp_roll}
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{cp}} = \VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} + q_V^{\mathrm{stat}},
	\qquad
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{roll}} = \VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} + q_V^{\mathrm{roll}}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer~1: Probabilistic Forecasting}
\label{sec:layer1}

\subsubsection{Base Model Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The first layer produces one-step-ahead probabilistic forecasts of returns from a fixed base model~$M$.

For sample-based forecasters (Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini, Moirai~1.1, and Lag-Llama), the forecast at time~$t$ consists of $N = 1{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws $\{\hat r_t^{i;M}\}_{i=1}^N$, yielding the empirical predictive CDF
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:ecdf_sample}
	\widehat F_t^M(x)
	= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\mathbf{1}\{\hat r_t^{i;M}\leq x\}.
\end{equation}
The raw VaR forecast is the empirical lower $\alpha$-quantile,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_sample}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\alpha;M}
	= -(\widehat F_t^M)^{-1}(\alpha).
\end{equation}

For quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0), the predictive object is a finite grid of conditional quantiles $\{\hat q_t(u_j)\}_{j=1}^{J}$, with $J = 9$ deciles. Since the audit operates at the 1\% lower-tail level, we obtain $\hat q_t(0.01)$ by fitting a three-parameter Student-$t$ distribution to the native predicted quantiles:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:tail_completion}
	(\hat\nu_t, \hat\mu_t, \hat\sigma_t)
	=
	\arg\min_{\nu, \mu, \sigma}
	\sum_{j=1}^{J}
	\left[
	\hat q_t(u_j)
	-
	\left\{
	\mu + \sigma\, t_{\nu}^{-1}(u_j)
	\right\}
	\right]^2,
	\qquad \nu > 2,\ \sigma > 0,
\end{equation}
and set
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_grid}
	\hat q_t(0.01)
	=
	\hat\mu_t + \hat\sigma_t\, t_{\hat\nu_t}^{-1}(0.01).
\end{equation}
The Student-$t$ closure is a parsimonious heavy-tailed default in financial VaR modelling \citep{bollerslev1987,hansen1994autoregressive,mcneil2000estimation}; the position of the high-$\bar R$ forecasters is invariant across Student-$t$, Gaussian, and linear closures (Section~\ref{app:tail_closure}).

For classical benchmarks (GJR-GARCH, GARCH-Normal, EWMA, Historical Simulation), VaR is computed from the model-implied conditional distribution or, for Historical Simulation, the empirical 250-day rolling distribution.

Expected Shortfall is reported separately at $\alpha = 0.025$ following the Basel III/FRTB convention \citep{bcbs2019frtb} and is computed from the same predictive object as VaR. Because ES is not elicitable as a standalone functional \citep{gneiting2011making}, the conformal construction is developed for VaR only; ES computation details and the heuristic ES shift are in Appendix~\ref{app:es}.

\subsubsection{Diagnosing Miscalibration}
\label{sec:diag}

We apply the standard regulatory backtests --- Kupiec~\citep{kupiec1995} for unconditional coverage, Christoffersen~\citep{christoffersen1998} for joint coverage and serial independence, and the Basel Traffic Light \citep{basel1996} for zone classification --- and define the violation indicator
\begin{equation}
	V_t = \mathbf{1}\bigl(r_t < -\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\alpha;M}\bigr).
\end{equation}

Tail-loss consistency is assessed through the $Z_2$ statistic \citep{acerbi2014back} computed with the time-averaged ES denominator $Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}$, which avoids division by near-zero daily ES on quiet days (Appendix~\ref{app:backtesting}). Beyond coverage, the Fissler--Ziegel scoring rule \citep{fissler2016higher} ranks $(\VaR, \ES)$ jointly; full construction is in Appendix~\ref{app:es}. The conformal magnitude introduced in Section~\ref{sec:layer2} complements these tests by quantifying miscalibration on a continuous, signed scale.

Beyond the per-asset backtests, four panel-level inferential procedures support the empirical audit: a diagnostic regression of the Quantile Score improvement on the conformal threshold, a cross-sectional association between the threshold and asset characteristics, a Diebold--Mariano pairwise forecast comparison with Driscoll--Kraay panel-HAC variance, and a panel-pooled Kupiec test with cluster-robust inference. Specifications are in Appendix~\ref{app:backtesting}; results enter the empirical sections at first use.

\subsection{Layer~2: Conformal Recalibration}
\label{sec:layer2}

\subsubsection{One-Sided Conformal VaR Correction}
\label{sec:cqr_var}

Conformal prediction provides a model-free framework for post-hoc calibration of predictive distributions \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,romano2019conformalized}. We adapt it to one-sided tail risk: forecast outputs are split chronologically into calibration and test sets with fraction $f_c = 0.70$ (robustness in Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

The nonconformity score $S_t$ from equation~\eqref{eq:score} is positive when the realised loss exceeds the predicted quantile. The conformal shift is the empirical $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the calibration scores,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qV}
	\qVstat
	= Q_{1-\alpha}\!\left(\{S_t\}_{t\in\mathcal D_n}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D_n$ is the calibration sample. The recalibrated lower quantile and VaR are then given by equation~\eqref{eq:var_cp_roll} with $q_V^{\mathrm{stat}} = \qVstat$. A positive $\qVstat$ shifts the predicted quantile downward, increasing reported VaR and correcting underestimation of tail risk; a negative value indicates over-conservatism.

The construction makes no parametric assumption on returns; the coverage guarantee under temporal dependence is developed in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

The one-sided design is dictated by the regulatory loss function, not by a symmetry consideration. Basel III/FRTB capital and the Yellow/Red zone triggers are functions of loss-side exceedances alone (Section~\ref{sec:economic}): a day on which the realised return falls below the reported VaR is counted, a day on which it comfortably exceeds it is not. A correction applied to the upper tail would therefore alter no capital charge and no zone assignment, while widening the reported interval and degrading sharpness. We accordingly shift only $\hat q_t^{lo}$ and leave the remainder of the predictive distribution untouched; two-sided and distributional alternatives are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}. Operational safeguards, including the non-negativity constraint $\qVstat \geq 0$, are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\begin{remark}[Variance scaling under tail sparsity]
	\label{rem:tail_sparsity}
	Tail calibration relies on events $\{r_t \leq \hat q_t^{lo}\}$, whose expected number is $\E[N_\alpha] = \alpha T$. Estimators fitted to this tail subset inherit variance of order $k/N_\alpha$ for $k$ parameters. By contrast, $\qVstat$ is a single order statistic of the full calibration sample. A one-parameter correction therefore enjoys a favourable bias--variance trade-off under tail sparsity, and the correction magnitude is interpretable as a diagnostic precisely because the construction adds no further parameters. Values of $\qVstat$ that dominate the scale of $\hat q_t^{lo}$ signal the high-$\bar R$ range.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Validity under Temporal Dependence}
\label{sec:validity}

Standard conformal coverage guarantees require exchangeability, which volatility clustering and serial dependence in financial returns violate. We therefore state an asymptotic coverage result under weak dependence.\footnote{We work with $\beta$-mixing rather than $\alpha$-mixing because the conformal threshold is an empirical quantile of the score process and the proof uses blocking/coupling arguments controlling total-variation distance between calibration and evaluation blocks.}

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:main}
	Let $S_t = \hat q_t^{lo} - r_t$ denote the nonconformity score process and let $n = \lfloor f_c T \rfloor$ be the calibration sample size.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(A\arabic*)}]
		\item\label{ass:stationary}
		$\{S_t\}$ is strictly stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing: $\beta(k) \le C\rho^k$, $\rho \in (0,1)$.
		\item\label{ass:density}
		The marginal distribution $F_S$ admits a density $f_S$ that is continuous and bounded away from zero near the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile $q_{1-\alpha}$.
		\item\label{ass:weak_dep}
		There exists a gap sequence $g_n \to \infty$, $g_n/n \to 0$, such that the test score $S_{n+g_n+1}$ is evaluated after the calibration sample $\mathcal D_n = (S_1, \ldots, S_n)$, separated by $g_n$ unused observations.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}
	\label{thm:dependent_conformal_var}
	Under Assumption~\ref{ass:main}, the single-split conformal VaR estimator satisfies
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:coverage_bound}
		\mathbb P\!\left(
		r_{n+g_n+1} \ge \hat q_{n+g_n+1}^{lo} - \qVstat
		\right)
		\ge
		1 - \alpha - \Delta_n,
	\end{equation}
	where $\Delta_n \le C\sqrt{(\log n)/n} + \beta(g_n)$ and $C > 0$ depends on the density near $q_{1-\alpha}$ and on the mixing rate.
\end{theorem}

The bound is conservative at typical financial sample sizes. On six representative model--asset pairs spanning estimated score persistence $\hat\rho \in [0.18, 0.67]$, the estimated remainder $\hat\Delta_n$ lies between 0.109 and 0.138 --- a coverage floor of approximately 88\% against empirical coverage averaging 98.9\%. Across all 240 model--asset pairs, empirical coverage exceeds the corresponding lower bound $1 - \alpha - \hat\Delta_n$. The bound provides a dependence-robust justification for the single-split correction; finite-sample performance is assessed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The remainder simplifies under standard GARCH dynamics.

\begin{corollary}
	\label{cor:garch_conformal_var}
	Let $\{r_t\}$ follow a GARCH(1,1) process satisfying $\E[\log(\alpha_1\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta_1)] < 0$. Then the score process $\{S_t\}$ is strictly stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing with $\beta(k) \le a\rho^k$. Choosing $g_n = c\log n$ with $c > 1/|\log\rho|$, Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} applies with $\Delta_n = \mathcal O(\sqrt{(\log n)/n})$.
\end{corollary}


\subsubsection{Rolling Conformal Calibration}
\label{sec:rolling_method}

Financial time series exhibit time-varying volatility and structural change, motivating a locally adaptive conformal correction. In the rolling implementation, the conformal threshold is recomputed at each time $t$ from a trailing window of size $w = 250$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qV_roll}
	\qVroll
	=
	Q_{1-\alpha}\!\left(\{S_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}\right),
\end{equation}
with the recalibrated lower quantile and VaR given by equation~\eqref{eq:var_cp_roll} substituting $q_V^{\mathrm{roll}}(t) = \qVroll$.

The rolling variant is an operational heuristic, not a construction with finite-sample conformal validity: Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} covers the single-split estimator only, and recomputing the threshold from a trailing window violates the single-split assumption.\footnote{Complementary results from the online learning literature \citep{zinkevich2003online} and the weighted conformal framework \citep{barber2023conformal,angelopoulos2024pid} bound time-average miscoverage and finite-window deviation under drift, but cover related objects --- exact OGD updates and exponentially weighted quantiles, respectively --- not the uniform-weight rolling quantile used here. Propositions~\ref{prop:ogd_regret} and~\ref{prop:rolling_drift} in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} adapt these bounds; the proofs are included for completeness.} Empirical support comes from the simulation study in Section~\ref{sec:simulation} and from the drift diagnostic $\hat\delta_w(t)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:rolling_drift}), which flags windows where local recalibration departs from the global one.

The formal conformal guarantee in Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} applies to the single-split estimator. The rolling estimator should be read as a locally adaptive engineering variant whose validity is assessed empirically and supported by the time-average and drift bounds in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, not as an exact conformal procedure.

\subsubsection{Alternative Recalibration Baselines}
\label{sec:alternatives}

We compare the one-parameter conformal shift against alternatives that vary on three axes: parameter count, correction structure (location only, location--scale, monotone, fully nonparametric), and target object (correction, conditional distribution, or tail). We compare against nine baselines: five single-shot post-hoc corrections, two distributional or nonlinear methods (GAMLSS-SST and gradient-boosted quantile regression), and two dedicated VaR models (EVT-POT and filtered historical simulation). Full specifications are in Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-specs}; Table~\ref{tab:alt_baselines} provides a compact reference. The comparison tests Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity}: at $\alpha = 0.01$, additional parameters increase variance faster than they reduce bias. Empirical results are in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}.

\subsection{Data and Models}
\label{sec:data_models}

\subsubsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis covers 24 financial assets across five classes: 11 equity indices, 3 government bond exchange-traded funds (ETFs), 4 commodities, 2 cryptocurrencies, and 4 foreign exchange (FX) pairs. Daily log-returns are computed from adjusted closing prices obtained from Yahoo Finance over January 2000--March 2026. Price series were retrieved on 13 March 2026. Cryptocurrency histories begin in 2014 for Bitcoin and 2017 for Ethereum.

Returns are computed on each asset's native trading calendar without cross-asset alignment, and no further data cleaning is applied: missing days are dropped rather than forward-filled, and asynchronous closes across classes are retained. The evaluation therefore reflects deployment conditions rather than a synchronised research panel.

Each asset's return series is partitioned chronologically: the single-split estimator uses the first 70\% as calibration and the remaining 30\% as the out-of-sample test window ($f_c = 0.70$, Section~\ref{sec:cqr_var}); the rolling estimator recomputes the threshold at each $t$ from a trailing window of size $w = 250$ (Section~\ref{sec:rolling_method}) and is evaluated on the same 30\% test window. The full asset list, per-asset sample sizes, and per-asset test windows are in Appendix~\ref{app:assets}.

\subsubsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We evaluate six TSFM forecasters in strict zero-shot mode (no updating or fine-tuning) against four classical benchmarks estimated per asset. Fine-tuning TSFMs is left to future work.

The TSFM set spans two predictive interfaces. Sample-based forecasters (Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2} with tokenised sequence models; Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2024lagllama} with a LLaMA-inspired architecture and Student-$t$ heads; Moirai~1.1 \citep{woo2024moirai}) return $N = 1{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws from the predictive distribution. Quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 \citep{das2024timesfm}; Moirai~2.0 \citep{liu2025moirai}) return a fixed nine-decile grid. Moirai is included in both formats, supporting the within-family contrast in Section~\ref{sec:interface_vs_architecture}.

The classical benchmarks --- GJR-GARCH \citep{glosten1993relation}, GARCH-Normal \citep{bollerslev1986generalized}, EWMA \citep{riskmetrics1996}, and Historical Simulation --- represent standard volatility and tail-risk models; estimation details are in Appendix~\ref{app:models} and Section~\ref{sec:exp_design}.

\subsubsection{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:exp_design}

\textbf{The comparison is deliberately asymmetric, and we state that here rather than as a qualification later.} The classical and dynamic benchmarks are estimated per asset and re-estimated at every step; the foundation models are deployed strictly zero-shot, with no fine-tuning and no per-asset adaptation. These are not equivalent adaptation regimes, and any Quantile Score comparison between the two groups reflects that difference as well as any difference in model quality. We retain the asymmetry because it is the operationally relevant contrast --- a zero-shot model is what a practitioner can deploy without an estimation pipeline per instrument --- but no conclusion in this paper rests on a TSFM-versus-GARCH score ranking. The audit's findings are within-group: the interface contrast is between two releases of the same family under identical deployment, and the recalibration results are computed separately for every forecaster.

All forecasts are one-step-ahead. TSFMs use a fixed context length of 512 observations, the default for all six pretrained checkpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:models}). Classical benchmarks are estimated on rolling windows of 250 observations, following the Basel convention \citep{basel1996}, with parameters re-estimated at each step.

A warm-up period (512 observations for TSFMs, 250 for classical benchmarks) precedes the calibration--test split. For full-history series (2000--2026), the test sample is approximately 1{,}500 observations per asset, yielding roughly 36{,}600 panel observations per model and 366 expected violations at $\alpha = 0.01$.

\section{Empirical Results}
\label{sec:results}

The primary evaluation unit is the model--asset pair, because regulatory risk systems are backtested at the desk, asset, or portfolio level. Panel-pooled tests provide complementary evidence of average calibration across the asset universe, not substitutes for per-asset backtesting.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Raw Diagnostics and the $\qVstat$ Ranking}{Raw Diagnostics and the qV Ranking}}
\label{sec:results_raw}

Table~\ref{tab:master} reports raw and conformally corrected diagnostics across all thirteen forecasters and 24 assets, ordered by $\bar R$. Tail calibration is highly heterogeneous: raw 1\% VaR violation rates range from 0.4\% for GJR-GARCH (over-conservative) to 99\% for TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 (severe under-conservatism), spanning more than two orders of magnitude. Within the Moirai family, Moirai~1.1 ($\hat\pi = 1.5\%$) and Moirai~2.0 ($\hat\pi = 98.8\%$) diverge sharply; the within-family contrast is examined in Section~\ref{sec:interface_vs_architecture}.

\subsubsection{The 99\% violation rates are interface failures, not forecaster failures}
\label{sec:interface_failure}

\textbf{[PLACEHOLDER --- this subsection is withdrawn pending the corrected series.]} The 99\% violation rates previously reported for TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 were traced to a sign error in the forecast-generation step, in which the stored $\alpha$-quantile was written as $-F^{-1}(\alpha)$ rather than $F^{-1}(\alpha)$; the resulting threshold was positive, so almost every return fell below it. The defect is documented in the replication package and the affected series have been recomputed. No claim about quantile-grid interfaces is made from those figures, and the text of this subsection is withheld until the corrected panel is complete.

The mechanism is the predictive interface. Both models emit a nine-decile grid whose lowest point is the 10\% quantile; the 1\% quantile is not produced by the model at all but extrapolated from that grid by an external closure step. The grid is a faithful summary of the central distribution and carries almost no information about the far tail, so the extrapolation is doing the work, and it extrapolates from deciles that are far too narrow. The correct reading is therefore not that these are bad forecasters whose output the correction rescues. It is that \emph{a coarse quantile grid cannot deliver a backtestable 1\% VaR without external tail completion}, and that once completion is required, the closure rule rather than the pretrained model determines tail accuracy. A practitioner can see this before deployment, from the output format alone --- which is what makes the finding useful.

The ordering by $\bar R$ is invariant across Student-$t$, Gaussian, and linear tail-completion rules (Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended}), so the position of the quantile-grid models is not an artefact of a single closure choice.\footnote{All forecast series pass the scripted validation gate of Appendix~\ref{app:validation_gate} before use.}

The ratio
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:replacement_ratio}
	R
	=
	\frac{|\qVstat|}{|\VaR_{\mathrm{raw}}|}
\end{equation}
measures how large the correction is relative to the threshold it adjusts. Values near zero mean the corrected forecast is the raw forecast; values above one mean the correction exceeds the raw VaR in magnitude, so the reported threshold is set by the calibration sample rather than by the model. Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini, TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 sit at the upper end; CAViaR, Moirai~1.1, Lag-Llama and the classical benchmarks at the lower, with $|\qVstat| < 0.011$. Whether the replacement label reflects missing tail information or severely misspecified scale is tested in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}, where simple scale-correction baselines fail to recover the replacement-regime forecasts.

\revised{A location--scale diagnostic supplies an independent, and now the only discrete, signal. Fitting $\VaR_{\mathrm{cp}}=\hat a+\hat b\,\VaR_{\mathrm{raw}}$ by $\alpha$-level quantile regression on each pair, a negative fitted slope is a near-certain replacement signature: $\hat b<0$ implies $R>1$ in $51$ of the $52$ pairs where it occurs, so an inverted slope is a \emph{sufficient} condition for replacement rather than an equivalent classifier. The disagreement runs in only one direction---replacement pairs ($R>1$) carrying a positive slope, which the sign test alone does not flag---and the two diagnostics agree on the regime for $79\%$ of the $216$ pairs overall. The diagnostic further resolves the correction into three mechanisms: in the low-$\bar R$ range ($R\le1$) the slope stays below unity and roughly $46\%$ of the shift is genuinely multiplicative (mean scale share $0.459$), whereas in the high-$\bar R$ range the shift is substitution rather than scale repair, surfacing either as slope inversion ($\hat b<0$, TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0) or as a degenerate raw magnitude on which the intercept dominates (Chronos). The replacement label thus reflects the base forecast being overwritten, not a recoverable scale error, consistent with the failure of scale-correction baselines noted above.}

Across all 240 model--asset pairs, $\qVstat$ ranges from $-0.007$ (GJR-GARCH, over-conservative) to $0.102$ (TimesFM~2.5, under-conservative). The diagnostic regression of Quantile Score improvement on the conformal threshold and the raw violation rate yields $R^2 = 0.782$, with a partial $R^2$ of $53.4\%$ for $\qVstat$ (Appendix~\ref{app:diag_regression}, Table~\ref{tab:diag_regression}).

Among high-$\bar R$ TSFMs, $\qVstat$ tracks annualised volatility ($\bar\rho_{\mathrm{repl}} = 0.96$) and tail frequency ($\bar\rho_{\mathrm{repl}} = 0.51$); among low-$\bar R$ forecasters the cross-sectional association weakens for Moirai~1.1 ($\rho_{\mathrm{vol}} = 0.67$) and inverts for GJR-GARCH, whose conditional variance dynamics absorb volatility into raw VaR. The signal-preserving classification therefore reflects a small corrective shift relative to raw VaR, not absorption of cross-sectional volatility by the base forecast. Per-model correlations are in Appendix~\ref{app:cross_sectional}.

\subsection{Static Recalibration}
\label{sec:recalib}

The corrected columns of Table~\ref{tab:master} show that marginal coverage is restored across the panel: 260 of 312 model--asset pairs reach the Basel Green Zone (83.3\%). What the ordering by $\bar R$ adds is that the \emph{amount} of correction required to get there spans four orders of magnitude, from $\bar R = 0.001$ for CAViaR to $\bar R = 23.5$ for Chronos-Mini. At the low end the corrected forecast is the raw forecast to three decimal places; at the high end it is an empirical fallback that retains almost nothing of the base model's tail estimate. Green-zone compliance is therefore not informative on its own about whether a corrected forecast carries the base model's information, which is what $\bar R$ is reported for.

We deliberately do not partition the panel. The distribution of $\bar R$ contains several gaps --- the largest, a factor of 97, separates CAViaR from Moirai~1.1 near the \emph{bottom} of the range, and the next, a factor of 8.9, separates Lag-Llama from Moirai~2.0 --- and no single cutoff is distinguished by the data. Any threshold would inherit that arbitrariness, and the continuous statistic conveys the same information without it.

Conditional coverage is weaker than unconditional coverage throughout, and the gap widens with $\bar R$. Restricting attention to pairs on which the Christoffersen independence test is \emph{defined} --- it is undefined whenever no two violations fall on consecutive days, which at $\alpha = 0.01$ is 126 of 240 pairs --- the low-$\bar R$ forecasters pass on 61.5\% of pairs against 13.6\% for the high-$\bar R$ group. We report the informative subset because counting an undefined test as a pass, as is common, inflates both figures: under that convention the same numbers read 86.1\% and 40.6\%. The degeneracy rate is itself a result: at the 1\% level, on samples of the length regulators use, the standard independence test has no evidence to work with on half the panel. Duration-based tests \citep{engle2004caviar}, which use the spacing between violations rather than immediate transitions, flag clustering on 47 of those 126 pairs. Recalibration restores both unconditional coverage and violation independence. In Panel~B, the Christoffersen pass rate drops to 40.6\%, consistent with the interpretation that the conformal layer acts as an empirical-quantile fallback, not a recovery of predictive tail dynamics. Green Zone recovery in the high-$\bar R$ range should therefore be interpreted as restored unconditional coverage, not as full conditional-coverage adequacy. The structural reasons why a scalar shift cannot reshape conditional dynamics are in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\subsection{When the Correction Helps, and What It Costs}
\label{sec:when_to_apply}

Aggregate improvements can conceal deteriorations. We therefore report the full
distribution of $\Delta\mathrm{QS} = \mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{raw}} -
\mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{corrected}}$ over model--asset pairs rather than its mean.

At $\alpha = 0.01$ the correction makes 27 of 240 pairs worse under the
single-split estimator and 84 of 240 worse under the rolling estimator. The
deteriorations are not scattered: they concentrate almost entirely on pairs whose
raw forecast was already well calibrated. Among pairs whose raw forecast passes
Kupiec on the test set, the single-split correction is statistically
indistinguishable from doing nothing (mean $\Delta\mathrm{QS} = -0.38\%$ of
$\mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{raw}}$, $p = 0.43$), while the rolling variant degrades 41
of 44 such pairs, with mean $-6.8\%$ and $p \approx 4 \times 10^{-12}$. The same
pattern holds at $\alpha \in \{0.025, 0.05, 0.10\}$. Regressing relative
$\Delta\mathrm{QS}$ on $|\hat\pi_{\mathrm{raw}} - \alpha|$ over the region where
the base forecast is usable gives a positive slope and a negative intercept for
both estimators, the intercept being three to four times more negative for
rolling.

Whether a deterioration matters depends on whether it buys anything. Of the 84
pairs the rolling estimator degrades, 33 move from Yellow to Green --- a trade a
risk manager can accept --- but 48 were already Green and gain no zone change at
all, and 2 move from Green to Yellow, worsening on both axes. On 60\% of the
degradations, therefore, the correction is not a trade-off but simply the wrong
intervention.

This yields a deployment rule that requires no classification: apply the
correction when the raw forecast fails a backtest (Basel zone worse than Green,
or Kupiec rejected), and not otherwise. At $\alpha = 0.01$ the rule applies to
196 of 240 pairs and skips 44. It avoids 41 of the 84 rolling deteriorations
while retaining \emph{all} 176 Basel zone upgrades, so the cost on the regulatory
axis is zero; the three skipped pairs that would have gained are worth a median
$+0.16\%$ against the $-6.0\%$ median of the losses avoided. The rule does not
bind at $\alpha \geq 0.025$, where nearly every raw forecast already fails its
backtest and the correction is always indicated. The decision problem is
therefore specific to the extreme tail --- which is where the identification
constraint of Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} operates.

\subsection{Rolling Recalibration}
\label{sec:rolling_results}

For the rolling implementation, we use the time-varying analogue
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:replacement_ratio_rolling}
	R_t
	=
	\frac{|\qVroll|}{|\widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t|}.
\end{equation}
$R_t$ is monitored as a continuous series rather than reduced to a state. Sustained elevation indicates that the correction, not the base forecast, is setting the reported threshold; transient spikes are local stress episodes. We impose no threshold and no persistence rule, so no reclassification step is required.

In our panel $\bar R$ ranges from $0.001$ to $23.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:master}). The spread is continuous rather than bimodal: the two largest gaps fall at factors of 97 and 8.9, at different points of the range, so the ordering carries the information a binary label would have discarded.

\subsection{The Moirai~1.1 vs 2.0 Within-Family Contrast}
\label{sec:interface_vs_architecture}

The Moirai~1.1 versus Moirai~2.0 contrast provides the audit's clearest within-family finding. Both models are masked-encoder TSFMs trained under closely related pretraining designs \citep{woo2024moirai,liu2025moirai}; they differ in output format (1{,}000 Monte Carlo samples for 1.1, a nine-decile grid for 2.0, with architectural details in Appendix~\ref{app:models}). Moirai~1.1 has $\hat\pi = 1.5\%$ and $\bar R \approx 0.11$, placing it in the low-$\bar R$ range; Moirai~2.0 has $\hat\pi = 98.8\%$ and $\bar R = 3.2$, placing it in the high-$\bar R$ range (invariant across closure rules; Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended}).

The implication is that the predictive interface appears to be a major source of tail-calibration differences within the Moirai family: Monte Carlo sampling preserves the distributional information needed for extreme-quantile estimation, while a coarse quantile grid discards it. Quantile-grid interfaces therefore require external tail completion to deliver backtestable 1\% VaR, and the closure quality is bounded by the information discarded in the discretisation step.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}
\label{sec:baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the conformal method with alternative recalibration procedures defined in Section~\ref{sec:alternatives}. Performance is governed less by model flexibility than by whether the recalibration layer respects the tail-sparsity constraint.

Among single-shot post-hoc procedures, the one-parameter conformal shift achieves competitive Quantile Score ($6.07 \times 10^{-4}$) while maintaining the highest Green-Zone rate (84.6\%). Linear quantile regression on residuals achieves lower QS ($5.29 \times 10^{-4}$) but at somewhat lower coverage (81.5\% Green), consistent with the variance--coverage trade-off predicted by Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity}. ACI, which adapts the miscoverage level sequentially, reaches higher coverage (96.3\%) but with wider intervals, reflecting a trade-off between calibration and efficiency. The rolling conformal variant dominates all three, reaching 97.5\% Green\revised{\footnote{A companion head-to-head in the supplement reports a $94.9\%$ rolling Green-zone share at the same $0.010$ mean violation rate; the difference from the $97.5\%$ here is entirely a matter of protocol, not of the estimator, since the supplement scores the Basel zone on the final $250$-day window over the $216$-pair subset while the figure here uses full-history Basel scoring over all pairs.}} without increasing parameter dimensionality. \revised{A like-for-like comparison against Adaptive Conformal Inference in the supplement sharpens this ranking on the operational axes. With the step size $\gamma$ selected by first-half validation---the grid value minimising the gap between held-out online coverage and the nominal rate---ACI attains broadly comparable zone coverage but at a materially higher capital charge, posting a mean $|\VaR|$ of $0.0750$ against $0.0443$ for the rolling estimator (a factor of $1.7$) together with a rougher day-over-day capital path, and no value in the $\gamma$-grid brings its capital below that of the rolling estimator. The rolling estimator therefore dominates ACI at the Basel/capital operating point, while ACI remains attractive when the loss function penalises zone placement over capital and a fully online, distribution-free update is required.}

More flexible alternatives do not consistently improve performance. GBM-based quantile regression achieves only 48.6\% Green, with a Kupiec rejection rate of 59\% (128/216), despite using lagged volatility features; its narrower intervals reflect under-coverage, not efficiency. Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} predicts that gains from additional flexibility are bounded above by the variance cost of fitting at $\alpha = 0.01$. A grid search on S\&P~500 confirms the prediction (Appendix~\ref{app:gbm_tuned}): the QS-optimal tuning achieves QS $4.40 \times 10^{-4}$ but rejects Kupiec on 5/9 base forecasters; a conservative tuning achieves 0/9 rejections at the cost of higher QS ($4.63 \times 10^{-4}$).

GAMLSS-SST performs better than the static conformal shift (88.0\% vs.\ 84.6\% Green; QS $5.38$ vs.\ $6.07 \times 10^{-4}$) but remains below the rolling conformal variant. The marginal gain from fitting a dynamic skewed-$t$ density is small relative to the gain from local temporal adaptation.

Purpose-built VaR models also face short-window constraints. Under the 250-day rolling protocol, GARCH-N with conformal correction outperforms EVT-POT and filtered historical simulation, with EVT-POT weakened by GPD estimation failures and frequent fallback to EWMA volatility (Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-specs}).

The evidence supports Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity}: in this empirical design, rolling one-parameter recalibration delivers the dominant coverage improvement, while additional distributional flexibility yields limited gains at $\alpha = 0.01$.

\subsection{Simulation, Validation, and Robustness}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:failure}

As documented in Section~\ref{sec:recalib}, a scalar shift restores unconditional violation frequency but cannot reshape the conditional dynamics of the violation process; this is a structural limitation, not a tuning issue. The rolling variant captures time variation in miscalibration magnitude but remains a location adjustment, not a fully conditional recalibration rule. More flexible state-dependent recalibration, such as one-sided extensions of Adaptive Conformal Inference \citep{gibbs2021adaptive} or the PID framework of \citet{angelopoulos2024pid}, lies beyond the present framework.

A related limitation concerns identification. The positive cross-sectional association between $\qVstat$ and asset-level volatility or tail frequency (Appendix~\ref{app:cross_sectional}) is consistent with pre-training domain mismatch, but the zero-shot design does not permit causal attribution. The audit identifies \emph{where} tail signal is missing, not \emph{why}.

\subsection{Operational Failure Modes}
\label{sec:operational_failure}

Operational failure modes can be mitigated. Small calibration sets ($n_{\mathrm{cal}} < 200$) produce unstable corrections; a minimum calibration size or cross-asset aggregation is advisable. Very high score persistence ($\hat\rho > 0.95$) weakens the $\beta$-mixing bound, and GARCH pre-filtering can reduce dependence. Over-conservative base forecasts ($\qVstat < 0$) may lose coverage under non-stationarity; imposing $\qVstat \geq 0$ provides a guard for systemically over-conservative models. Structural breaks can cause temporary under-protection when the calibration window predates the break; shortening $w$ or incorporating stressed subsamples increases adaptivity at the cost of higher variance.

Figure~\ref{fig:covid_response} illustrates the structural-break case during the COVID-19 shock. The trailing-window design produces a measurable lag: six of ten forecasters reach their post-shock $\qVroll$ maximum within the acute window, while the remaining four exhibit delayed plateaux of 77--161 calendar days. The drift diagnostic $\hat{\delta}_w(t)$ flags this regime in real time (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}, Figure~\ref{fig:drift_diagnostic}), allowing operational deployment to detect the lag before it propagates.

\subsection{Economic Impact}
\label{sec:economic}

The capital arithmetic below is illustrative under the Basel~II/pre-FRTB internal-models multiplier framework. Under FRTB the binding capital measure is ES at 97.5\%; the VaR-based traffic-light arithmetic here describes the backtesting channel, which remains relevant for model validation but is not the primary capital channel.

Traffic Light zone classifications determine the capital multiplier $k \in [3, 4]$ applied to internal-model VaR. Let $W = \widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{corr}}/\widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{base}}$ denote the relative widening of the VaR threshold after correction. The capital-charge ratio between a corrected Green-zone forecast and an uncorrected Yellow-zone forecast is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:capital_ratio}
	\frac{\mathrm{Capital}_{\mathrm{Green,corr}}}{\mathrm{Capital}_{\mathrm{Yellow,base}}}
	=
	\frac{k_{\mathrm{Green}}}{k_{\mathrm{Yellow}}}\, W.
\end{equation}
A capital saving occurs when this ratio is below one. Using $k_{\mathrm{Green}} = 3.00$ and $k_{\mathrm{Yellow}} \approx 3.65$, the break-even width is $k_{\mathrm{Yellow}}/k_{\mathrm{Green}} \approx 1.22$: a Yellow-to-Green transition remains capital-saving as long as the corrected VaR threshold widens by less than about 22\%.

Lag-Llama on S\&P~500 has $W = 1.05$, so corrected Green-zone capital is $3.00 \times 1.05 = 3.15$ against an uncorrected Yellow-zone capital of $3.65$ --- a 13.7\% per-day reduction. Cumulated over the 1{,}824-day test window (2018--2026), the saving rises to 17.8\% (Figure~\ref{fig:capital_charge}).

Replacement-regime models face a less favourable trade-off. TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 require wider corrected thresholds ($W \approx 1.60$--$1.75$ relative to GJR-GARCH), exceeding the break-even value. At $W = 1.60$, the capital ratio is $(3.00/3.65) \times 1.60 = 1.315$: the wider VaR threshold more than offsets the Green-zone multiplier benefit.

\subsection{Practitioner Guidance}
\label{sec:practitioner}

The audit translates into three deployment rules.

\paragraph*{Per-asset VaR with adequate history}
When sufficient history is available, GJR-GARCH with conformal correction is the preferred configuration: it delivers the strongest per-asset Quantile Score performance (Section~\ref{sec:multiquantile}) and requires no pre-training infrastructure. For users requiring an explicit conditional density, GAMLSS-SST is a viable alternative (88.0\% Green, Table~\ref{tab:baselines}), albeit with higher estimation burden.

\paragraph*{Large-universe deployment}
When per-asset estimation is infeasible because of scale, limited history, or latency constraints, a TSFM combined with one-parameter conformal correction offers a scalable coverage-oriented alternative. The diagnostic magnitude $|\qVstat|$ should be monitored as a quality indicator: assets with persistently large corrections should be flagged for exclusion, fallback, or replacement.

\paragraph*{Model screening}
The rolling ratio $R_t = |\qVroll|/|\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}}|$ (equation~\eqref{eq:replacement_ratio_rolling}) should be monitored continuously, alongside the Basel zone and the Quantile Score, with no classification step. Read together the three answer different questions: the zone says whether the reported threshold is compliant, the Quantile Score says what sharpness it costs, and $R$ says how much of the reported threshold is the correction rather than the model. A forecaster can be compliant and uninformative --- Chronos-Mini reaches the Green Zone on 19 of 24 assets with $\bar R = 23.5$ --- and the zone alone cannot distinguish that case from CAViaR, which is compliant at $\bar R = 0.001$.

The sign of $R$ carries the diagnosis and should not be discarded. A small negative value indicates an over-conservative base that the correction tightens, releasing capital; GJR-GARCH is the only forecaster in our panel on that side, with $\qVstat < 0$ on 23 of 24 assets. A small positive value indicates a nearly calibrated base that the correction widens slightly.

Deployment advice follows from Section~\ref{sec:when_to_apply} rather than from a label: apply the correction when the raw forecast fails a backtest, and not otherwise. At $\alpha = 0.01$ that rule skips 44 of 240 pairs, avoids 41 of the 84 degradations the rolling estimator would otherwise cause, and retains all 176 Basel zone upgrades --- zero cost on the regulatory axis. It does not bind at $\alpha \geq 0.025$, where almost every raw forecast already fails, so the decision problem is specific to the extreme tail.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}

Extreme-tail forecast recalibration is constrained by a simple identification problem: at the 1\% level, the number of informative violations is small. This paper shows that, under this tail-sparse regime, a one-parameter conformal shift can serve not only as a recalibration device, but also as an audit statistic. Its magnitude, measured relative to the raw VaR forecast, reveals whether the base model retains usable tail information or whether the correction effectively replaces the forecast.

The audit's primary finding concerns the predictive interface. Within a single model family, the sample-based release is close to calibrated at the 1\% level and the quantile-grid release violates on almost every day; the same division holds across the wider set. Quantile-grid outputs do not carry enough information to produce a backtestable 1\% VaR, and once external tail completion supplies the missing tail, it is the closure rule rather than the pretrained model that determines accuracy. This is a property of how a forecast is exposed, not of how well it was trained, and it is actionable: it is visible before deployment, from the output format alone.

We make no claim that the correction improves forecasting ability. It restores marginal coverage, and that is all. Three results delimit it. It is an intervention with an indication rather than a general improvement: on base forecasts already passing Kupiec, the single-split correction is statistically indistinguishable from doing nothing, while the rolling variant degrades 93\% of them, and 60\% of those degradations purchase no change of Basel zone. It reaches only part of the problem: in the decomposition of \citet{gneiting2023regression}, a constant shift addresses a median 35\% of total miscalibration on the usable forecasters, the remainder being conditional. And it does not restore violation independence: where the Christoffersen test is defined it rejects on 62\% of pairs after correction, and duration-based tests flag clustering on a further 47 pairs where that test is undefined by construction.

The statistic itself is not new. For the quantile loss the score-minimising constant shift is the empirical residual quantile, whose score consequence is the unconditional miscalibration term of \citet{gneiting2023regression}; what $R$ adds is a scale, not a quantity, being expressed in units directly comparable to the VaR it corrects. Fitted per-asset GARCH benchmarks and CAViaR retain a Quantile Score advantage over the zero-shot foundation models, which is a statement about adaptation regimes rather than model quality. Whether fine-tuning removes the interface penalty, and whether a pooled independence statistic can detect the clustering that per-asset tests miss in the extreme tail, remain open.

\subsection{Expected Shortfall Computation and Heuristic Adjustment}
\label{app:es_raw}

For each base forecaster, raw ES is computed from the same predictive object
that produces the VaR forecast. For sample-based forecasters
(Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini, Moirai~1.1, and Lag-Llama), raw ES is the
empirical conditional tail mean,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_raw_es_sample}
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}(0.025)
	=
	-\frac{1}{\lfloor 0.025 N\rfloor}
	\sum_{i:\,\hat r_t^{(i)}\le \hat q_t(0.025)}
	\hat r_t^{(i)}.
\end{equation}
With $N=1{,}000$, this average is computed from the 25 lowest predictive
draws.

For quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0), raw ES is
computed from the same fitted Student-$t$ distribution used for VaR
tail-completion. Let $(\hat\nu_t,\hat\mu_t,\hat\sigma_t)$ denote the fitted
parameters. The lower-tail conditional expectation is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_raw_es_student}
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}(0.025)
	=
	-\Biggl(
	\hat\mu_t
	-
	\hat\sigma_t
	\frac{
		f_{\hat\nu_t}\bigl(t^{-1}_{\hat\nu_t}(0.025)\bigr)
		\left[
		\hat\nu_t+
		\bigl(t^{-1}_{\hat\nu_t}(0.025)\bigr)^2
		\right]
	}{
		(\hat\nu_t-1)\cdot 0.025
	}
	\Biggr),
\end{equation}
where $f_\nu(\cdot)$ and $t_\nu^{-1}(\cdot)$ denote the density and quantile
functions of the standard Student-$t$ distribution. The restriction
$\hat\nu_t>2$ ensures a well-defined scale.
For classical benchmarks, ES is computed from the model's native conditional
distribution: skewed-$t$ ES for GJR-GARCH
\citep{hansen1994autoregressive}, Gaussian ES for GARCH-Normal and EWMA,
and the empirical tail mean of the 250-day rolling window for Historical
Simulation.

The conformal correction shifts the predicted lower quantile by $\qVstat$, so as a supplementary diagnostic we apply the same location shift to ES:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_es_cp_shift}
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{cp}}
	=
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}+\qVstat,
	\qquad
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{roll}}
	=
	\widehat{\ES}_t^{\mathrm{raw}}+\qVroll.
\end{equation}
Under the positive-loss convention, $\qVstat>0$ indicates that the base
model underestimates tail risk, so both VaR and ES increase.
This adjustment preserves the location-shift interpretation of the VaR
correction but does not address tail-shape misspecification.

\subsection{Why Joint VaR--ES Conformal Recalibration Is Outside Scope}
\label{app:es_obstacles}

A formal conformal treatment of ES is more delicate than the VaR correction
studied in the main text. ES is not elicitable as a standalone functional
\citep{gneiting2011making}. Although $(\VaR,\ES)$ is jointly elicitable
\citep{fissler2016higher}, a joint conformal construction would require a
nonconformity score that accounts simultaneously for violation frequency and
tail-loss magnitude, introducing additional modelling choices.

Joint VaR--ES recalibration also creates a conditioning mismatch: raw ES
errors are evaluated on days defined by raw VaR violations, whereas corrected
ES would be evaluated on days defined by corrected VaR violations. Because
the conformal shift changes the violation set, the relevant tail-loss samples
need not coincide. ES is also more sensitive to tail sparsity than VaR,
since it depends on the average severity beyond the threshold rather than
the threshold alone; under temporal dependence, ES residuals therefore
inherit a more complex dependence structure than the one-sided VaR score.
For these reasons, joint VaR--ES conformal validity under temporal
dependence is left for future work.

\subsection{Empirical Diagnostic}
\label{app:es_empirical}

ES backtesting uses the Acerbi--Sz\'{e}kely $Z_2$ statistic. Let
$V_t=\mathbf{1}\{r_t<-\widehat{\VaR}_t\}$ denote the VaR violation
indicator. The statistic is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_z2}
	Z_2
	=
	\frac{1}{T\alpha}
	\sum_{t=1}^{T}
	\frac{V_t r_t}{\widehat{\ES}_t}
	+1 .
\end{equation}
Under correct joint tail specification, $Z_2$ should be close to zero.
The numerically stabilised variant and pass/fail conventions are detailed
in Appendix~\ref{app:backtesting}. Since the ES correction is heuristic,
$Z_2$ is interpreted only as a diagnostic of whether reported ES forecasts
are broadly consistent with realised tail losses.

\subsection{Baseline Specifications}
\label{app:baseline-specs}

\paragraph*{Single-shot post-hoc corrections}
Five baselines apply a single calibration map fitted on the
calibration split.

\textit{Historical quantile replacement} discards the base forecast
and returns the empirical $\alpha$-quantile of the calibration-window
returns,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:hq}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{HQ}}
	=
	-\,Q_{\alpha}\!\left(\{r_\tau\}_{\tau\in\mathcal C}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal C$ is the calibration set. Zero free parameters; the
base model is ignored entirely.

\textit{Scale correction} multiplies the raw forecast by a scalar
$c\in\mathbb R_{+}$ chosen so that the rescaled violation rate matches
$\alpha$ over $\mathcal C$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:sc}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{SC}} = c\,\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{raw}},
	\qquad
	c=\arg\min_{c>0}
	\left|
	\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal C}
	\mathbf 1\{r_\tau<-c\,\widehat{\VaR}_\tau^{\mathrm{raw}}\}-\alpha
	\right|.
\end{equation}
One parameter; preserves forecast dynamics but cannot correct level
bias of the wrong sign.

\textit{Linear quantile regression} fits an affine map at level
$\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qr_baseline}
	Q_\alpha(r_t\mid \hat q_t^{lo})
	= a + b\,\hat q_t^{lo},
	\qquad
	(\hat a,\hat b)
	=\arg\min_{a,b}
	\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal C}
	\rho_\alpha\!\left(r_\tau-a-b\,\hat q_\tau^{lo}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\alpha(u)=u(\alpha-\mathbf 1\{u<0\})$ is the pinball loss.
Two parameters: an intercept matching the conformal shift and a slope
that the conformal shift fixes at one.

\textit{Isotonic regression} replaces the linear map with a monotone
nonparametric mapping $\widehat g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, fitted by
pool-adjacent-violators on the calibration scores
$\{(\hat q_\tau^{lo},r_\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal C}$ with $\widehat g$
non-decreasing. Effectively $|\mathcal C|-1$ free parameters; flexible
in principle but unstable at the 1\% tail, where the effective sample
is of order $\alpha T$.

\textit{Adaptive Conformal Inference} (ACI) is sequential: it replaces
the fixed miscoverage level $\alpha$ with an online update
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:aci}
	\alpha_t=\alpha_{t-1}+\gamma\!\left(\alpha-\mathbf 1\{V_t\}\right),
	\qquad \gamma>0,
\end{equation}
where $V_t$ is the realised violation indicator
\citep{gibbs2021adaptive}. One free parameter $\gamma$; adapts the
conformal threshold without distributional assumptions.

\paragraph*{Distributional and nonlinear post-hoc corrections}
Two baselines move beyond scalar or monotone maps.

\textit{GAMLSS-SST} \citep{rigby2005generalized} models the conditional
return distribution as a skewed Student-$t$ density
$\mathcal{SST}(\mu_t,\sigma_t,\nu,\tau)$ with location driven by
$\hat q_t^{lo}$ and log-scale by lagged squared returns:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:gamlss}
	\mu_t=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\hat q_t^{lo},
	\quad
	\log\sigma_t = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\,r_{t-1}^2,
	\quad
	r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{SST}(\mu_t,\sigma_t,\nu,\tau).
\end{equation}
Four parameters $(\beta_1,\gamma_1,\nu,\tau)$ per asset; tests whether
a full conditional density improves on the one-parameter shift.

\textit{Gradient-boosted quantile regression} (GBM-QR) fits a nonlinear
quantile function via boosted regression trees minimising the pinball
loss,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:gbm_qr}
	\widehat f
	=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal F_{\mathrm{GBM}}}
	\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal C}
	\rho_\alpha\!\left(r_\tau-f(x_\tau)\right),
\end{equation}
with features $x_\tau$ comprising $\hat q_\tau^{lo}$ and lagged
realised volatility. The most flexible baseline; tests whether
nonlinearity extracts tail signal that linear methods miss.

\paragraph*{Dedicated VaR models}
Two baselines bypass the post-hoc framing and replace the base
forecast.

\textit{Extreme Value Theory with Peaks-Over-Threshold} (EVT-POT) fits
a generalised Pareto distribution $\mathrm{GPD}(\xi,\beta)$ to
threshold exceedances of GARCH-filtered residuals $\hat z_t$
\citep{mcneil2000estimation}:
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:evt_pot_gpd}
	\Pr(\hat z_t-u>y\mid \hat z_t>u)
	&=\left(1+\xi\,y/\beta\right)^{-1/\xi}, \\
	\label{eq:evt_pot_var}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{POT}}
	&=\hat\sigma_t\!\left[
	u+\tfrac{\beta}{\xi}\!\left((\tfrac{n}{n_u}\alpha)^{-\xi}-1\right)
	\right],
\end{align}
with $u$ the 90th percentile of $|\hat z_t|$, $n_u$ the exceedance
count, and $n$ the window size. Two parameters $(\xi,\beta)$ per
window; the only baseline with an explicit extreme-value tail model.

\textit{Filtered Historical Simulation} (FHS) standardises returns by
GARCH volatility and takes the empirical $\alpha$-quantile of the
standardised residuals \citep{baroneadesi1999var}:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:fhs}
	\hat z_\tau=\hat\varepsilon_\tau/\hat\sigma_\tau,
	\qquad
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\mathrm{FHS}}
	=\hat\sigma_t\,Q_\alpha\!\left(\{\hat z_\tau\}_{\tau\in\mathcal C}\right).
\end{equation}
Combines GARCH dynamics with a nonparametric tail; inherits GARCH
estimation instability in short windows.

\section{Backtesting Implementation Details}
\label{app:backtesting}
%============================================================

The Kupiec proportion of failures (POF) likelihood ratio statistic
tests $H_0:\hat\pi=\alpha$ against two-sided alternatives
\citep{kupiec1995}. For the boundary case $x=0$, the likelihood
ratio is evaluated using the continuous-limit convention
$(x/T)^x=1$; the $p$-value is computed from $\chi^2_1$.

The Christoffersen conditional coverage test \citep{christoffersen1998}
augments the unconditional coverage test with an independence component.
The joint statistic is $\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{CC}}=
\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}}+\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{IND}}\sim\chi^2_2$.

The $Z_2$ statistic (Eq.~\ref{eq:app_z2}) evaluates Expected Shortfall
consistency. In our implementation, we replace the per-day ES
denominator with the time-averaged ES,
$\overline{\ES}=T^{-1}\sum_{t}\widehat{\ES}_t^\alpha$, giving
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_z2_mod}
	Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}
	=
	\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_t r_t}
	{T\alpha\,\overline{\ES}}
	+1.
\end{equation}
This modification avoids division by near-zero daily ES values; the
mean absolute difference from the canonical formulation is 0.035 $Z_2$
units, and the modified and canonical versions agree on the 5\% pass/fail
classification for 97\% of model--asset pairs.


\subsection{Diagnostic Regression Specification}
\label{app:diag_regression_spec}

The diagnostic regression of the Quantile Score improvement on the
conformal threshold and the raw violation rate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dqs_regression}
\Delta\mathrm{QS}_{m,a}
= \beta_0
+ \beta_1\,(\qVstat)_{m,a}
+ \beta_2\,\hat\pi^{\mathrm{raw}}_{m,a}
+ \varepsilon_{m,a},
\end{equation}
estimated across $n=240$ model--asset pairs $(m,a)$. Standard errors
are computed under one-way clustering by asset (24 clusters) and,
separately, by model (10 clusters). The partial $R^{2}$ for $\qVstat$
is computed by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell residualisation on
$\hat\pi^{\mathrm{raw}}$. Coefficients are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:diag_regression}.

\subsection{Diebold--Mariano with Panel HAC}
\label{app:dm_panel}

Quantile Score differences between forecasters $j$ and $k$ are tested
with the \citet{diebold1995comparing} statistic
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dm_stat}
\mathrm{DM}_{jk}
= \frac{\bar d_{jk}}
{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\bar d_{jk})}},
\qquad
d_{jk,t} = \mathrm{QS}^{j}_{t}-\mathrm{QS}^{k}_{t},
\end{equation}
with the \citet{harvey1997testing} small-sample correction. To
accommodate cross-asset dependence, the variance is the
\citet{driscoll1998consistent} panel HAC estimator with a Bartlett
kernel and \citet{newey1994automatic} automatic bandwidth.

\subsection{Panel-Pooled Kupiec}
\label{app:panel_kupiec}

The panel-pooled Kupiec statistic pools the violation series across
the 24 assets, giving approximately $36{,}600$ test observations per
model. Under the null $H_0\colon\hat\pi_{m,a}=\alpha$ for all $a$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:panel_kupiec}
\mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}}^{\mathrm{panel}}
=
-2\log\!\left\{
\frac{(1-\alpha)^{N-X}\alpha^{X}}
{(1-X/N)^{N-X}(X/N)^{X}}
\right\},
\quad
X=\sum_{a,t}V_{a,t},
\quad
N=\sum_a T_a,
\end{equation}
distributed asymptotically as $\chi^{2}_1$. Cluster-robust inference
at the asset level is obtained from the same Driscoll--Kraay panel
HAC estimator used for the Diebold--Mariano statistic, with violations
clustered by asset; the implied number of independent clusters is 24.
Wild-cluster bootstrap finite-sample $p$-values are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:panel_robustness}
(Table~\ref{tab:wildcluster_kupiec}).

\subsection{Supporting Lemmas}

\begin{lemma}[Inheritance of weak dependence by the score process]
	\label{lem:score_mixing}
	Let $\{r_t\}$ be strictly stationary and $\beta$-mixing with coefficients
	$\beta_r(k)$. Suppose the forecast $\hat q_t^{lo}$ is
	$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable and generated by a time-invariant
	measurable functional $g$, i.e.,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_forecast_functional}
		\hat q_t^{lo}=g(r_{t-1},r_{t-2},\ldots).
	\end{equation}
	Then the score process $S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ is strictly stationary
	and $\beta$-mixing with coefficients satisfying
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_score_mixing_bound}
		\beta_S(k)\le \beta_r(k-1),
		\qquad k\ge2.
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	By assumption, $\{r_t\}$ is strictly stationary. Since $\hat q_t^{lo}$ is
	a time-invariant measurable functional of the past, the joint process
	$(\hat q_t^{lo},r_t)$ is also strictly stationary. Therefore,
	$S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ inherits strict stationarity.
	
	Let $\mathcal S_{-\infty}^t=\sigma(S_s:s\le t)$ and
	$\mathcal S_{t+k}^{\infty}=\sigma(S_s:s\ge t+k)$. Since $S_t$ is a
	measurable function of $(r_t,r_{t-1},\ldots)$,
	\[
	\mathcal S_{-\infty}^t
	\subseteq
	\mathcal R_{-\infty}^t:=\sigma(r_s:s\le t),
	\]
	and
	\[
	\mathcal S_{t+k}^{\infty}
	\subseteq
	\mathcal R_{t+k-1}^{\infty}:=\sigma(r_s:s\ge t+k-1).
	\]
	By monotonicity of $\beta$-mixing coefficients under measurable
	transformations,
	\[
	\beta_S(k)
	\le
	\beta(\mathcal R_{-\infty}^t,\mathcal R_{t+k-1}^{\infty})
	=
	\beta_r(k-1).
	\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Empirical quantile deviation under $\beta$-mixing]
	\label{lem:quantile_mixing}
	Under Assumption~\ref{ass:main}, let
	\[
	\qVstat=\hat F_n^{-1}(1-\alpha),
	\qquad
	q_{1-\alpha}=F_S^{-1}(1-\alpha).
	\]
	Then
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_quantile_rate}
		|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
		=
		\mathcal O_p\!\left(
		\frac{1}{f_S(q_{1-\alpha})}
		\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
		\right).
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Under geometric $\beta$-mixing as in Assumption~\ref{ass:main}\ref{ass:stationary},
	the empirical CDF satisfies the uniform concentration bound
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_ecdf_bound}
		\sup_{s\in\mathbb R}|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|
		=
		\mathcal O_p\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right);
	\end{equation}
	see \citet[Theorem 2.1]{yu1994rates} and
	\citet[Theorem 7.2]{rio2017asymptotic}.
	 Since $\qVstat$ is the
	empirical $(1-\alpha)$-quantile,
	\[
	\hat F_n(\qVstat)=1-\alpha+\mathcal O(1/n).
	\]
	Thus,
	\[
	|F_S(\qVstat)-(1-\alpha)|
	\le
	\sup_s|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|+\mathcal O(1/n).
	\]
	By the density condition in Assumption~\ref{ass:main},
	$f_S(q_{1-\alpha})$ is bounded away from zero in a neighbourhood of
	$q_{1-\alpha}$. A first-order expansion around $q_{1-\alpha}$ gives
	\[
	|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
	\le
	\frac{1}{f_S(q_{1-\alpha})}
	\left[
	\sup_s|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|+\mathcal O(1/n)
	\right],
	\]
	which yields the stated rate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var}}

\begin{proof}
	The proof proceeds in three steps.
	
	\textit{Step 1: Reduction to score control.}
	By definition of the nonconformity score
	$S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:proof_score_event}
		S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat
		\Longleftrightarrow
		r_{n+g_n+1}\ge \hat q_{n+g_n+1}^{lo}-\qVstat.
	\end{equation}
	It therefore suffices to show
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:proof_target_coverage}
		\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\Delta_n.
	\end{equation}
	
\textit{Step 2: Quantile approximation.}
Let $q_{1-\alpha}=F_S^{-1}(1-\alpha)$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:quantile_mixing},
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_qv_rate}
	|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
	=
	\mathcal O_p\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
\end{equation}
By the density condition,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_fs_qv_bound}
	F_S(\qVstat)
	\ge
	1-\alpha
	-
	C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
\end{equation}
for a finite constant $C>0$.

\textit{Step 3: Gap-separated dependence control.}
By $\beta$-mixing, conditional on the calibration sample
$\mathcal D_n=(S_1,\ldots,S_n)$,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_conditional_gap}
	\bigl|
	\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat\mid\mathcal D_n)
	-
	F_S(\qVstat)
	\bigr|
	\le
	\beta(g_n).
\end{equation}
Taking expectations over $\mathcal D_n$ and combining with
\eqref{eq:proof_fs_qv_bound} gives
\[
\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat)
\ge
\mathbb E\!\left[F_S(\qVstat)\right]-\beta(g_n)
\ge
1-\alpha
-
C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
-\beta(g_n).
\]
Setting
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_delta_n}
	\Delta_n
	=
	C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\beta(g_n)
\end{equation}
completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:garch_conformal_var}}

\begin{proof}
	Under $\omega>0$, $\alpha_1,\beta_1\ge0$, and
	$\mathbb E[\log(\alpha_1\varepsilon_t^2+\beta_1)]<0$, the GARCH(1,1)
	process admits a unique strictly stationary solution
	\citep[Theorem~2.10]{francq2019garch}. Under standard regularity
	conditions, it is geometrically $\beta$-mixing
	\citep{francq2019garch,lindner2009stationarity}:
	\[
	\beta_r(k)\le a\rho^k,
	\qquad \rho\in(0,1).
	\]
	By Lemma~\ref{lem:score_mixing}, the score process is also strictly
	stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing. The density condition holds
	under the assumed continuous innovation density near the target quantile.
	
	Choosing $g_n=c\log n$ with $c>1/|\log\rho|$ gives
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_garch_gap_rate}
		\beta(g_n)\le a\rho^{c\log n}
		=
		\mathcal O(n^{-c|\log\rho|})
		=
		o\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
	\end{equation}
	Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} therefore applies with
	\[
	\Delta_n
	=
	\mathcal O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
	\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Online Gradient Descent Regret Bound for Rolling Quantiles}
\label{app:ogd_regret}

The following two results adapt existing bounds to the rolling conformal
setting; their scope relative to the empirical implementation is discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:rolling_method}.

\begin{proposition}[Online quantile-tracking support for rolling calibration]
	\label{prop:ogd_regret}
	Let $q_t$ evolve according to
	$q_{t+1}=q_t+\eta[(1-\alpha)-\mathbf{1}\{s_t\le q_t\}]$,
	with bounded scores $|s_t|\le R$ and step size $\eta=R/\sqrt T$. Then
	$|T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha|\le O(T^{-1/2})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ogd_regret}]
	The pinball loss is convex and 1-Lipschitz in $q$ on the feasible
	set $\mathcal Q=[-R,R]$. Standard online gradient descent results
	\citep[Theorem~3.1]{zinkevich2003online} imply average regret of
	order $R/\sqrt T$ for step size $\eta=R/\sqrt T$.
	Under Assumption~\ref{ass:main}\ref{ass:density}, the loss is
	locally Lipschitz in the indicator parametrisation near the
	population minimiser, so $\mathcal O(T^{-1/2})$ regret on the
	pinball loss implies $\mathcal O(T^{-1/2})$ deviation in
	time-averaged miscoverage, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

This bound is dependence-free: it holds for arbitrary score sequences and
requires no mixing, stationarity, or exchangeability assumptions. It controls
marginal time-average coverage, not conditional or per-window
coverage. The rolling uniform-weight quantile used in practice is a
finite-window empirical quantile, not literal OGD; in stationary
regimes, both target the same population quantile, while under
non-stationarity the rolling window reduces bias at the cost of increased
variance near regime transitions.

\subsection{Non-Exchangeable Coverage Bound with Distributional Drift}
\label{app:rolling_drift}

\begin{proposition}[Finite-window coverage under drift]
	\label{prop:rolling_drift}
	Let
	\[
	\qVroll=Q_{1-\alpha}\!\left(\{s_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}\right)
	\]
	and define
	\[
	\delta_w(t)=\max_{\tau\in[t-w,t-1]}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)).
	\]
	Under local stationarity conditions analogous to
	Assumption~\ref{ass:main},
	\[
	\P(r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll)\ge 1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w,
	\]
	where $\Delta_w\le C\sqrt{(\log w)/w}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:rolling_drift}]
	Let $S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ and
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_qvt}
		\qVroll
		=
		Q_{1-\alpha}\left(\{S_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}\right).
	\end{equation}
	The coverage event satisfies
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_score_event}
		r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll
		\Longleftrightarrow
		S_t\le \qVroll.
	\end{equation}
	
	Following the weighted conformal framework of
	\citet{barber2023conformal}, the coverage deviation under
	non-exchangeability is bounded by the average total variation distance
	between calibration-window score distributions and the test distribution:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_tv_bound}
		\left|
		\mathbb P(S_t\le\qVroll)-(1-\alpha)
		\right|
		\le
		\frac{1}{w}
		\sum_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}
		d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)\right).
	\end{equation}
	By definition,
	\[
	\frac{1}{w}
	\sum_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}
	d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)\right)
	\le
	\delta_w(t).
	\]
	The empirical quantile estimated from a dependent window of size $w$
	adds the finite-sample remainder
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_delta_w}
		\Delta_w\le C\sqrt{\frac{\log w}{w}}.
	\end{equation}
	Combining the drift and estimation terms yields
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_score_bound}
		\mathbb P(S_t\le\qVroll)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w.
	\end{equation}
	Returning to the score representation gives
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_bound}
		\mathbb P\!\left(
		r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll
		\right)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w.
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}

The bound separates distributional drift, captured by $\delta_w(t)$, from
finite-sample estimation error under dependence, captured by $\Delta_w$.
In the empirical implementation, $\delta_w(t)$ is proxied by the empirical
total variation distance between the first and second halves of the rolling
window. Using $\sqrt w$ histogram bins, define
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_delta_hat}
	\hat\delta_w(t)
	=
	\frac{1}{2}\sum_b |\hat p_{1,b}-\hat p_{2,b}|,
\end{equation}
where $\hat p_{1,b}$ and $\hat p_{2,b}$ are empirical bin frequencies over
the first and second halves of the window. When $\hat\delta_w(t)$ exceeds a
practitioner-chosen threshold, the bound weakens and a shorter window or
temporary deployment pause may be warranted.

\subsection{Tail-Completion Rule Sensitivity}
\label{app:tail_closure}

TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 require tail completion to obtain the
1\% quantile from their nine-decile grids
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}); the position of the high-$\bar R$ forecasters
therefore depends on the extrapolation rule, and a single rule
should not be load-bearing.

Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended} compares the default
Student-$t$ closure with Gaussian closure and linear extrapolation
across three representative assets. All closures yield $R>1$ for
both models, confirming that the position of the high-$\bar R$ forecasters
is not an artefact of the Student-$t$ tail-completion layer. The
inner-seven-deciles refit ($u\in\{0.2,\ldots,0.8\}$) is the more
demanding check: dropping the outermost quantiles that anchor the
tail, we still recover $\qVstat$ and ordering by $\bar R$
identical to the full nine-decile fit for all six model--asset
combinations. The extrapolation is determined by the body of the
predictive distribution, not by its extreme quantiles.

Moirai~1.1 provides a clean control. As a sample-based forecaster,
it bypasses tail completion entirely and remains in the
low-$\bar R$ range ($R\approx0.11$). The contrast within the
Moirai family --- same architecture, same training, no tail
completion for 1.1 versus three closure rules and an inner-seven-deciles
refit tested for 2.0, all yielding the same ordering by $\bar R$ ---
isolates the forecast interface as the driver, separately from the
closure choice.

\subsection{Gap Parameter Ablation}
\label{app:gap_ablation}

Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} requires a gap of
$g_n \to \infty$ unused observations between calibration and
evaluation, ensuring that the test point is far enough from the
calibration sample for the $\beta$-mixing bound to be tight. The
empirical implementation uses adjacent splits ($g_n = 0$): the
calibration sample ends at time $n$ and the evaluation begins at
$n+1$, with no observations discarded in between. This is the
operational standard in conformal prediction, but is not covered
directly by the theorem. To check whether the gap requirement
matters in practice, we compare two implementations on four
representative model--asset pairs: $g_n = 0$ (the operational
choice) and $g_n = \lceil c \log n \rceil$ (the minimal gap
satisfying the theorem), where $c = 1/|\log \hat\rho|$ and
$\hat\rho$ is the AR(1) coefficient of the calibration scores.

Table~\ref{tab:gap_ablation} shows that the choice of $g_n$ has
negligible empirical impact. The absolute difference in corrected
violation rates is at most 0.2 percentage points, and Basel Traffic
Light classifications are identical for all four pairs. The
theoretical gap requirement is therefore empirically non-binding
for the four pairs tested, which span the score-persistence range
$\hat\rho \in [0.18, 0.67]$.

\section{Series Validation Gate}
\label{app:validation_gate}

Every forecast series used in this paper passes a scripted validation gate
before it is admitted to the analysis. The gate is run in continuous
integration, emits the table below, and blocks promotion of any series that
fails a check; a failing series receives a written diagnosis and no tolerance is
widened to accommodate it. The implementation is
\texttt{scripts/promotion\_gate.py} in the replication package.

\begin{table}[H]
	\centering
	\caption{Series validation checks. Each exists because a defect of that
		class was found in this project's own pipeline. The applicability
		column records the estimator classes a check applies to; scoping is
		declared in the series manifest rather than applied implicitly.}
	\label{tab:validation_gate}
	\footnotesize
	\begin{tabular}{p{3.1cm}p{7.4cm}p{2.6cm}}

		\textbf{Check} & \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Applies to} \\

		\hline\hline
	\end{tabular}

The last two checks apply only to sample-based forecasters. An empirical
estimator such as Historical Simulation returns an order statistic of a
250-observation window and therefore takes few distinct values by construction;
that is a property of the estimator rather than a defect, and it is recorded as
a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} rather than treated as a failure.

Two further invariants are enforced over the data directories themselves rather
than over individual series: every file present must be named in the series
manifest, and every name in the manifest must exist on disk. Both directions are
required. The manifest records, for each series, the model, asset, path, version
and estimator class.
