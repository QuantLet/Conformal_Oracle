\begin{abstract}

	A tail-risk backtest reports \emph{that} a Value-at-Risk series miscovers. It cannot report \emph{why}. We show that this gap is where forecasting pipelines fail, and that the failures are neither rare nor exotic.

	We audit a \nMainAssets-asset, \nMainForecasters-forecaster panel and trace five defects in our own pipeline to specific lines of code: two sign inversions, an unstandardised Student-$t$ quantile in a GARCH benchmark, and two data-handling faults. The cleanest case is in published third-party code and reproduces from a public checkpoint without our data. Chronos-T5 represents its one-step predictive law as a categorical distribution over 4093 quantised bins; its packaged sampler defaults to $\texttt{top\_k} = 50$, discarding 98.8\% of that support, from the tails inward, before any quantile is read. The median is barely moved, so point-forecast evaluation is normal, while the 1\% violation rate reaches \nPiSmallDefaultOne{} and \nPiMiniDefaultOne. A controlled dose--response on identical contexts and seeds establishes the mechanism, and the parameters a practitioner would vary --- temperature, nucleus sampling --- move predictive dispersion by 0.001 and 0.006. Reading the law in closed form instead of sampling it has no sampling parameters, costs one forward pass, and moves the rate to \nPiSmallAnalyticOne{} and \nPiMiniAnalyticOne.

	Such failures survive the instruments applied to them. Kupiec's test rejects \nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\% of pairs at $\alpha = 0.01$, defective and merely imperfect series alike; the Christoffersen statistic is degenerate on \nSeqCCUndefRawOne\% of raw pairs and \nSeqCCUndefCorOne\% after recalibration, where its asymptotics do not hold; and a conformal shift targets marginal coverage whatever forecaster it corrects, so on a sign-inverted series it delivered 19 of \nMainAssets{} Basel green zones and every backtest downstream of it passed.

	We therefore validate structurally, before a series is calibrated or evaluated: two invariants that follow from what a quantile forecast is, one invariant tested through a proxy, and seven plausibility bands whose thresholds we state rather than derive. The gate blocks \nGateBlocked{} of \nGateSeries{} series here. Correctly configured, the zero-shot foundation models in this panel are neither catastrophically miscalibrated nor competitive --- they sit alongside GARCH-family benchmarks at the 1\% level, and a per-asset CAViaR fitted the ordinary way outperforms all of them.

	\bigskip\noindent
	\textbf{Keywords:} Value-at-Risk; Structural validation; Time series
	foundation models; Conformal prediction; Regulatory backtesting;
	Reproducibility

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A Value-at-Risk forecast is not a model output. It is the result of reducing a
model output --- a set of sampled paths, a grid of predictive quantiles, a fitted
parametric law, an order statistic over a window --- to a single number, and that
reduction is performed by code that the forecasting literature does not treat as
an object of study. This paper reports what happens when it is studied: in a
24-asset, 16-forecaster Value-at-Risk panel, the dominant driver of measured
extreme-tail failure is the configuration of that reduction step, not the
architecture of the model, its pretraining corpus, or the format in which it
exposes its predictive distribution.

The evidence is unusually direct, because the defects are our own. An audit of
the pipeline behind an earlier version of this paper traced five errors to
specific lines of code: two foundation-model series stored the $\alpha$-quantile
with its sign inverted; one classical GJR-GARCH benchmark applied an
unstandardised Student-$t$ quantile with its degrees of freedom hard-coded; and
two data-handling faults propagated a ticker alias and a stale forecast vintage.
Each is arithmetic rather than judgement, and each is documented with the
diagnostic that establishes it in the replication package. Their joint effect was
not noise. They moved reported 1\% violation rates by factors between 4 and 70,
and they moved them in both directions --- the GJR-GARCH defect inflated every
threshold by roughly 45\%, producing a 0.42\% violation rate against a nominal
1\% and placing the benchmark in the Basel green zone on all 24 assets. A defect
that flatters its owner attracts no scrutiny; that one was found last.

We develop the argument on the case that is not ours, because it is in published
third-party code and reproduces from a public checkpoint without our data.
Chronos-T5 \citep{ansari2024chronos} represents its predictive distribution as a
categorical law over 4093 quantised value bins. Its packaged sampling routine
defaults to $\texttt{top\_k} = 50$, a value inherited from text generation, which
discards 98.8\% of that support before any quantile is computed --- and discards
it from the tails inward. The median is barely affected, so a point-forecast
evaluation of this configuration is entirely normal. The 1\% tail is destroyed:
the empirical violation rate reaches 0.3884 and 0.4188 for the two checkpoint
sizes we audit, a thirty-nine-fold exceedance that would place every asset in the
Basel red zone. This is not a bug in Chronos. It is a documented default, correct
for the task it was designed for, silently wrong for this one.

Three properties make such failures durable rather than merely possible. First,
they are invisible to point-forecast evaluation, which is where forecasting
models are normally validated. Second, they are invisible to a responsible
sensitivity analysis: varying temperature across a fourfold range moves
predictive dispersion by 0.001 and nucleus sampling by 0.006, so an analyst who
sweeps the parameters that are documented and discussed will correctly report the
model as robust. Third --- and this is the part that concerns the tail-risk
literature specifically --- they are invisible to the backtests that regulate
this domain.

That third claim is measured rather than asserted. At $\alpha = 0.01$ the
independence test of \citet{christoffersen1998} is \emph{undefined} on 34.6\% of
our 312 model--asset pairs, because a 1\% tail generates too few exceedances to
populate a transition table, and on 53.5\% of them once the forecasts have been
recalibrated --- which is where every backtest in the earlier version of this
paper was computed, since a correction that spaces violations out disables the
test further. The share falls monotonically to 0.0\% at $\alpha = 0.10$ under
both readings. A degenerate table is an absence of evidence, but the natural
implementation returns ``no rejection'', which reads as a pass. Unconditional
coverage \citep{kupiec1995} is better defined and rejects
\nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\% of pairs at $\alpha = 0.01$ against
\nSeqKupiecRejRawTen\% at $\alpha = 0.10$; when a test rejects the majority
of defensible models, its rejection of any one of them carries little
information, and a genuinely defective series is not distinguishable from a
merely imperfect one. Both instruments are weakest exactly at the level at which
the Basel traffic-light system operates \citep{basel1996}.

Post-hoc recalibration does not rescue the situation; it conceals it. A
split-conformal shift restores marginal coverage regardless of the forecaster it
is applied to --- exactly and in finite samples under exchangeability
\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution}, and asymptotically under the
$\beta$-mixing conditions that actually hold here (Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var}).
The distinction matters for the theory and not at all for the argument of this
paragraph: on our data it delivers. Applied to a sign-inverted
series it placed 19 of 24 assets in the Basel green zone. Every backtest reported
in the earlier version of this paper was computed downstream of recalibration, so
every one of them passed. The errors survived because the method worked.

\paragraph{Contributions} First, we establish the mechanism of the sampling
defect by controlled experiment rather than inference, and remove it. Holding
contexts, seeds and weights fixed and varying only the sampling configuration,
predictive dispersion recovers monotonically as the truncation is relaxed and one
thousand draws under the default contain exactly 50 distinct values on every
asset and both checkpoints. We then show that sampling is unnecessary at a
one-step horizon, where the predictive law is categorical and its quantiles are
available in closed form, and give an estimator that reads them directly from the
model's logits: it agrees with full-vocabulary sampling to within Monte Carlo
error, has no $\texttt{num\_samples}$, no $\texttt{top\_k}$ and no seed, and
costs one forward pass. Under it the violation rate falls from 0.3884 and 0.4188
to 0.0175 and 0.0178.

Second, we quantify the blindness of the standard tail diagnostics, as above,
and separate two claims that are usually merged: that these tests are being
misapplied, and that they are being applied where they have the least power. It
is the second. This is the argument for validating a forecast series
structurally, before it is calibrated or evaluated.

Third, we propose such a validation gate --- ten checks on a forecast series,
of which two are invariants, one an invariant tested through a proxy, and seven
plausibility bands with thresholds we state rather than derive. Eight need
nothing but the series and the returns; two need an evaluation window, and we say
so rather than claim the gate is entirely free of one. We report its scoping
honestly in the other direction too: two of the checks are inapplicable to order-statistic estimators and
say so rather than returning a verdict, and all ten were written after the
defects were known, so their performance on those defects is in-sample by
construction. The gate blocks \nGateBlocked{} of \nGateSeries{} series in this panel.

Fourth, for the recalibration layer we retain an asymptotic coverage guarantee
for the single-split estimator under $\beta$-mixing dependence with explicit
gap-separation conditions, together with finite-window bounds for the rolling
variant under distributional drift, and we report the intervention's
\emph{indication} rather than its average effect. Recalibration is close to free
where it is not needed and harmful where the base forecast is already calibrated:
under the rolling estimator, \nWellCalRollWorse{} of the \nWellCalRollN{} pairs
that pass Kupiec on the test window are degraded by correcting them --- a
description of who is harmed, not yet a rule for deciding. Gating the correction on a failed raw backtest
helps, but far less than an in-sample evaluation of that rule suggests: keyed on
the calibration window, which is the only information available at the decision
date, it avoids \nGateRollAvoidedPct\% of the damage at $\alpha = 0.01$ and costs
\nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of \nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades. Keyed on
the evaluation window it would avoid \nGateRollOracleAvoidedPct\% and cost none,
and we report that only as the oracle it is.

\paragraph{What this paper does not claim} It does not claim that foundation
models are unsuitable for tail-risk forecasting, nor that they are ready for it.
Correctly configured, the six zero-shot foundation-model series in our panel are
neither catastrophically miscalibrated nor competitive: their raw 1\% violation
rates run from 0.0143 to 0.0294, which places them alongside the GARCH-family
benchmarks on the same assets and windows, and a per-asset CAViaR
\citep{engle2004caviar} fitted the ordinary way outperforms all of them. It does
not claim that configuration failure explains tail miscalibration in general ---
we observe five defects in one pipeline, and the frequency of such defects in the
literature is unknown, though we note that neither of the two recent tail-risk
papers we surveyed that use sample-based foundation models reports a single
generation parameter. And it does not claim that the ten structural checks are
sufficient. They are necessary conditions, cheap ones, and the argument for them
is that the alternative --- coverage --- provably cannot do this job.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}

This paper relates to three strands: tail miscalibration in probabilistic forecasters, regulatory backtesting of tail-risk forecasts, and post-hoc calibration under temporal dependence.

Tail miscalibration is well-documented across forecaster classes. Parametric volatility models can systematically under- or over-estimate extreme quantiles under heavy-tailed innovations or regime shifts \citep{christoffersen1998,mcneil2000estimation,nolde2017elicitability}. Recent time series foundation models (TSFMs) --- including Chronos \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}, TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, Moirai \citep{woo2024moirai,liu2025moirai}, and Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2024lagllama} --- achieve competitive zero-shot forecasting performance but exhibit a similar pattern: \citet{goel2024tsfmvar} show that pre-trained quantiles require fine-tuning for acceptable Value-at-Risk (VaR) coverage, and \citet{pele2025llmvar} identify tail compression in instruction-tuned models, with similar miscalibration documented for volatility forecasting \citep{goel2025volatility,rahimikia2025revisiting}. These findings, spanning parametric and foundation-model regimes alike, motivate post-hoc calibration but do not address the identification constraint imposed by extreme-tail sparsity, and they stop short of a taxonomy of when post-hoc correction adjusts an informative base versus when it acts as an empirical fallback.

Regulatory backtesting procedures \citep{kupiec1995,christoffersen1998,basel1996,acerbi2014back,fissler2016higher,nolde2017elicitability} detect violations of nominal coverage, exceedance dependence, and tail-loss inconsistency, but do not prescribe forecast corrections.

Post-hoc calibration adjusts the output of a fixed forecaster without retraining \citep{gneiting2013combining,taillardat2016calibrated}. These procedures sit within the broader probabilistic-forecasting literature on calibration--sharpness trade-offs \citep{gneiting2007probabilistic,gneiting2014probabilistic}, where calibration restoration is balanced against sharpness loss. Model-based alternatives, such as CAViaR \citep{engle2004caviar} and conditional-density frameworks such as the Generalised Additive Model for Location, Scale and Shape \citep[GAMLSS;][]{rigby2005generalized}, estimate tail behaviour directly but impose structure on the conditional distribution. Conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic}, applied to quantile regression by \citet{romano2019conformalized} as conformalized quantile regression (CQR), provides a model-free alternative. Standard guarantees rely on exchangeability, which is violated in financial time series by volatility clustering, serial dependence, and structural change; extensions to dependent or non-exchangeable data include weighted conformal methods \citep{barber2023conformal}, blocking schemes \citep{chernozhukov2018exact}, and adaptive procedures \citep{zaffran2022conformal,xu2024conformal,angelopoulos2024pid}.

The TSFM literature receives closer attention above because this class is recent and has not yet been the subject of a systematic 1\% tail audit; the parametric volatility literature is mature and enters this paper through standard benchmarks rather than fresh review.

Building on these strands, this paper differs from all of them in where it looks. \citet{goel2024tsfmvar} and \citet{goel2025volatility} reduce TSFM tail miscalibration by fine-tuning; \citet{gibbs2021adaptive} deliver adaptive coverage under nonstationarity by online updates of the miscoverage rate; \citet{tamingtailrisk2026} extend conformal risk control to nonstationary portfolio VaR through regime-similarity weighting. Each treats the forecast series as given and improves what is done to it. We ask instead whether the series is what it is claimed to be, and find that in our own pipeline it repeatedly was not --- for reasons that lie in the code that converts a predictive distribution into a threshold, and that no procedure downstream of that conversion can detect.

There is a further gap this paper can only point at. Neither of the two recent tail-risk studies we surveyed that use sample-based foundation models reports a single generation parameter --- no \texttt{num\_samples}, \texttt{top\_k}, \texttt{top\_p}, temperature or seed appears in either. \citet{rahimikia2025revisiting} is the clean case: it is centrally about foundation models in finance, Chronos is its most-used model, and none of these parameters occurs anywhere in the text. Two papers do not establish a practice, and we do not claim they do; what they establish is that the reporting convention which would make this class of defect visible to a reader does not currently exist.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:framework}

The audit has two layers: a base model $M$ producing one-step-ahead probabilistic forecasts (Section~\ref{sec:layer1}), and a one-parameter conformal shift that recalibrates the predicted lower quantile (Section~\ref{sec:layer2}). Two implementations of the second layer are developed in Section~\ref{sec:layer2}: a single-split estimator, which admits asymptotic coverage under $\beta$-mixing dependence, and a rolling variant.

\paragraph*{Notation}
Let $r_t$ denote the daily log return and $\hat q_t^{lo} = (\widehat F_t^M)^{-1}(\alpha)$ the predicted lower $\alpha$-quantile from base model~$M$; we fix $\alpha = 0.01$. VaR is reported as a positive loss threshold:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_raw}
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}.
\end{equation}
The nonconformity score is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:score}
	S_t = \hat q_t^{lo} - r_t,
\end{equation}
and the conformal shift is denoted $q_V$, with single-split and rolling variants
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_cp_roll}
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{cp}} = \VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} + q_V^{\mathrm{stat}},
	\qquad
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{roll}} = \VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} + q_V^{\mathrm{roll}}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer~1: Probabilistic Forecasting}
\label{sec:layer1}

\subsubsection{Base Model Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The first layer produces one-step-ahead probabilistic forecasts of returns from a fixed base model~$M$.

For sample-based forecasters (Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini, Moirai~1.1, and Lag-Llama), the forecast at time~$t$ consists of $N = 1{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws $\{\hat r_t^{i;M}\}_{i=1}^N$, yielding the empirical predictive CDF
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:ecdf_sample}
	\widehat F_t^M(x)
	= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\mathbf{1}\{\hat r_t^{i;M}\leq x\}.
\end{equation}
The raw VaR forecast is the empirical lower $\alpha$-quantile,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_sample}
	\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\alpha;M}
	= -(\widehat F_t^M)^{-1}(\alpha).
\end{equation}

For quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0), the predictive object is a finite grid of conditional quantiles $\{\hat q_t(u_j)\}_{j=1}^{J}$, with $J = 9$ deciles. Since the audit operates at the 1\% lower-tail level, we obtain $\hat q_t(0.01)$ by fitting a three-parameter Student-$t$ distribution to the native predicted quantiles:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:tail_completion}
	(\hat\nu_t, \hat\mu_t, \hat\sigma_t)
	=
	\arg\min_{\nu, \mu, \sigma}
	\sum_{j=1}^{J}
	\left[
	\hat q_t(u_j)
	-
	\left\{
	\mu + \sigma\, t_{\nu}^{-1}(u_j)
	\right\}
	\right]^2,
	\qquad \nu > 2,\ \sigma > 0,
\end{equation}
and set
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_grid}
	\hat q_t(0.01)
	=
	\hat\mu_t + \hat\sigma_t\, t_{\hat\nu_t}^{-1}(0.01).
\end{equation}
The Student-$t$ closure is a parsimonious heavy-tailed default in financial VaR modelling \citep{bollerslev1987,hansen1994autoregressive,mcneil2000estimation}. It is not, however, an innocuous choice: Appendix~\ref{app:tail_closure} shows that the closure rule governs the tail behaviour of a grid model far more than the model does, so this decision is reported as part of the forecaster rather than as an implementation detail.

For classical benchmarks (GJR-GARCH, GARCH-Normal, EWMA, Historical Simulation), VaR is computed from the model-implied conditional distribution or, for Historical Simulation, the empirical 250-day rolling distribution.

Expected Shortfall is reported separately at $\alpha = 0.025$ following the Basel III/FRTB convention \citep{bcbs2019frtb} and is computed from the same predictive object as VaR. Because ES is not elicitable as a standalone functional \citep{gneiting2011making}, the conformal construction is developed for VaR only; ES computation details and the heuristic ES shift are in Supplement~S.1.

\subsubsection{Diagnosing Miscalibration}
\label{sec:diag}

We apply the standard regulatory backtests --- Kupiec~\citep{kupiec1995} for unconditional coverage, Christoffersen~\citep{christoffersen1998} for joint coverage and serial independence, and the Basel Traffic Light \citep{basel1996} for zone classification --- and define the violation indicator
\begin{equation}
	V_t = \mathbf{1}\bigl(r_t < -\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\alpha;M}\bigr).
\end{equation}

Tail-loss consistency is assessed through the $Z_2$ statistic \citep{acerbi2014back} computed with the time-averaged ES denominator $Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}$, which avoids division by near-zero daily ES on quiet days (Supplement~S.3). Beyond coverage, the Fissler--Ziegel scoring rule \citep{fissler2016higher} ranks $(\VaR, \ES)$ jointly; full construction is in Supplement~S.1. The conformal magnitude introduced in Section~\ref{sec:layer2} complements these tests by quantifying miscalibration on a continuous, signed scale.

Beyond the per-asset backtests, four panel-level inferential procedures support the empirical audit: a diagnostic regression of the Quantile Score improvement on the conformal threshold, a cross-sectional association between the threshold and asset characteristics, a Diebold--Mariano pairwise forecast comparison with Driscoll--Kraay panel-HAC variance, and a panel-pooled Kupiec test with cluster-robust inference. Specifications are in Supplement~S.3; results enter the empirical sections at first use.

\subsection{Layer~2: Conformal Recalibration}
\label{sec:layer2}

\subsubsection{One-Sided Conformal VaR Correction}
\label{sec:cqr_var}

Conformal prediction provides a model-free framework for post-hoc calibration of predictive distributions \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,romano2019conformalized}. We adapt it to one-sided tail risk: forecast outputs are split chronologically into calibration and test sets with fraction $f_c = 0.70$ (robustness in Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

The nonconformity score $S_t$ from equation~\eqref{eq:score} is positive when the realised loss exceeds the predicted quantile. The conformal shift is the empirical $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the calibration scores,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qV}
	\qVstat
	= Q_{1-\alpha}\!\left(\{S_t\}_{t\in\mathcal D_n}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D_n$ is the calibration sample. The recalibrated lower quantile and VaR are then given by equation~\eqref{eq:var_cp_roll} with $q_V^{\mathrm{stat}} = \qVstat$. A positive $\qVstat$ shifts the predicted quantile downward, increasing reported VaR and correcting underestimation of tail risk; a negative value indicates over-conservatism.

The construction makes no parametric assumption on returns; the coverage guarantee under temporal dependence is developed in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

The one-sided design is dictated by the regulatory loss function, not by a symmetry consideration. Basel III/FRTB capital and the Yellow/Red zone triggers are functions of loss-side exceedances alone (Section~\ref{sec:economic}): a day on which the realised return falls below the reported VaR is counted, a day on which it comfortably exceeds it is not. A correction applied to the upper tail would therefore alter no capital charge and no zone assignment, while widening the reported interval and degrading sharpness. We accordingly shift only $\hat q_t^{lo}$ and leave the remainder of the predictive distribution untouched; two-sided and distributional alternatives are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}. Operational safeguards, including the non-negativity constraint $\qVstat \geq 0$, are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\begin{remark}[Variance scaling under tail sparsity]
	\label{rem:tail_sparsity}
	Tail calibration relies on events $\{r_t \leq \hat q_t^{lo}\}$, whose expected number is $\E[N_\alpha] = \alpha T$. Estimators fitted to this tail subset inherit variance of order $k/N_\alpha$ for $k$ parameters. By contrast, $\qVstat$ is a single order statistic of the full calibration sample. A one-parameter correction therefore enjoys a favourable bias--variance trade-off under tail sparsity, and the correction magnitude is interpretable as a diagnostic precisely because the construction adds no further parameters. Values of $\qVstat$ that dominate the scale of $\hat q_t^{lo}$ signal the high-$\bar R$ range.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Validity under Temporal Dependence}
\label{sec:validity}

Standard conformal coverage guarantees require exchangeability, which volatility clustering and serial dependence in financial returns violate. We therefore state an asymptotic coverage result under weak dependence.\footnote{We work with $\beta$-mixing rather than $\alpha$-mixing because the conformal threshold is an empirical quantile of the score process and the proof uses blocking/coupling arguments controlling total-variation distance between calibration and evaluation blocks.}

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:main}
	Let $S_t = \hat q_t^{lo} - r_t$ denote the nonconformity score process and let $n = \lfloor f_c T \rfloor$ be the calibration sample size.
	\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(A\arabic*)}]
		\item\label{ass:stationary}
		$\{S_t\}$ is strictly stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing: $\beta(k) \le C\rho^k$, $\rho \in (0,1)$.
		\item\label{ass:density}
		The marginal distribution $F_S$ admits a density $f_S$ that is continuous and bounded away from zero near the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile $q_{1-\alpha}$.
		\item\label{ass:weak_dep}
		There exists a gap sequence $g_n \to \infty$, $g_n/n \to 0$, such that the test score $S_{n+g_n+1}$ is evaluated after the calibration sample $\mathcal D_n = (S_1, \ldots, S_n)$, separated by $g_n$ unused observations.
	\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}
	\label{thm:dependent_conformal_var}
	Under Assumption~\ref{ass:main}, the single-split conformal VaR estimator satisfies
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:coverage_bound}
		\mathbb P\!\left(
		r_{n+g_n+1} \ge \hat q_{n+g_n+1}^{lo} - \qVstat
		\right)
		\ge
		1 - \alpha - \Delta_n,
	\end{equation}
	where $\Delta_n \le C\sqrt{(\log n)/n} + \beta(g_n)$ and $C > 0$ depends on the density near $q_{1-\alpha}$ and on the mixing rate.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[What the theorem assumes, and what the protocol does]
	\label{rem:gap_vs_protocol}
	Two gaps between this result and the empirical design are worth stating
	rather than leaving for a reader to find.

	First, the theorem requires a separation $g_n\to\infty$ between the
	calibration block and the evaluation point, and by
	Lemma~\ref{lem:score_mixing} that separation has to exceed the forecaster's
	context length $m$ before the mixing bound bites at all. The protocol used
	throughout the empirical sections is a \emph{contiguous} $70/30$ split:
	$g_n = 0$. The theorem therefore does not apply to the estimator as run. We
	report the gap ablation for exactly this reason (Supplement~S.4): inserting
	a gap moves $\hat\pi$ by at most $0.0005$ over the full sample and $0.0058$
	within the COVID window, so the cost of the discrepancy is measurable and
	small on this panel --- which is evidence, not a proof, and is offered as
	such.

	Second, the finite-sample conformal guarantee is a statement about
	exchangeable scores. Financial return series are not exchangeable, which is
	what this theorem exists to address; its conclusion is asymptotic, with an
	explicit remainder, and no finite-sample coverage claim is made anywhere in
	this paper for the dependent case.
\end{remark}

The bound is conservative at typical financial sample sizes. On six representative model--asset pairs spanning estimated score persistence $\hat\rho \in [0.18, 0.67]$, the estimated remainder $\hat\Delta_n$ lies between 0.109 and 0.138 --- a coverage floor of approximately 88\% against empirical coverage averaging 98.9\%. Across all 312 model--asset pairs, empirical coverage exceeds the corresponding lower bound $1 - \alpha - \hat\Delta_n$: the worst corrected coverage in the panel is $0.968$, against a floor of at most $0.891$. The bound provides a dependence-robust justification for the single-split correction; finite-sample performance is assessed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The remainder simplifies under standard GARCH dynamics.

\begin{corollary}
	\label{cor:garch_conformal_var}
	Let $\{r_t\}$ follow a GARCH(1,1) process satisfying $\E[\log(\alpha_1\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta_1)] < 0$. Then the score process $\{S_t\}$ is strictly stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing with $\beta(k) \le a\rho^k$. Choosing $g_n = c\log n$ with $c > 1/|\log\rho|$, Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} applies with $\Delta_n = \mathcal O(\sqrt{(\log n)/n})$.
\end{corollary}


\subsubsection{Rolling Conformal Calibration}
\label{sec:rolling_method}

Financial time series exhibit time-varying volatility and structural change, motivating a locally adaptive conformal correction. In the rolling implementation, the conformal threshold is recomputed at each time $t$ from a trailing window of size $w = 250$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qV_roll}
	\qVroll
	=
	Q_{1-\alpha}\!\left(\{S_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}\right),
\end{equation}
with the recalibrated lower quantile and VaR given by equation~\eqref{eq:var_cp_roll} substituting $q_V^{\mathrm{roll}}(t) = \qVroll$.

The rolling variant is an operational heuristic, not a construction with finite-sample conformal validity: Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} covers the single-split estimator only, and recomputing the threshold from a trailing window violates the single-split assumption.\footnote{Complementary results from the online learning literature \citep{zinkevich2003online} and the weighted conformal framework \citep{barber2023conformal,angelopoulos2024pid} bound time-average miscoverage and finite-window deviation under drift, but cover related objects --- exact OGD updates and exponentially weighted quantiles, respectively --- not the uniform-weight rolling quantile used here. Propositions~\ref{prop:ogd_regret} and~\ref{prop:rolling_drift} in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} adapt these bounds; the proofs are included for completeness.} Empirical support comes from the simulation study in Section~\ref{sec:simulation} and from the drift diagnostic $\hat\delta_w(t)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:rolling_drift}), which flags windows where local recalibration departs from the global one.

The formal conformal guarantee in Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} applies to the single-split estimator. The rolling estimator should be read as a locally adaptive engineering variant whose validity is assessed empirically and supported by the time-average and drift bounds in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, not as an exact conformal procedure.

We compare against nine baselines: five single-shot post-hoc corrections, two distributional or nonlinear methods (GAMLSS-SST and gradient-boosted quantile regression), and two dedicated VaR models (EVT-POT and filtered historical simulation). \subsection{Data and Models}
\label{sec:data_models}

\subsubsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis covers 24 financial assets across five classes: 11 equity indices, 3 government bond exchange-traded funds (ETFs), 4 commodities, 2 cryptocurrencies, and 4 foreign exchange (FX) pairs. Daily log-returns are computed from adjusted closing prices obtained from Yahoo Finance over January 2000--March 2026. Price series were retrieved on 13 March 2026. Cryptocurrency histories begin in 2014 for Bitcoin and 2017 for Ethereum.

Returns are computed on each asset's native trading calendar without cross-asset alignment, and no further data cleaning is applied: missing days are dropped rather than forward-filled, and asynchronous closes across classes are retained. The evaluation therefore reflects deployment conditions rather than a synchronised research panel.

Each asset's return series is partitioned chronologically: the single-split estimator uses the first 70\% as calibration and the remaining 30\% as the out-of-sample test window ($f_c = 0.70$, Section~\ref{sec:cqr_var}); the rolling estimator recomputes the threshold at each $t$ from a trailing window of size $w = 250$ (Section~\ref{sec:rolling_method}) and is evaluated on the same 30\% test window. The full asset list, per-asset sample sizes, and per-asset test windows are in Appendix~\ref{app:assets}.

\subsubsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We evaluate six TSFM forecasters in strict zero-shot mode (no updating or fine-tuning) against four classical benchmarks estimated per asset. Fine-tuning TSFMs is left to future work.

The TSFM set spans two predictive interfaces. Sample-based forecasters (Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2} with tokenised sequence models; Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2024lagllama} with a LLaMA-inspired architecture and Student-$t$ heads; Moirai~1.1 \citep{woo2024moirai}) return $N = 1{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws from the predictive distribution. Quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 \citep{das2024timesfm}; Moirai~2.0 \citep{liu2025moirai}) return a fixed nine-decile grid, from which a 1\% quantile must be supplied by a tail-completion step. Each Chronos checkpoint enters the panel \emph{twice}: once as the shipped series, sampled under the checkpoint's own default configuration, and once read analytically from the same weights by the estimator of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism:analytic}. The pair is the paper's controlled comparison, and it holds the model fixed while varying only the code that turns its output into a threshold.

The classical benchmarks --- GJR-GARCH \citep{glosten1993relation}, GARCH-Normal \citep{bollerslev1986generalized}, EWMA \citep{riskmetrics1996}, and Historical Simulation --- represent standard volatility and tail-risk models; estimation details are in Appendix~\ref{app:models} and Section~\ref{sec:exp_design}.

\subsubsection{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:exp_design}

\textbf{Two panels, named once.} The paper reports two, and mixing them is the
easiest way to produce a number nobody can reconcile, so they are defined here
and tagged wherever they appear.

\emph{The main panel} is \nMainForecasters{} forecasters $\times$ \nMainAssets{}
assets $=$ \nMainPairs{} pairs at $\alpha = 0.01$. This is Table~\ref{tab:master}.
It includes CAViaR-AS, CAViaR-SAV and GAS-$t$, which are estimated directly from
returns by a separate routine and produce no stored forecast series.

\emph{The sequence panel} is \nSeqForecasters{} forecasters $\times$
\nMainAssets{} assets $=$ \nSeqPairs{} pairs, evaluated at four levels. It is the
subset with stored per-date series, and every analysis that needs one runs on it:
the Christoffersen degeneracy rates by level, the Diebold--Mariano comparisons,
the wild-cluster bootstrap, the panel-pooled coverage tests, the deterioration
counts behind the indication rule, and Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}.

The two agree wherever they overlap --- the sequence panel's static Basel zones
reproduce Table~\ref{tab:master} on all \nSeqPairs{} shared pairs --- and every
figure in the text is generated from the artefacts by
\texttt{scripts/paper\_numbers.py}, which fails the build if a value in the
manuscript drifts from the value in the data.

\textbf{The comparison is deliberately asymmetric, and we state that here rather than as a qualification later.} The classical and dynamic benchmarks are estimated per asset and re-estimated at every step; the foundation models are deployed strictly zero-shot, with no fine-tuning and no per-asset adaptation. These are not equivalent adaptation regimes, and any Quantile Score comparison between the two groups reflects that difference as well as any difference in model quality. We retain the asymmetry because it is the operationally relevant contrast --- a zero-shot model is what a practitioner can deploy without an estimation pipeline per instrument --- but no conclusion in this paper rests on a TSFM-versus-GARCH score ranking. The audit's findings are within-group: the interface contrast is between two releases of the same family under identical deployment, and the recalibration results are computed separately for every forecaster.

All forecasts are one-step-ahead. TSFMs use a fixed context length of 512 observations, the default for all six pretrained checkpoints (Appendix~\ref{app:models}). Classical benchmarks are estimated on rolling windows of 250 observations, following the Basel convention \citep{basel1996}, with parameters re-estimated at each step.

A warm-up period (512 observations for TSFMs, 250 for classical benchmarks) precedes the calibration--test split. For full-history series (2000--2026), the test sample is approximately 1{,}500 observations per asset, yielding roughly 36{,}600 panel observations per model and 366 expected violations at $\alpha = 0.01$.

\section{The Sampling Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

This section establishes, by controlled experiment, that a single documented
default in a checkpoint's packaged inference routine accounts for a
thirty-nine-fold exceedance of a nominal 1\% Value-at-Risk, and then removes the
sampler from the estimation path entirely. The case is developed on Chronos-T5
\citep{ansari2024chronos} because it is the one defect in this paper that lives
in published third-party code and reproduces from a public checkpoint without our
data. Everything in this section is therefore verifiable by a reader who has
neither our forecasts nor our returns.

\subsection{What the default does}
\label{sec:mechanism:default}

Chronos-T5 does not emit a parametric predictive law. It quantises the scaled
context into 4093 value bins and treats forecasting as next-token prediction over
that vocabulary, so its one-step predictive distribution is categorical with 4093
atoms. Predictions are drawn from it by the same sampling machinery used for
text, and that machinery has a $\texttt{top\_k}$ parameter, defaulted to 50:
before a single path is drawn, the support is restricted to the 50 most probable
bins and the remaining 4043 are assigned probability zero.

Two properties of this restriction matter here. It removes 98.8\% of the support,
and it removes it \emph{from the tails inward}, because the discarded bins are by
construction the least probable ones. A quantile read from the surviving support
is therefore a quantile of a different, radically narrower distribution --- while
the centre of that distribution, and hence any point forecast, is barely moved.

\subsection{Design}
\label{sec:mechanism:design}

The experiment varies one thing. For each of two checkpoints
(\texttt{chronos-t5-small}, \texttt{chronos-t5-mini}), four assets of unlike
character (SP500, GOLD, BTC, EURUSD) and 200 evenly spaced dates with a full
512-observation context, we draw 1000 samples at horizon one under eight sampling
configurations: $\texttt{top\_k} \in \{50, 200, 1000, 4094\}$,\footnote{The
vocabulary holds 4096 tokens, of which two are special, leaving 4093 value bins
plus the pad token. Setting $\texttt{top\_k} = 4094$ therefore disables the
truncation with one place to spare; it is not an off-by-one against the 4093 bins
quoted elsewhere in this paper.} then
$\texttt{top\_p} \in \{0.9, 0.99\}$ and temperature $\in \{0.5, 2.0\}$ with
$\texttt{top\_k}$ held at its default. Context, seed and weights are identical
across configurations, so the only thing that differs between cells is the
sampling rule.

Three readings were fixed before the experiment was run. If the truncation is the
mechanism, then (i) predictive dispersion recovers monotonically in
$\texttt{top\_k}$ and reaches its target only at full vocabulary, (ii) dispersion
is unmoved by temperature and by nucleus sampling, and (iii) the number of
distinct values among 1000 draws under the default equals $\texttt{top\_k}$
exactly, on every asset and both checkpoints.

All three readings hold (Table~\ref{tab:dose_response}). Dispersion recovers

The third row of the lower block is the reason the defect is durable. A fourfold
change in temperature moves dispersion by 0.001, and nucleus sampling moves it by

and exposed in every tutorial; an analyst who conducts a careful sensitivity
analysis over them obtains a flat response and correctly reports the model as
robust. The one parameter that matters is the one nobody varies.

\subsection{Reading the distribution instead of sampling it}
\label{sec:mechanism:analytic}

At a one-step horizon the sampler is unnecessary. The predictive law is
categorical and exactly available, so its quantiles can be read in closed form:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item apply the softmax to the logits, giving probabilities over token
	identifiers;
	\item map each identifier to its bin centre, offset by the special-token
	count;
	\item multiply by the tokenizer's own scale, the mean absolute value of the
	context;
	\item sort the support and take the cumulative sum of the probabilities;
	\item read $\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha$ as the smallest value whose cumulative
	probability reaches $\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}

This estimator has no $\texttt{num\_samples}$, no $\texttt{top\_k}$, no
$\texttt{top\_p}$, no temperature and no seed. There is no configuration to get
wrong, and it costs one forward pass.

A reconstruction of a model's support is a claim about that model and is checked
against it before anything is concluded from it. Over 40 dates on SP500 we
compare the analytic quantiles against sampling at full vocabulary with 4000
draws: predictive standard deviations agree to within 0.3\% (1.041 against
1.038 in units of realised volatility), and the violation rates at all four
levels agree to four decimal places. The two routes disagree only where Monte
Carlo error predicts they should. The sampled default, on the same dates, gives
dispersion 0.128 and a 1\% violation rate of 0.3942.

Cost is not a consideration either. Across 24 assets and both checkpoints the
analytic route required one forward pass per date --- 121{,}923 dates per
checkpoint --- and completed in roughly ten minutes per checkpoint on a laptop
GPU, against 1000 sampled paths per date for the sampling route. At horizon one,
reading the distribution is both more accurate than sampling it and cheaper.

\label{sec:mechanism:consequence}

\paragraph{The default produces a series that is not a quantile forecast} Read

0.0175 against a nominal 0.0100 is 1.75 times nominal, and unconditional coverage

--- 1.75, 1.35, 1.15, 1.04 for Chronos-Small and 1.78, 1.33, 1.12, 0.99 for
Chronos-Mini --- so that at $\alpha = 0.10$ the analytic forecasts are
essentially calibrated, passing Kupiec on 20 and 22 of 24 assets. What survives
the correction is a specifically \emph{tail} deficiency, which is a claim about
the model and its quantisation grid rather than about the interface through which
it is read.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Raw Diagnostics and the Ordering by $\bar R$}{Raw Diagnostics and the Ordering by R}}

series. Including the two truncated series the range is a factor of 38, and that
factor is a property of a sampling parameter rather than of any forecaster.

The panel is uniformly under-covered at the 1\% level and nothing in it is well
calibrated there. No forecaster passes unconditional coverage on more than 15 of
24 assets, the best being a per-asset CAViaR fitted the ordinary way
\citep{engle2004caviar}; the six foundation-model series pass on between 0 and 13.
Across the whole table \nMainKupiecRawPasses{} of \nMainPairs{} pairs pass raw, and \nMainKupiecCorPasses{} pass after correction.
A reader looking for a foundation model that is competitive at the extreme tail
will not find one here, and a reader looking for the catastrophe reported in
earlier work on this panel will not find that either.

\subsubsection{What the ratio $R$ measures, and what it does not}
\label{sec:R_meaning}

The ratio
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:replacement_ratio}
	R
	=
	\frac{|\qVstat|}{|\VaR_{\mathrm{raw}}|}
\end{equation}
measures how large the conformal correction is relative to the threshold it
adjusts. Values near zero mean the corrected forecast is the raw forecast; values
above one mean the correction exceeds the raw VaR in magnitude, so the reported
threshold is set by the calibration sample rather than by the model. In this
panel only the two truncated Chronos series exceed one, at $\bar R = 17.3$ and
$23.5$; everything else lies between $0.001$ and $0.36$.

An earlier version of this paper presented $R$ as an audit statistic carrying
information that the standard backtests do not. That claim does not survive its
own test and is withdrawn. Among the well-specified forecasters of this panel,
$R$ is a rank-preserving transform of the raw violation rate: the Spearman
correlation between $\bar R$ and $\hat\pi_{\mathrm{raw}}$ is $\nSpearmanRPi$ across
the \nSpearmanRPiN{} well-specified forecasters of Table~\ref{tab:master}, and a detection rule built on the violation rate
alone reproduces every sensitivity and specificity figure we had attributed to
$R$. This is the sharper form of a referee's objection, and it is correct: $R$ is
a scale-interpretable restatement of a quantity already printed in the adjacent
column, useful for saying \emph{how much} of a forecast survives correction, and
not an independent instrument.

The relation to the decomposition of \citet{gneiting2023regression} is the same
statement in their language. $\qVstat$ estimates the unconditional
miscalibration component uMCB, the part of the miscalibration that a constant
shift can remove, and that is an identity between estimators rather than an
empirical finding. What is empirical is its size: on well-specified series a
constant shift addresses a median of \nuMCBSharePct\% of total miscalibration, so the
majority of what is wrong with these forecasts is conditional and a scalar
correction cannot reach it.

\subsubsection{The quantile-grid interface, and what is left of the claim}
\label{sec:interface_failure}

The working paper reported raw 1\% violation rates of 98.8\% for Moirai~2.0 and
99.0\% for TimesFM~2.5 and read them as evidence that a coarse quantile grid
cannot deliver a backtestable 1\% VaR. Both figures were a sign inversion in our
own generation code, and the corrected series run at $\hat\pi =
\nRawPiMoiraiTwo$ and $\nRawPiTimesFM$ (Table~\ref{tab:audit_trail}).

No interface claim is supportable on the corrected panel, in either direction.
The best raw foundation-model series at the 1\% level is a quantile-grid model,
TimesFM~2.5 at $\nRawPiTimesFM$; the sample-based Moirai~1.1 is at
$\nRawPiMoiraiOne$, the analytic Chronos series at $\nRawPiChronosSmallA$ and
$\nRawPiChronosMiniA$, the quantile-grid Moirai~2.0 at $\nRawPiMoiraiTwo$, and
the sample-based Lag-Llama at $\nRawPiLagLlama$. The two interfaces do not
separate.

What survives is narrower and is a statement about specification rather than
about models. A nine-decile grid whose lowest point is the 10\% quantile does
not contain a 1\% quantile, so a tail-completion step must supply it and becomes
part of the forecaster. That step is consequential: across three assets the same
grids give $\bar R$ between 0.005 and 1.70 depending on whether the closure is
Student-$t$, Gaussian or linear, and on BTC the choice moves both models from
the Basel yellow zone to the red one (Appendix~\ref{app:tail_closure}). Reporting
``TimesFM~2.5 at the 1\% level'' without naming the closure describes an object
that does not exist.\footnote{Every series in this table has been through the
scripted validation gate of Section~\ref{sec:gate} and
Appendix~\ref{app:validation_gate}. Four do not pass it: the two Chronos series
sampled at the checkpoint default, which fail five checks each, and their
analytic counterparts, which fail the coverage band on one asset apiece. The
first two are retained deliberately, as the measured consequence of the default
rather than as forecasters; a blocked series is reported with its diagnosis, not
silently dropped.}

\subsection{Static Recalibration}
\label{sec:recalib}

The corrected columns of Table~\ref{tab:master} show that marginal coverage is restored across the panel: \nMainGreen{} of \nMainPairs{} model--asset pairs reach the Basel Green Zone (\nMainGreenPct\%). What the ordering by $\bar R$ adds is the \emph{amount} of correction required to get there. For thirteen of the sixteen forecasters it is between $0.001$ and $0.18$ --- the corrected forecast is the raw forecast to two or three decimal places --- while the two series sampled at the checkpoint default sit at $17.3$ and $23.5$, where the corrected forecast is an empirical fallback retaining nothing of the base model's tail estimate. Green-zone compliance after correction is therefore uninformative on its own about whether the corrected forecast carries any of the base model's information: the truncated series reach it as comfortably as the rest.

We deliberately do not partition the panel. The distribution of $\bar R$ contains several gaps --- the largest, a factor of 97, separates CAViaR from Moirai~1.1 near the \emph{bottom} of the range, and the next, a factor of 8.9, separates Lag-Llama from Moirai~2.0 --- and no single cutoff is distinguished by the data. Any threshold would inherit that arbitrariness, and the continuous statistic conveys the same information without it.

Conditional coverage is a different matter, and the reason is partly that the test is often unavailable. The Christoffersen independence statistic is undefined whenever no two violations fall on consecutive days, which after correction at $\alpha = 0.01$ is the case for \nMainCCUndefined{} of the \nMainPairs{} pairs of the main panel. On the \nMainCCDefined{} pairs where it is defined, \nMainCCPass{} pass --- \nMainCCPassPct\%. We report the informative subset because counting an undefined test as a pass, as is common, would turn the same evidence into a \nMainCCAsPassPct\% pass rate constructed largely from tables that do not exist. The degeneracy rate is itself a result, and Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} treats it as one: at the 1\% level, on samples of the length regulators use, the standard independence test has no evidence to work with on more than half the panel. Green Zone recovery should therefore be read as restored unconditional coverage and nothing more. The structural reasons why a scalar shift cannot reshape conditional dynamics are in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\subsection{When the Correction Helps, and What It Costs}
\label{sec:when_to_apply}

Aggregate improvements can conceal deteriorations. We therefore report the full
distribution of $\Delta\mathrm{QS} = \mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{raw}} -
\mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{corrected}}$ over model--asset pairs rather than its mean.

At $\alpha = 0.01$ the correction makes 66 of 312 pairs worse under the
single-split estimator and 174 of 312 worse under the rolling estimator. The
deteriorations are not scattered: they concentrate on pairs whose raw forecast
was already well calibrated, where the correction has nothing to remove and adds
the estimation noise of $\qVstat$. Of the 94 pairs whose raw forecast passes
Kupiec on the test set, the single-split correction degrades 58 (mean
$\Delta\mathrm{QS} = -1.32\%$ of $\mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{raw}}$, Wilcoxon
$p = 9.3 \times 10^{-5}$) and the rolling variant degrades 89, with mean
$-7.35\%$ and $p \approx 10^{-16}$. The rolling estimator re-estimates $\qVstat$
on a 250-day window and is correspondingly noisier, which is why its
deterioration rate is roughly double at every level. The pattern holds at
$\alpha \in \{0.025, 0.05, 0.10\}$, where it applies to a larger share of the
panel because more raw forecasts are already acceptable at those levels.

This is a sharper answer than the earlier version of this paper could give. With
two series running at $\hat\pi \approx 0.99$ in the panel, ``deterioration''
was not a meaningful notion for them and their magnitudes dominated every pooled
statistic; removing them makes the effect on the remaining pairs visible rather
than changing it.

Whether a deterioration matters depends on whether it buys anything. Of the 174
pairs the rolling estimator degrades, 72 move from Yellow to Green --- a trade a
risk manager can accept --- but 100 gain no zone change at all, 99 of them
because they were already Green, and 2 move from Green to Yellow, worsening on
both axes. On 59\% of the degradations the correction is therefore not a
trade-off but the wrong intervention.

This suggests a deployment rule that requires no classification: apply the
correction when the raw forecast fails a backtest (Basel zone worse than Green,
or Kupiec rejected), and not otherwise. \emph{Which} backtest matters, and the
distinction is not cosmetic.

Gated on the evaluation window, the rule looks excellent: at $\alpha = 0.01$ it
applies to \nGateRollOracleApplied{} of \nSeqPairs{} pairs, skips
\nGateRollOracleSkipped, avoids \nGateRollOracleAvoided{} of the
\nDegradedRoll{} rolling deteriorations --- \nGateRollOracleAvoidedPct\% of the
damage --- and retains \emph{all} \nGateRollOracleUpgrades{} Basel zone
upgrades. But that rule reads the window it is then scored on. It is an oracle,
and we report it only as an upper bound on what gating can buy.

The deployable rule gates on the calibration window, which is the information
available on the day the decision is taken. It is materially weaker. At
$\alpha = 0.01$ it applies to \nGateRollApplied{} pairs and skips only
\nGateRollSkipped; it avoids \nGateRollAvoided{} deteriorations,
\nGateRollAvoidedPct\% of the damage rather than half; and it costs
\nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of the \nGateRollUpgrades{} zone upgrades, so the claim
that gating is free on the regulatory axis does not survive contact with the
constraint. Under the single split the deployable rule avoids
\nGateStaticAvoided{} of the \nDegradedStatic{} deteriorations and costs
\nGateStaticUpgradesLost{} of \nGateStaticUpgrades{} upgrades.

The honest summary is therefore weaker than the one an in-sample evaluation
would support, and still useful: a calibration-window backtest carries enough
signal to avoid about a quarter of the damage at a cost of about a tenth of the
zone upgrades, and the gap between that and the oracle is a measure of how
poorly the calibration window predicts the test window at $\alpha = 0.01$ ---
which is the tail-sparsity constraint of Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} showing
up in a decision problem rather than in an estimator. The rule does not bind at
$\alpha \geq 0.025$, where a majority of raw forecasts fail their backtest and
the correction is indicated for most pairs. The rejection rate falls with the
level --- \nSeqKupiecRejRawTwoFive\%, \nSeqKupiecRejRawFive\% and
\nSeqKupiecRejRawTen\% at $\alpha = 0.025$, $0.05$ and $0.10$ --- so the rule
does bind at the higher levels, on a shrinking minority of pairs; what is
specific to $\alpha = 0.01$ is the size of the damage it avoids. The decision problem is therefore
specific to the extreme tail --- which is where the identification constraint of
Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} operates.

\subsection{Rolling Recalibration}
\label{sec:rolling_results}

For the rolling implementation, we use the time-varying analogue
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:replacement_ratio_rolling}
	R_t
	=
	\frac{|\qVroll|}{|\widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t|}.
\end{equation}
$R_t$ is monitored as a continuous series rather than reduced to a state. Sustained elevation indicates that the correction, not the base forecast, is setting the reported threshold; transient spikes are local stress episodes. We impose no threshold and no persistence rule, so no reclassification step is required.

In our panel $\bar R$ ranges from $0.001$ to $23.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:master}), but the top of that range is the sampling default rather than a forecaster: excluding the two truncated Chronos series the spread is $0.001$ to $0.36$, and it is continuous rather than bimodal.

Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_qv} shows the rolling 250-day $\qVroll$ for representative models on the S\&P~500.

The largest gains arise for asset classes with time-varying volatility. Commodities are the weakest class under single-split calibration (67.3\% Green, 35 of 52 pairs) and reach 100\% under the rolling variant; bonds go from 89.7\% to 100\%, while crypto and FX are already at 100\% under either. The pattern matches Proposition~\ref{prop:rolling_drift}: when the score distribution drifts (large $\delta_w(t)$), a fixed calibration sample is insufficient, but a trailing window absorbs the drift. Detailed asset-class results are in Supplement~S.6.

\subsection{Multi-Quantile Evaluation and Forecast Comparison}
\label{sec:multiquantile}

The multi-quantile evaluation separates the two Chronos configurations more sharply than the 1\% table does. Corrected violation rates sit close to nominal at every level for every series --- that is what the guarantee delivers --- but the per-asset Kupiec rejection counts do not. Read analytically, Chronos-Small is rejected on 5, 9, 5 and 4 of 24 assets at $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.10\}$; sampled at the checkpoint default, the same weights give 10, 14, 16 and 15. Recalibration at one level cannot repair a predictive distribution that is too narrow at all of them (Supplement~S.6).

At the panel level, EWMA is the only forecaster rejected by the cluster-robust test ($p_{\mathrm{cluster}} = 0.035$, $\hat\pi = 0.0119$). The pooled test treats 36{,}000 observations as independent and rejects deviations that are economically negligible; the cluster-robust version, which allows dependence within an asset, does not.

Asset-class disaggregation shows FX and cryptocurrencies achieve full Green classification under single-split calibration; commodities are weaker (mainly WTI and natural gas), with the gap largely closed under the rolling variant (Section~\ref{sec:rolling_results}, Supplement~S.6).

\subsection{The Moirai within-family comparison, withdrawn}
\label{sec:interface_vs_architecture}

The working paper presented Moirai~1.1 against Moirai~2.0 as a within-family
control isolating the predictive interface. It is withdrawn for two independent
reasons. The measurement was wrong (Table~\ref{tab:audit_trail}): recomputed, the
two releases run at $\nRawPiMoiraiOne$ and $\nRawPiMoiraiTwo$, a gap of 0.24
percentage points. And the design was not a control: Moirai~1.1 is a
masked-encoder model with multi-patch inputs and a mixture output, while
Moirai~2.0 replaces all three with a decoder-only architecture, a single patch
and a quantile loss, trained on a different corpus
\citep{woo2024moirai,liu2025moirai}. Output format, architecture and pretraining
data change together, so even a large gap could not have been attributed to the
interface.

The second reason is the more useful one to carry forward. A within-family label
is not evidence of a controlled comparison, and what differs between two releases
is a primary-source question to settle before the comparison is drawn rather
than after.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}
\label{sec:baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the conformal method with alternative recalibration procedures defined in Section~\ref{sec:alternatives}, over all 312 model--asset pairs. Performance is governed less by model flexibility than by whether the recalibration layer respects the tail-sparsity constraint. Note the raw row: its mean violation rate is $0.078$, which is not a statement about forecasters but about the two series sampled at the checkpoint default, and it was $0.319$ before the corrections of Section~\ref{sec:interface_failure}.

Among single-shot post-hoc procedures, the one-parameter conformal shift achieves the best Quantile Score ($5.14 \times 10^{-4}$) and the highest Green-Zone rate of the single-shot methods (89.1\%). Linear quantile regression on residuals is close behind on score ($5.22 \times 10^{-4}$) at lower coverage (81.4\% Green), consistent with the variance--coverage trade-off predicted by Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity}. ACI, which adapts the miscoverage level sequentially, reaches higher coverage (97.4\%) but with wider intervals, reflecting a trade-off between calibration and efficiency. The rolling conformal variant reaches 99.0\% Green\revised{\footnote{The supplement's head-to-head scores the Basel zone on the final $250$-day window; the figure here uses full-history scoring over all \nSeqPairs{} pairs. The gap between the two is protocol, not estimator.}} without increasing parameter dimensionality. \revised{A like-for-like comparison against Adaptive Conformal Inference in the supplement sharpens this ranking on the operational axes. With the step size $\gamma$ selected by first-half validation---the grid value minimising the gap between held-out online coverage and the nominal rate---ACI attains broadly comparable zone coverage but at a materially higher capital charge, posting a mean $|\VaR|$ of $0.0750$ against $0.0443$ for the rolling estimator (a factor of $1.7$) together with a rougher day-over-day capital path, and no value in the $\gamma$-grid brings its capital below that of the rolling estimator. The rolling estimator therefore dominates ACI at the Basel/capital operating point, while ACI remains attractive when the loss function penalises zone placement over capital and a fully online, distribution-free update is required.}

More flexible alternatives do not consistently improve performance. GBM-based quantile regression achieves only 50.6\% Green, with a Kupiec rejection rate of 59\% (183/312), despite using lagged volatility features; its narrower intervals reflect under-coverage, not efficiency. Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} predicts that gains from additional flexibility are bounded above by the variance cost of fitting at $\alpha = 0.01$. A grid search confirms the prediction (Supplement~S.2.2): the QS-optimal tuning achieves QS $4.38 \times 10^{-4}$ but rejects Kupiec on 7 of 13 base forecasters; a conservative tuning achieves 0 rejections at the cost of higher QS ($4.61 \times 10^{-4}$).

GAMLSS-SST is close to the static conformal shift on coverage (87.5\% vs.\ 89.1\% Green) and marginally worse on score (QS $5.42$ vs.\ $5.14 \times 10^{-4}$), and both remain below the rolling conformal variant. The marginal gain from fitting a dynamic skewed-$t$ density is small relative to the gain from local temporal adaptation.

Purpose-built VaR models also face short-window constraints. Under the 250-day rolling protocol, GARCH-N with conformal correction outperforms EVT-POT and filtered historical simulation, with EVT-POT weakened by GPD estimation failures and frequent fallback to EWMA volatility (Supplement~S.2).

The evidence supports Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity}: in this empirical design, rolling one-parameter recalibration delivers the dominant coverage improvement, while additional distributional flexibility yields limited gains at $\alpha = 0.01$.

Five robustness checks, reported in full in Supplement~S.4, confirm that the audit findings are stable: (i)~calibration-fraction sensitivity is limited over $f_c \in [0.50, 0.80]$; (ii)~rolling-window length variation ($w \in \{125, 250, 500\}$) preserves the ordering by $\bar R$; (iii)~the tail-completion rule, by contrast, is \emph{not} innocuous: it moves $\bar R$ by a factor of 3 to 76 within a single model--asset pair and can change the Basel zone (Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended}), which is why the rule is reported as part of the forecaster; (iv)~the theoretical gap $g_n$ between calibration and evaluation has negligible empirical impact (Table~S.6 of the supplement); and (v)~stationary block-bootstrap confidence intervals for $\qVstat$ separate the two regimes cleanly, ruling out sampling variability as the source of the regime gap.

\section{What the Standard Diagnostics Cannot Diagnose}
\label{sec:blindspot}

Four defective series passed every backtest reported in the earlier version of
this paper. This section asks why. The answer is not that the tests were applied
carelessly, and it is not that they are blind to miscalibration --- Kupiec's test
rejects most of this panel, defective series included. It is narrower and more
awkward: a coverage test reports \emph{that} a series miscovers, never
\emph{why}. It cannot separate a model that is genuinely poor in the tail from
one whose predictive distribution was truncated by a sampling default, whose
quantile was stored with the wrong sign, or whose returns were misaligned. All
four present as the same rejection. Add that at $\alpha = 0.01$ these tests have
their least power exactly where the regulatory framework places them, and that
the correction applied downstream of them removes the evidence they would need,
and a defect can pass through a pipeline untouched.

\subsection{The independence test is frequently undefined}
\label{sec:blindspot_cc}

The independence component of \citet{christoffersen1998} conditions on the
transition counts of the exceedance indicator. When no two violations fall on
consecutive days the table is degenerate: $\hat\pi_{11}$ is estimated at the
boundary of the parameter space, the likelihood ratio can be defined by
continuity but its $\chi^2_1$ calibration is not valid there, and the test has
essentially no power against the alternative it is meant to detect. Standard
implementations differ in what they do about this --- ours returns a missing
value, others return a $p$-value computed from a statistic whose asymptotics do
not hold. We call the test undefined in that state, and the substantive point
does not depend on the word: in a degenerate table the test carries no evidence
either way. At $\alpha = 0.01$ this is not a corner case: across our 312
model--asset pairs the test is undefined on 34.6\% of the raw series, and on
53.5\% of the same pairs after recalibration. The share falls monotonically with
the nominal level --- 11.5\%, 1.0\% and 0.0\% raw at $\alpha = 0.025$, $0.05$ and
$0.10$ --- so this is a property of the tail probability, not of our panel.

Two things follow. First, recalibration makes the problem worse rather than
better, because a correction that restores coverage also spaces violations out,
and the tests are usually run on the corrected series. Second, and more
seriously, a degenerate table is an absence of evidence, but the natural
implementation returns ``no rejection'', which reads as a pass. A test with no power on half of its
applications, reported as passing on all of them, is not evidence of
independence. Where the test \emph{is} defined it
rejects on 27.5\% of raw pairs and 48.3\% of recalibrated ones, so the
non-degenerate cases are not reassuring either.

\subsection{Unconditional coverage rejects almost everything}
\label{sec:blindspot_kupiec}

The test of \citet{kupiec1995} is always defined, and at $\alpha = 0.01$ it
rejects 69.9\% of the 312 pairs, against 39.4\% at $\alpha = 0.10$. No forecaster
in the panel passes on more than \nMainBestKupiec{} of \nMainAssets{} assets at the 1\% level. A test that
rejects the majority of defensible models carries little information about any
one of them, and --- decisively for the question of this paper --- it does not
separate a series with a traced defect from one that is merely imperfect.

We measured that separation rather than asserting it. Treating the five series
with traced defects as positives and the eight without as negatives, Kupiec's
majority-fail rule attains sensitivity 5/5 at specificity 2/8, a Fisher exact
$p$ of $0.49$: it flags 11 of 13 forecasters, which is not discrimination. The
independence test attains 0/5. A severe cut on the violation rate --- rejecting
$\hat\pi > 0.10$ or $\hat\pi < 0.005$ --- attains 5/5 at 8/8, $p = 0.00078$. The
finding is about a \emph{threshold}, not about a statistic: what separates defects
from ordinary miscalibration is severity, and Kupiec's threshold is calibrated to
sample size instead. All six Kupiec flags on forecasters with no traced defect
were followed to a verdict, as pre-registered; none is a defect, so the 2/8
figure stands as measured rather than as an artefact of unexamined positives.

\subsection{Recalibration conceals what it corrects}
\label{sec:blindspot_conformal}

Split-conformal recalibration targets marginal coverage regardless of the
forecaster it is applied to --- exactly in finite samples when the scores are
exchangeable \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution}, and asymptotically
under the dependence assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var},
which are the ones this panel satisfies. Either way the guarantee is indifferent
to the forecaster, and in this panel it delivers:
335 of 384 pairs reach the Basel green zone after correction. Applied to the
Chronos series truncated at $\texttt{top\_k} = 50$ --- a series whose predictive
dispersion is 0.12 of realised volatility and whose exceedance rate is nearly
invariant to the nominal level --- it produces $\hat\pi = 0.0108$ and places 19 of
24 assets in the green zone. Applied to the sign-inverted series it did the same.

Every coverage-based backtest downstream of that step therefore passes, and this
is not a failure of the method: a correction that guarantees coverage cannot
diagnose the forecaster it corrects, precisely because the guarantee holds
whatever the forecaster is. The practical implication is that a pipeline which
validates \emph{after} recalibration has, by construction, no test of the
forecaster at all.

\subsection{The uncomfortable retrospective}
\label{sec:blindspot_retro}

The instruments were not silent. In the submitted version of this paper, Table~1
partitioned the panel on $R > 1$ into ``signal-preserving'' and ``effective
replacement'' regimes. The replacement panel contained exactly four series ---
Chronos-Small at $R = 17.3$, Chronos-Mini at $23.5$, TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0
at $3.2$ --- and those four are exactly the four defective foundation-model
series. The fifth defect appears in the same submission: in the cross-sectional
table, GJR-GARCH's correlation between $\qVstat$ and annualised volatility is
$-0.786$, the only negative value against $+0.67$ to $+0.97$ for every other
model, and the manuscript explained it as conditional variance dynamics absorbing
volatility into raw VaR.

Every defect family was flagged, in printed tables, before anyone knew a defect
existed --- and every flag was read as a property of the model. This disposes of
the obvious objection that the detection result above is circular, since the
flags predate the discovery. It also states the real lesson more sharply than any
sensitivity figure can: the failure was one of interpretation, not of
instrumentation. A large correction was reported as a finding about a forecaster
when it should have been read as a question about a pipeline.

\section{Structural Validation}
\label{sec:gate}

If coverage cannot detect these failures, validation must not be based on
coverage. We therefore check a forecast series against properties it must have
\emph{to be a Value-at-Risk series at all}, before it is calibrated, evaluated or
compared with anything.

Ten such checks are stated in Appendix~\ref{app:validation_gate} and implemented
in \texttt{scripts/promotion\_gate.py}: a lower quantile whose median is not
negative, quantiles that are not ordered across $\alpha$, an exceedance rate that
does not respond to the nominal level, a predictive dispersion far from realised
volatility, a support of a handful of distinct values.

They are not all the same kind of thing, and we distinguish them rather than
present ten items of equal standing. Two are invariants that follow from what a
quantile forecast is and carry no tunable constant; one is an invariant tested
through a proxy with a threshold; the remaining seven are plausibility bands
whose cutoffs we chose and state. Eight of the ten need nothing but the forecast
series and the returns. The other two --- $\alpha$-responsiveness and coverage
plausibility --- need an evaluation window, so the gate is not entirely upstream
of the data a backtest consumes. What separates them from a backtest is the
question they ask: not whether a coverage rate is statistically distinguishable
from nominal, but whether the series responds to the level it was asked for at
all. No $p$-value is computed, no benchmark is required, and none of the ten
needs access to the hardware that produced the forecasts.

\paragraph{Scoping is declared, not assumed} Two checks are inapplicable to
order-statistic estimators, which may legitimately tie across adjacent $\alpha$
and hold constant for long stretches as a window slides; a discrete predictive
support may tie for the same reason. The gate records the estimator class of each
series in a manifest and declares the check inapplicable rather than returning a
verdict on it. A check that cannot fail informatively should say so.

\paragraph{Result on this panel} The gate blocks \nGateBlocked{} of the \nGateSeries{} promoted series.
Two are the Chronos series sampled at the checkpoint default, which fail
monotonicity, scale, $\alpha$-response, coverage and dispersion on all 24 assets.
Two are their analytic counterparts, which fail only the coverage-plausibility
band, and only on one asset each --- the residual tail deficiency of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism:consequence}, correctly recorded as a block requiring
a written diagnosis rather than silently admitted.

\paragraph{What this evidence is worth} These checks were written after the
defects were known, several of them because a specific defect had already got
past whatever checks existed at the time. Their performance on those defects is
in-sample by construction and is not evidence of their general sensitivity, and
we do not present it as such. What can be claimed is narrower and still useful:
each check is a property a practitioner would endorse before seeing any data,
each is arithmetic rather than inferential, and each addresses a failure mode
that the coverage-based tests of Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} provably cannot
address. The truncated series fail five of the ten checks; the analytic series
fail none of the structural ones.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:failure}

As documented in Section~\ref{sec:recalib}, a scalar shift restores unconditional violation frequency but cannot reshape the conditional dynamics of the violation process; this is a structural limitation, not a tuning issue. The rolling variant captures time variation in miscalibration magnitude but remains a location adjustment, not a fully conditional recalibration rule. More flexible state-dependent recalibration, such as one-sided extensions of Adaptive Conformal Inference \citep{gibbs2021adaptive} or the PID framework of \citet{angelopoulos2024pid}, lies beyond the present framework.

A related limitation concerns identification. The positive cross-sectional association between $\qVstat$ and asset-level volatility or tail frequency (Supplement~S.5.1) is consistent with pre-training domain mismatch, but the zero-shot design does not permit causal attribution. The audit identifies \emph{where} tail signal is missing, not \emph{why}.

\subsection{Operational Failure Modes}
\label{sec:operational_failure}

Operational failure modes can be mitigated. Small calibration sets ($n_{\mathrm{cal}} < 200$) produce unstable corrections; a minimum calibration size or cross-asset aggregation is advisable. Very high score persistence ($\hat\rho > 0.95$) weakens the $\beta$-mixing bound, and GARCH pre-filtering can reduce dependence. Over-conservative base forecasts ($\qVstat < 0$) may lose coverage under non-stationarity; imposing $\qVstat \geq 0$ provides a guard for systemically over-conservative models. Structural breaks can cause temporary under-protection when the calibration window predates the break; shortening $w$ or incorporating stressed subsamples increases adaptivity at the cost of higher variance.

Figure~\ref{fig:covid_response} illustrates the structural-break case during the COVID-19 shock. The trailing-window design produces a measurable lag: seven of the thirteen forecasters reach their post-shock $\qVroll$ maximum within the acute window, four peak 77 calendar days later, and the two GJR-GARCH variants take 161 days. The lag is a property of the window rather than of the forecaster class --- Historical Simulation and EWMA are among the fastest, GARCH-N among the slowest. The drift diagnostic $\hat{\delta}_w(t)$ flags this regime in real time (Supplement~S.4, Figure~S.1 of the supplement), allowing operational deployment to detect the lag before it propagates.

\subsection{Economic Impact}
\label{sec:economic}

The capital arithmetic below is illustrative under the Basel~II/pre-FRTB internal-models multiplier framework. Under FRTB the binding capital measure is ES at 97.5\%; the VaR-based traffic-light arithmetic here describes the backtesting channel, which remains relevant for model validation but is not the primary capital channel.

Traffic Light zone classifications determine the capital multiplier $k \in [3, 4]$ applied to internal-model VaR. Let $W = \widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{corr}}/\widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{base}}$ denote the relative widening of the VaR threshold after correction. The capital-charge ratio between a corrected Green-zone forecast and an uncorrected Yellow-zone forecast is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:capital_ratio}
	\frac{\mathrm{Capital}_{\mathrm{Green,corr}}}{\mathrm{Capital}_{\mathrm{Yellow,base}}}
	=
	\frac{k_{\mathrm{Green}}}{k_{\mathrm{Yellow}}}\, W.
\end{equation}
A capital saving occurs when this ratio is below one. Using $k_{\mathrm{Green}} = 3.00$ and $k_{\mathrm{Yellow}} \approx 3.65$, the break-even width is $k_{\mathrm{Yellow}}/k_{\mathrm{Green}} \approx 1.22$: a Yellow-to-Green transition remains capital-saving as long as the corrected VaR threshold widens by less than about 22\%.

Lag-Llama on S\&P~500 has $W = 1.05$, so corrected Green-zone capital is $3.00 \times 1.05 = 3.15$ against an uncorrected Yellow-zone capital of $3.65$ --- a 13.7\% per-day reduction. Cumulated over the 1{,}824-day test window (2018--2026), the saving rises to 17.8\% (Figure~\ref{fig:capital_charge}).

On the corrected panel no forecaster is anywhere near the break-even widening. Relative to GJR-GARCH the corrected thresholds run from $W = 0.98$ for the analytic Chronos series to $W = 1.14$ for Lag-Llama, with TimesFM~2.5 at $\nWidthRatioTimesFM$ and Moirai~2.0 at $\nWidthRatioMoiraiTwo$. The capital arithmetic therefore favours correction across the panel, which is a weaker and duller statement than the one this paragraph used to make: the earlier $W \approx 1.6$--$1.75$ for the two quantile-grid models was the sign defect, whose inverted thresholds the correction had to replace outright.

\subsection{Practitioner Guidance}
\label{sec:practitioner}

The audit translates into three deployment rules.

\paragraph*{Per-asset VaR with adequate history}
When sufficient history is available, GJR-GARCH-$t$ with conformal correction is the preferred configuration: it delivers the strongest per-asset Quantile Score in the panel ($4.67 \times 10^{-4}$ corrected, against $4.67$--$5.83$ across the table) and the highest Basel green count under the single split (Section~\ref{sec:multiquantile}) and requires no pre-training infrastructure. For users requiring an explicit conditional density, GAMLSS-SST is a viable alternative (88.0\% Green, Table~\ref{tab:baselines}), albeit with higher estimation burden.

\paragraph*{Large-universe deployment}
When per-asset estimation is infeasible because of scale, limited history, or latency constraints, a TSFM combined with one-parameter conformal correction offers a scalable coverage-oriented alternative. The diagnostic magnitude $|\qVstat|$ should be monitored as a quality indicator: assets with persistently large corrections should be flagged for exclusion, fallback, or replacement.

\paragraph*{Model screening}
The rolling ratio $R_t = |\qVroll|/|\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}}|$ (equation~\eqref{eq:replacement_ratio_rolling}) should be monitored continuously, alongside the Basel zone and the Quantile Score, with no classification step. Read together the three answer different questions: the zone says whether the reported threshold is compliant, the Quantile Score says what sharpness it costs, and $R$ says how much of the reported threshold is the correction rather than the model. A forecaster can be compliant and uninformative --- Chronos-Mini reaches the Green Zone on 19 of 24 assets with $\bar R = 23.5$ --- and the zone alone cannot distinguish that case from CAViaR, which is compliant at $\bar R = 0.001$.

The sign of $\qVstat$ carries the diagnosis and should not be discarded. ($R$
itself cannot: equations~\eqref{eq:replacement_ratio} and
\eqref{eq:replacement_ratio_rolling} define it through absolute values, so it is
non-negative by construction and the signed quantity has to be reported beside
it.) A negative $\qVstat$ indicates an over-conservative base that the correction tightens, releasing capital. On the corrected panel that case is rare and no longer belongs to any one forecaster: $\qVstat < 0$ on three assets for TimesFM~2.5, two for Moirai~2.0, one each for Moirai~1.1 and GJR-GARCH-$t$, and none elsewhere. GJR-GARCH itself is now on the other side, at $\qVstat > 0$ on all \nMainAssets{} assets --- it was the systematically over-conservative forecaster of earlier drafts only because its quantile map inflated every threshold by 45\%.

Deployment advice follows from Section~\ref{sec:when_to_apply} rather than from a label: apply the correction when the raw forecast fails a backtest, and not otherwise. At $\alpha = 0.01$, keyed on the calibration window, that rule skips \nGateRollSkipped{} of \nSeqPairs{} pairs, avoids \nGateRollAvoided{} of the \nDegradedRoll{} degradations the rolling estimator would otherwise cause, and costs \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of \nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades. It is not free on the regulatory axis, and a version of it that appears to be free is one that has been evaluated on the window it used to decide. It does not bind at $\alpha \geq 0.025$, where almost every raw forecast already fails, so the decision problem is specific to the extreme tail.

Before any of that, validate the series structurally (Section~\ref{sec:gate}). The advice is ordered deliberately: a recalibration decision taken on a series that has not been checked is a decision about an artefact, and the correction will make it look correct.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}

A Value-at-Risk forecast is produced by code, and the step in which a predictive
distribution becomes a threshold is where the pipelines we examined failed. In
this panel that step, not the architecture of the model or the format in which it
exposes its distribution, is what separates a forecaster that is roughly usable
at the 1\% level from one that violates on 40\% of days. The clearest instance is
a documented sampling default in a public checkpoint --- correct for the task it
was written for, silently catastrophic for this one --- and the mechanism is
established by controlled experiment rather than inferred: dispersion recovers
monotonically as the truncation is relaxed, one thousand draws under the default
contain exactly fifty distinct values, and the parameters a careful analyst would
vary move nothing.

The finding that matters beyond this panel is that the instruments in standard
use cannot see such failures. At $\alpha = 0.01$ the independence test is
undefined on a third of raw pairs and on more than half of recalibrated ones,
while unconditional coverage rejects seven pairs in ten and so cannot separate a
defect from ordinary miscalibration. Post-hoc recalibration compounds the
problem, because a guarantee that holds regardless of the forecaster cannot
diagnose the forecaster: applied to a truncated series it restored
$\hat\pi = 0.0108$ and 19 of 24 Basel green zones, and every backtest downstream
of it passed. Validation that runs after a correction is not validation of the
model.

We therefore propose checking forecast series structurally, against properties
they must have to be Value-at-Risk series at all, before they are calibrated or
evaluated. Ten such checks are cheap, require no benchmark or test window, and
block 4 of the 13 series promoted here. We state plainly that they were written
after the defects were known and that their performance on those defects is
in-sample; the argument for them is not their measured sensitivity but that the
alternative provably cannot do the job.

On recalibration itself, our claims narrow rather than change. The conformal
shift restores marginal coverage under $\beta$-mixing dependence, and that is
all it does. It is an intervention with an indication: under the rolling
estimator, \nWellCalRollWorse{} of the \nWellCalRollN{} pairs that pass Kupiec on
the test window are degraded by correcting them, and gating the correction on a
calibration-window backtest avoids \nGateRollAvoidedPct\% of that damage at the
cost of \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of \nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades ---
a quarter of the benefit that the same rule shows when it is allowed to peek at
the evaluation window, which is how such rules are usually reported. It
reaches only part of the problem --- in the decomposition of
\citet{gneiting2023regression} a constant shift addresses a median of \nuMCBSharePct\% of
total miscalibration on well-specified series, the remainder being conditional
--- and it does not restore violation independence. The magnitude $R$ that an
earlier version of this paper offered as an audit statistic is withdrawn as such:
among well-specified forecasters it is a rank-preserving transform of the
violation rate, and we retain it only as a scale-interpretable statement of how
much of a forecast survives correction.

What the corrected panel says about foundation models is deflationary and, we
think, more useful than what we previously reported. Configured correctly, the
zero-shot series here are neither the catastrophe of our earlier draft nor
competitive: they sit alongside GARCH-family benchmarks at the 1\% level, a
per-asset CAViaR fitted the ordinary way outperforms all of them, and the
residual miscalibration of the best of them is concentrated in the tail and
largely gone by $\alpha = 0.10$. Whether fine-tuning closes that tail gap remains
open. What is no longer open, in our view, is whether a tail-risk evaluation can
be trusted without a structural check of the series it evaluates.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}
%============================================================

This appendix collects supporting lemmas and the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var}, stated in
Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

\subsection{Supporting Lemmas}

\begin{lemma}[Inheritance of weak dependence by the score process]
	\label{lem:score_mixing}
	Let $\{r_t\}$ be strictly stationary and $\beta$-mixing with coefficients
	$\beta_r(k)$. Suppose the forecast $\hat q_t^{lo}$ is
	$\mathcal F_{t-1}$-measurable and generated by a time-invariant measurable
	functional $g$ of a \emph{finite context} of length $m$,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_forecast_functional}
		\hat q_t^{lo}=g(r_{t-1},\ldots,r_{t-m}).
	\end{equation}
	Then the score process $S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ is strictly stationary
	and $\beta$-mixing with coefficients satisfying
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_score_mixing_bound}
		\beta_S(k)\le \beta_r(k-m),
		\qquad k> m.
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{remark}[Why the context must be finite, and the earlier statement was wrong]
	\label{rem:finite_context}
	An earlier version of this lemma allowed $\hat q_t^{lo}=g(r_{t-1},r_{t-2},
	\ldots)$, an infinite context, and concluded $\beta_S(k)\le\beta_r(k-1)$.
	That is false as stated. The proof requires
	$\sigma(S_s : s\ge t+k)\subseteq\sigma(r_u : u\ge t+k-1)$, and with an
	infinite context $S_s$ depends on returns arbitrarily far before $t+k-1$,
	so the inclusion fails and no separation of the two $\sigma$-fields is
	obtained. Restricting $g$ to a window of length $m$ repairs it and costs
	nothing here: every forecaster in this paper uses a finite context by
	construction --- $m = 512$ for the foundation models, $m = 250$ for the
	GARCH-family, EWMA and historical-simulation benchmarks
	(Section~\ref{sec:exp_design}). Recursive filters such as EWMA are the one
	case needing care; the implementation used here truncates the recursion at
	the same 250-observation window, which is what makes $m$ finite for it too.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
	By assumption, $\{r_t\}$ is strictly stationary. Since $\hat q_t^{lo}$ is
	a time-invariant measurable functional of the past, the joint process
	$(\hat q_t^{lo},r_t)$ is also strictly stationary. Therefore,
	$S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ inherits strict stationarity.
	
	Let $\mathcal S_{-\infty}^t=\sigma(S_s:s\le t)$ and
	$\mathcal S_{t+k}^{\infty}=\sigma(S_s:s\ge t+k)$. By
	\eqref{eq:app_forecast_functional}, $S_t$ is a measurable function of
	$(r_t,r_{t-1},\ldots,r_{t-m})$, so
	\[
	\mathcal S_{-\infty}^t
	\subseteq
	\mathcal R_{-\infty}^t:=\sigma(r_s:s\le t),
	\]
	and, crucially, the forward field reaches back only $m$ steps:
	\[
	\mathcal S_{t+k}^{\infty}
	\subseteq
	\mathcal R_{t+k-m}^{\infty}:=\sigma(r_s:s\ge t+k-m).
	\]
	It is this second inclusion that fails without a finite context
	(Remark~\ref{rem:finite_context}).
	By monotonicity of $\beta$-mixing coefficients under measurable
	transformations,
	\[
	\beta_S(k)
	\le
	\beta(\mathcal R_{-\infty}^t,\mathcal R_{t+k-m}^{\infty})
	=
	\beta_r(k-m),
	\qquad k>m,
	\]
	which is \eqref{eq:app_score_mixing_bound}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Empirical quantile deviation under $\beta$-mixing]
	\label{lem:quantile_mixing}
	Under Assumption~\ref{ass:main}, let
	\[
	\qVstat=\hat F_n^{-1}(1-\alpha),
	\qquad
	q_{1-\alpha}=F_S^{-1}(1-\alpha).
	\]
	Then
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_quantile_rate}
		|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
		=
		\mathcal O_p\!\left(
		\frac{1}{f_S(q_{1-\alpha})}
		\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
		\right).
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Under geometric $\beta$-mixing as in Assumption~\ref{ass:main}\ref{ass:stationary},
	the empirical CDF satisfies the uniform concentration bound
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_ecdf_bound}
		\sup_{s\in\mathbb R}|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|
		=
		\mathcal O_p\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right);
	\end{equation}
	see \citet[Theorem 2.1]{yu1994rates} and
	\citet[Theorem 7.2]{rio2017asymptotic}.
	 Since $\qVstat$ is the
	empirical $(1-\alpha)$-quantile,
	\[
	\hat F_n(\qVstat)=1-\alpha+\mathcal O(1/n).
	\]
	Thus,
	\[
	|F_S(\qVstat)-(1-\alpha)|
	\le
	\sup_s|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|+\mathcal O(1/n).
	\]
	By the density condition in Assumption~\ref{ass:main},
	$f_S(q_{1-\alpha})$ is bounded away from zero in a neighbourhood of
	$q_{1-\alpha}$. A first-order expansion around $q_{1-\alpha}$ gives
	\[
	|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
	\le
	\frac{1}{f_S(q_{1-\alpha})}
	\left[
	\sup_s|\hat F_n(s)-F_S(s)|+\mathcal O(1/n)
	\right],
	\]
	which yields the stated rate.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var}}

\begin{proof}
	The proof proceeds in three steps.
	
	\textit{Step 1: Reduction to score control.}
	By definition of the nonconformity score
	$S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:proof_score_event}
		S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat
		\Longleftrightarrow
		r_{n+g_n+1}\ge \hat q_{n+g_n+1}^{lo}-\qVstat.
	\end{equation}
	It therefore suffices to show
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:proof_target_coverage}
		\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\Delta_n.
	\end{equation}
	
\textit{Step 2: Quantile approximation.}
Let $q_{1-\alpha}=F_S^{-1}(1-\alpha)$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:quantile_mixing},
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_qv_rate}
	|\qVstat-q_{1-\alpha}|
	=
	\mathcal O_p\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
\end{equation}
By the density condition,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_fs_qv_bound}
	F_S(\qVstat)
	\ge
	1-\alpha
	-
	C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
\end{equation}
for a finite constant $C>0$.

\textit{Step 3: Gap-separated dependence control.}
By $\beta$-mixing, conditional on the calibration sample
$\mathcal D_n=(S_1,\ldots,S_n)$,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_conditional_gap}
	\bigl|
	\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat\mid\mathcal D_n)
	-
	F_S(\qVstat)
	\bigr|
	\le
	\beta(g_n).
\end{equation}
Taking expectations over $\mathcal D_n$ and combining with
\eqref{eq:proof_fs_qv_bound} gives
\[
\mathbb P(S_{n+g_n+1}\le\qVstat)
\ge
\mathbb E\!\left[F_S(\qVstat)\right]-\beta(g_n)
\ge
1-\alpha
-
C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}
-\beta(g_n).
\]
Setting
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:proof_delta_n}
	\Delta_n
	=
	C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\beta(g_n)
\end{equation}
completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:garch_conformal_var}}

\begin{proof}
	Under $\omega>0$, $\alpha_1,\beta_1\ge0$, and
	$\mathbb E[\log(\alpha_1\varepsilon_t^2+\beta_1)]<0$, the GARCH(1,1)
	process admits a unique strictly stationary solution
	\citep[Theorem~2.10]{francq2019garch}. Under standard regularity
	conditions, it is geometrically $\beta$-mixing
	\citep{francq2019garch,lindner2009stationarity}:
	\[
	\beta_r(k)\le a\rho^k,
	\qquad \rho\in(0,1).
	\]
	By Lemma~\ref{lem:score_mixing}, the score process is also strictly
	stationary and geometrically $\beta$-mixing. The density condition holds
	under the assumed continuous innovation density near the target quantile.
	
	Choosing $g_n=c\log n$ with $c>1/|\log\rho|$ gives
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_garch_gap_rate}
		\beta(g_n)\le a\rho^{c\log n}
		=
		\mathcal O(n^{-c|\log\rho|})
		=
		o\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
	\end{equation}
	Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} therefore applies with
	\[
	\Delta_n
	=
	\mathcal O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).
	\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Online Quantile Tracking: an Exact Telescoping Bound}
\label{app:ogd_regret}

The result below concerns the online quantile update, and its scope relative to
the estimator actually used in the empirical sections is stated after the proof.
It is deterministic: it holds for every score sequence, with no probabilistic
assumption whatsoever.

\begin{proposition}[Time-average coverage of the online quantile update]
	\label{prop:ogd_regret}
	Let $q_1 \in [-R, R]$ and let $q_t$ evolve according to
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:ogd_update}
		q_{t+1}=q_t+\eta\!\left[(1-\alpha)-\mathbf 1\{s_t\le q_t\}\right]
		= q_t + \eta\!\left[\mathbf 1\{s_t> q_t\}-\alpha\right],
	\end{equation}
	with step size $\eta>0$ and scores satisfying $|s_t|\le R$ for all $t$. Then
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:ogd_bound}
		\left|\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha\right|
		\;\le\;
		\frac{2R+\eta}{\eta T},
	\end{equation}
	and in particular, for $\eta=R/\sqrt T$,
	\[
	\left|\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha\right|
	\le \frac{2+T^{-1/2}}{\sqrt T}\le \frac{3}{\sqrt T}.
	\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ogd_regret}]
	\emph{Step 1: the iterates stay bounded, without projection.} We claim
	$q_t\in[-R-\eta\alpha,\;R+\eta(1-\alpha)]$ for every $t\ge1$. The claim
	holds at $t=1$ by assumption. Suppose it holds at $t$. For the upper end,
	either $q_t\le R$, in which case
	$q_{t+1}\le q_t+\eta(1-\alpha)\le R+\eta(1-\alpha)$; or $q_t>R\ge s_t$, in
	which case $\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}=0$, the update is $-\eta\alpha<0$, and
	$q_{t+1}<q_t$. For the lower end, either $q_t\ge -R$, so
	$q_{t+1}\ge q_t-\eta\alpha\ge -R-\eta\alpha$; or $q_t<-R\le s_t$, so
	$\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}=1$, the update is $+\eta(1-\alpha)>0$, and
	$q_{t+1}>q_t$. In both directions the iterate is pulled back as soon as it
	leaves $[-R,R]$, so no projection step is needed and the range has width at
	most $2R+\eta$.

	\emph{Step 2: telescoping.} Summing the second form of
	\eqref{eq:ogd_update} over $t=1,\ldots,T$,
	\[
	\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha\right]
	=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(q_{t+1}-q_t\right)
	=q_{T+1}-q_1 .
	\]
	Dividing by $\eta T$ and applying Step 1,
	\[
	\left|\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf 1\{s_t>q_t\}-\alpha\right|
	=\frac{|q_{T+1}-q_1|}{\eta T}
	\le\frac{2R+\eta}{\eta T},
	\]
	which is \eqref{eq:ogd_bound}. Substituting $\eta=R/\sqrt T$ gives
	$\eta T=R\sqrt T$ and the stated rate.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What this replaces, and what it does not deliver]
	\label{rem:ogd_scope}
	An earlier version of this proposition was proved by invoking the
	online-convex-optimisation regret bound of \citet{zinkevich2003online} and
	then converting average regret on the pinball loss into average
	miscoverage. That conversion needs a local Lipschitz argument in the
	indicator parametrisation and does not follow from the regret bound alone.
	The proof above needs none of it: the update telescopes exactly, and the
	only work is showing the iterates cannot escape a bounded interval. The
	constant is explicit and the statement is pathwise.

	Three limitations should be read alongside it. It controls the
	\emph{time-average} of the coverage indicator, not conditional coverage and
	not coverage over any subwindow: a sequence can satisfy
	\eqref{eq:ogd_bound} while violating badly in one regime and
	over-covering in another, which is exactly the behaviour
	Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} warns about. It requires bounded scores, which
	rules out nothing empirically but is an assumption. And, most importantly
	for this paper, it is a statement about the \emph{online update}
	\eqref{eq:ogd_update}, whereas the rolling estimator used throughout the
	empirical sections is a trailing-window empirical quantile, which is not
	literal online gradient descent. In stationary regimes the two target the
	same population quantile; under drift the window trades bias for variance
	near transitions. We therefore cite this bound for what it is --- a
	dependence-free guarantee for one estimator in the family --- and not as
	cover for the estimator we run.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Non-Exchangeable Coverage Bound with Distributional Drift}
\label{app:rolling_drift}

The next statement is a \emph{supporting bound}, not a result we ask the reader
to lean on. Its conclusion holds under the total-variation drift measure defined
below, but that measure is not estimable from a single path without further
assumptions, and the constant it produces is too loose to be informative at
$\alpha = 0.01$. We keep it because it makes precise \emph{which} quantity has to
be small for a trailing window to work, and we use $\hat\delta_w(t)$ operationally
as a monitoring diagnostic rather than as a certified bound.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-window coverage under drift; heuristic]
	\label{prop:rolling_drift}
	Let
	\[
	\qVroll=Q_{1-\alpha}\!\left(\{s_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}\right)
	\]
	and define
	\[
	\delta_w(t)=\max_{\tau\in[t-w,t-1]}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)).
	\]
	Under local stationarity conditions analogous to
	Assumption~\ref{ass:main},
	\[
	\P(r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll)\ge 1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w,
	\]
	where $\Delta_w\le C\sqrt{(\log w)/w}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:rolling_drift}]
	Let $S_t=\hat q_t^{lo}-r_t$ and
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_qvt}
		\qVroll
		=
		Q_{1-\alpha}\left(\{S_\tau\}_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}\right).
	\end{equation}
	The coverage event satisfies
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_score_event}
		r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll
		\Longleftrightarrow
		S_t\le \qVroll.
	\end{equation}
	
	Following the weighted conformal framework of
	\citet{barber2023conformal}, the coverage deviation under
	non-exchangeability is bounded by the average total variation distance
	between calibration-window score distributions and the test distribution:
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_tv_bound}
		\left|
		\mathbb P(S_t\le\qVroll)-(1-\alpha)
		\right|
		\le
		\frac{1}{w}
		\sum_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}
		d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)\right).
	\end{equation}
	By definition,
	\[
	\frac{1}{w}
	\sum_{\tau=t-w}^{t-1}
	d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal L(S_\tau),\mathcal L(S_t)\right)
	\le
	\delta_w(t).
	\]
	The empirical quantile estimated from a dependent window of size $w$
	adds the finite-sample remainder
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_delta_w}
		\Delta_w\le C\sqrt{\frac{\log w}{w}}.
	\end{equation}
	Combining the drift and estimation terms yields
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_score_bound}
		\mathbb P(S_t\le\qVroll)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w.
	\end{equation}
	Returning to the score representation gives
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:app_rolling_bound}
		\mathbb P\!\left(
		r_t\ge \hat q_t^{lo}-\qVroll
		\right)
		\ge
		1-\alpha-\delta_w(t)-\Delta_w.
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}

The bound separates distributional drift, captured by $\delta_w(t)$, from
finite-sample estimation error under dependence, captured by $\Delta_w$.
In the empirical implementation, $\delta_w(t)$ is proxied by the empirical
total variation distance between the first and second halves of the rolling
window. Using $\sqrt w$ histogram bins, define
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:app_delta_hat}
	\hat\delta_w(t)
	=
	\frac{1}{2}\sum_b |\hat p_{1,b}-\hat p_{2,b}|,
\end{equation}
where $\hat p_{1,b}$ and $\hat p_{2,b}$ are empirical bin frequencies over
the first and second halves of the window. When $\hat\delta_w(t)$ exceeds a
practitioner-chosen threshold, the bound weakens and a shorter window or
temporary deployment pause may be warranted.

\section{Tail Completion Determines the Tail}
\label{app:tail_closure}

TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0 do not report a 1\% quantile. Their output is a
nine-decile grid whose lowest point is the 10\% quantile
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}), so the threshold that is backtested comes from an
external completion step. This appendix asks how much that step matters, and the
answer is: more than the model does.

Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended} compares the Student-$t$ closure used
throughout the paper against a Gaussian closure and a linear extrapolation from
the two lowest deciles, on three assets. The same grids, on the same days, give
$\bar R$ ranging from $0.005$ to $1.70$. Within a single model--asset pair the
closure rule moves $\bar R$ by a factor of 3.3 (Moirai~2.0 on BTC) to 76
(Moirai~2.0 on NATGAS), and the raw violation rate with it: TimesFM~2.5 on SP500
runs at $\hat\pi = 0.019$ under the Student-$t$ closure and $0.075$ under linear
extrapolation. On BTC the choice moves both models out of the Basel yellow zone
into the red one after correction.

The practical consequence is the one the body of the paper draws in a different
setting. A quantile-grid forecaster is not a forecaster until a completion rule
is specified, and the rule is part of the forecaster in the same sense that
$\texttt{top\_k}$ is part of a sampled one. Reporting ``TimesFM~2.5 at the 1\%
level'' without naming the closure describes an object that does not exist.

\paragraph{The inner-seven-deciles check is vacuous, and we now say so} Earlier
versions presented a refit on $u \in \{0.2, \ldots, 0.8\}$ --- dropping the
outermost deciles that anchor the tail --- as the more demanding test, and
reported that it recovered $\qVstat$ and the ordering exactly. It does: to six
decimal places, on all six model--asset combinations
(Table~\ref{tab:tail_closure_extended}). That agreement is an artefact of the
construction. The deciles are not native model output; they are reconstructed
from the stored Student-$t$ parameters $(\nu, \mu, \sigma)$, so fitting a
Student-$t$ to any subset of them recovers those parameters exactly and the
check cannot fail. It is uninformative about the model, and it is reported here
only because it was previously reported as evidence.

The Gaussian and linear closures are informative precisely because they impose a
different functional form, and they are the ones that move the answer.

\section{Series Validation Gate}
\label{app:validation_gate}

\paragraph{A note on sign, before anything else} Equation~\eqref{eq:var_raw}
defines $\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}$, a \emph{positive} threshold.
The forecast files, and therefore the gate, store and check the \emph{lower
quantile} $\hat q^{lo}_\alpha$, which is negative. The two conventions differ by
a sign, the checks below are written in the second, and the stored column is
nonetheless named \texttt{VaR\_}$\alpha$.

That naming is not a detail we are excusing; it is the mechanism by which two of
the five defects in this paper survived. A column named for a risk measure while
holding a quantile invites exactly one error, and that error was made twice, in
two different generators, by two different people. The gate checks the sign
explicitly because the name does not.

\bigskip

Every forecast series used in this paper is run through a scripted validation
gate before it is admitted to the analysis. The gate runs in continuous
integration, emits the table below, and blocks promotion of any series that
fails; a blocked series receives a written diagnosis and no tolerance is widened
to accommodate it. The implementation is \texttt{scripts/promotion\_gate.py}.

\paragraph{What kind of thing each check is} Two of the ten are invariants: they
follow from what a quantile forecast is, and have no tunable constant. One is an
invariant tested by a proxy with a threshold. The remaining seven are
\emph{plausibility bands} --- statements about what a Value-at-Risk series for
daily financial returns should look like, with cutoffs we chose and state rather
than derive. We separate them because the distinction is the difference between
a check that cannot be argued with and a check that can, and a reader deciding
whether to adopt this gate is entitled to know which is which. The bands are
deliberately wide: every one of them admits a range within which a forecaster may
be poor without being defective.

\paragraph{Two of them are not free of a backtest, and we say so}
$\alpha$-responsiveness and coverage plausibility both need realised returns over
an evaluation window, so the gate is not entirely upstream of the data a backtest
uses. What separates them from a backtest is not the input but the question. A
backtest asks whether a coverage rate is statistically distinguishable from
nominal, calibrates its threshold to sample size, and at $\alpha = 0.01$ rejects
most of a defensible panel. These two ask whether the series responds to the
level it was asked for at all, and whether its exceedance rate is within a factor
of five of nominal --- questions a defective series fails by orders of magnitude
and an ordinary one passes comfortably. The truncated Chronos series fail
$\alpha$-responsiveness with a ratio of 1.08 against a threshold of 3, and
coverage with $\hat\pi/\alpha = 39$ against a bound of 5. No plausible tightening
of a Kupiec test produces that separation, because Kupiec is not asking that
question.

\paragraph{Scoping is declared, not inferred} Support cardinality and tail reach
apply only to sample-based forecasters, and monotonicity is weak for
order-statistic estimators. Historical Simulation returns an order statistic of a
250-observation window: it takes few distinct values by construction and may tie
across adjacent $\alpha$. That is a property of the estimator, not a defect, and
the gate declares the check inapplicable from the estimator class recorded in the
series manifest rather than returning a verdict it cannot support.

Two further invariants are enforced over the data directories rather than over
individual series: every file present must be named in the series manifest, and
every name in the manifest must exist on disk. Both directions are required. The
manifest records, for each series, the model, asset, path, version and estimator
class.
