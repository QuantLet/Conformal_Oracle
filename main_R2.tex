\begin{abstract}

	A Value-at-Risk backtest reports \emph{that} a forecast series miscovers, and the Basel traffic light reports less than that. We show that the classification is asymptotically degenerate: as the evaluation window grows, the probability of a green verdict tends to one or to zero according to whether the long-run violation rate falls below or above the zone boundary $\tau = 4/250$, so the verdict identifies the sign of $\pi - \tau$ and nothing else. At $\alpha = 0.01$ the boundary is \nTLTauOverAlpha{} times nominal, and a forecaster whose reported threshold cuts off up to that much of the conditional mass is passed with probability tending to one --- a Value-at-Risk understated by \nTLUndTFive\% under standardised Student-$t_5$ innovations. The interval does not shrink with evidence. In a Monte Carlo over \nMCDgps{} data-generating processes and sample sizes to $T = \nMCTMax$, a series miscovering by half is classified green \nMCGreenTFiveSmall\% of the time at $T = \nMCTMin$ and \nMCGreenTFiveLarge\% at $T = \nMCTMax$: more data makes the classification more confident, not more informative. A diagnostic that identifies only a sign cannot say which forecasts need correcting.

	Recalibration answers the question of how to correct a series, and leaves open the question of which series to correct. A one-sided split-conformal shift restores marginal coverage exactly in finite samples under exchangeability, and asymptotically under geometric $\beta$-mixing with an explicit remainder $\Delta_n \le C\sqrt{(\log n)/n} + \beta(g_n)$; the separation condition the theorem requires is one the contiguous split does not meet, and the gap is reported rather than assumed away. On \nMainAssets{} assets and \nMainForecasters{} forecasters the correction moves \nMainKupiecRawPasses{} unconditional-coverage passes to \nMainKupiecCorPasses{} and \nMainGreen{} of \nMainPairs{} pairs into the green zone --- and it does so whichever forecaster it is applied to, which is why the corrected column cannot rank anything.

	Recalibration is therefore an intervention, and the question it poses is one of indication rather than of average effect. It is close to free where it is not needed and harmful where the base forecast is already calibrated: under the rolling estimator \nWellCalRollWorse{} of the \nWellCalRollN{} pairs that pass Kupiec are degraded by correcting them. Gating on a failed raw backtest helps, and inherits the limitation above: keyed on the calibration window, the only information available at the decision date, it avoids \nGateRollAvoided{} of \nDegradedRoll{} deteriorations (\nGateRollAvoidedPct\%) against \nGateRollOracleAvoidedPct\% for the same rule keyed on the evaluation window, which is an oracle bound and not a deployable rule. Its advertised cost is \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of \nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades, of which \nGateRollUpgradesLostWorse{} are pairs the correction degrades on the quantile score: the zone counts them as benefit because it reads a threshold crossing, which is the same limitation the first paragraph states, arriving in the ledger.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A Value-at-Risk forecast is not a model output. It is the result of reducing a
model output --- a set of sampled paths, a grid of predictive quantiles, a fitted
parametric law, an order statistic over a window --- to a single number, and that
reduction is performed by code that the forecasting literature does not treat as
an object of study. In the \nMainAssets-asset,
\nMainForecasters-forecaster Value-at-Risk panel studied here, the configuration of that
reduction step --- rather than the architecture of the model, its pretraining
corpus, or the format in which it exposes its predictive distribution --- is what
separates a series that is roughly usable at the 1\% level from one that violates
on four days in ten.

The difficulty this creates is one of identification, not of measurement. A
coverage backtest examines a single scalar property of a realised series: the
frequency of exceedances, and in its conditional form their clustering. When it
rejects, the rejection is equally consistent with a genuinely poor tail model and
with an implementation failure anywhere in the reduction step or in the data
assembly that feeds it. These are different objects calling for different
remedies, and the test returns the same verdict for all of them. No procedure
applied downstream of the reduction step recovers the distinction, because the
information that would identify it has been discarded by then.

A systematic audit of the forecast-production pipeline behind this panel isolates
five such failure modes, each located in a specific step and each established by
a diagnostic that is arithmetic rather than inferential. They are set out once,
in Table~S.31, and not re-enumerated thereafter. Their
measured effect on reported 1\% violation rates spans factors between five and seventy, and it runs in both directions: one of them inflates every threshold by
roughly 45\%, producing a \nRawPiGJRDefectivePct\% violation rate against a nominal 1\% and a Basel
green zone on all \nMainAssets{} assets. A reduction rule that reports fewer
exceedances than nominal attracts no scrutiny from a system built to count them.

\paragraph{A controlled case} One of these failure modes can be demonstrated
without reference to this study's data at all, and it is the one on which we
develop the argument. Chronos-T5 \citep{ansari2024chronos} represents its
one-step predictive distribution as a categorical law over 4093 quantised value
bins. Its packaged sampling routine defaults to $\texttt{top\_k} = 50$, a value
inherited from text generation, which restricts that support to its 50 most
probable atoms before any quantile is computed --- and does so from the tails
inward, since the discarded bins are by construction the least probable. The
median is barely affected, so a point-forecast evaluation of this configuration
is entirely unremarkable. The 1\% tail is not: the empirical violation rate
reaches \nPiSmallDefaultOne{} and \nPiMiniDefaultOne{} for the two checkpoint
sizes examined here, an exceedance that would place every asset in the Basel red
zone. This is not a fault in Chronos. It is a documented default, correct for the
task it was designed for and silently wrong for this one, and it reproduces from
a public checkpoint and published code by any reader who runs it.

Three properties make such failures durable rather than merely possible. First,
they are invisible to point-forecast evaluation, which is where forecasting
models are normally validated. Second, they are invisible to a responsible
sensitivity analysis: varying temperature across a fourfold range moves predictive dispersion by \nLitTempGap{} and nucleus sampling by \nLitNucleusGap, so an analyst who
sweeps the parameters that are documented and discussed obtains a flat response
and correctly reports the model as robust. Third --- and this is the part that
concerns the tail-risk literature specifically --- they are invisible to the
backtests that regulate this domain.

That third claim was measured. At $\alpha = 0.01$ the
independence test of \citet{christoffersen1998} is \emph{degenerate} on
\nSeqCCUndefRawOne\% of the \nSeqPairs{} model--asset pairs with stored series,
and on \nSeqCCUndefCorOne\% of them once the forecasts have been recalibrated.
The mechanism is narrower than a sparse tail. The transition table is populated
in every one of those cases --- \nSeqCCBothZeroRaw{} of them have no exceedance
transitions at all --- and what is empty is a single cell of it: the
\nSeqCCNoConsecRaw{} degenerate pairs raw, and \nSeqCCNoConsecCor{} corrected,
are exactly those with no two exceedances on consecutive days, so $n_{11} = 0$
while $n_{10} > 0$. A correction that spaces exceedances out empties that cell
more often, which is why the share rises rather than falls. In that
state the transition probability is estimated at the boundary of the parameter
space and the $\chi^2_1$ calibration of the likelihood ratio does not hold; the
share falls monotonically to \nSeqCCUndefRawTen\% at $\alpha = 0.10$ under both
readings. A degenerate table is an absence of evidence, but the natural
implementation returns ``no rejection'', which reads as a pass. Unconditional
coverage \citep{kupiec1995} is always defined and rejects \nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\%
of pairs at $\alpha = 0.01$ against \nSeqKupiecRejRawTen\% at $\alpha = 0.10$;
when a test rejects the majority of defensible models, its rejection of any one
of them carries little information, and it does not separate a series carrying an
implementation failure from one that is merely imperfect. Both instruments are
weakest exactly at the level at which the Basel traffic-light system operates
\citep{basel1996}.

Post-hoc recalibration does not resolve the situation; it conceals it. A
split-conformal shift restores marginal coverage regardless of the forecaster it
is applied to --- exactly and in finite samples under exchangeability
\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution}, and asymptotically under the
$\beta$-mixing conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var}, which
require a separation between calibration and evaluation that the contiguous split
used here does not provide, as Section~\ref{sec:theory_gap} sets out. Neither
guarantee therefore covers the estimator exactly as it is run; the distinction
matters for the theory and not for the point of this paragraph, since on these
data the correction delivers coverage either way. Applied to a series whose predictive
standard deviation is \nDispDefaultSmall{} of realised volatility, it returns
$\hat\pi = \nTruncCorPi$ and places \nTruncCorGreen{} of \nMainAssets{} assets in
the Basel green zone. A pipeline that validates after recalibration has,
by construction, no test of the forecaster left to run.

\paragraph{Contributions} First, we establish the mechanism of the sampling
failure by controlled experiment rather than inference, and remove it. Holding
contexts, seeds and weights fixed and varying only the sampling configuration,
predictive dispersion recovers monotonically as the truncation is relaxed, and
one thousand draws under the default contain exactly 50 distinct values on every
asset and both checkpoints. We then show that sampling is unnecessary at a
one-step horizon, where the predictive law is categorical and its quantiles are
available in closed form, and give an estimator that reads them directly from the
model's logits: it agrees with full-vocabulary sampling to within Monte Carlo
error, has no $\texttt{num\_samples}$, no $\texttt{top\_k}$ and no seed, and
costs one forward pass. Under it the violation rate falls from
\nPiSmallDefaultOne{} and \nPiMiniDefaultOne{} to \nPiSmallAnalyticOne{} and
\nPiMiniAnalyticOne.

Second, we quantify the blindness of the standard tail diagnostics, as above,
and separate two claims that are usually merged: that these tests are being
misapplied, and that they are being applied where they have the least power. It
is the second. This is the argument for validating a forecast series
structurally, before it is calibrated or evaluated.

Third, a validation gate --- ten checks on a forecast series, of
which two are invariants that follow from what a quantile forecast is, one is an
invariant tested through a proxy, and seven are plausibility bands with
stated rather than derived thresholds. Seven need nothing but the series and the
returns; one needs the model's sampled paths and two need an evaluation window,
so the gate is not entirely free of one. Two
checks are inapplicable to order-statistic estimators and record that rather than
returning a verdict, and all ten were specified after the failure modes had been
identified, so their performance on those failure modes is in-sample by
construction. The gate blocks \nGateBlocked{} of \nGateSeries{} series in this
panel.

Fourth, the recalibration layer carries an asymptotic coverage guarantee for
the single-split estimator under $\beta$-mixing dependence with explicit
gap-separation conditions, together with finite-window bounds for the rolling
variant under distributional drift, and we report the intervention's
\emph{indication} rather than its average effect. Recalibration is close to free
where it is not needed and harmful where the base forecast is already calibrated:
under the rolling estimator, \nWellCalRollWorse{} of the \nWellCalRollN{} pairs
that pass Kupiec on the test window are degraded by correcting them --- a
description of who is harmed, not yet a rule for deciding. Gating the correction
on a failed raw backtest helps, but far less than an in-sample evaluation of that
rule suggests: keyed on the calibration window, which is the only information
available at the decision date, it avoids \nGateRollAvoided{} of the
\nDegradedRoll{} deteriorations at $\alpha = 0.01$ ---
\nGateRollAvoidedPct\% --- and costs \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of
\nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades. Keyed on the evaluation window it would
avoid \nGateRollOracleAvoidedPct\% and cost none, which is an oracle bound rather
than a deployable rule.

\paragraph{What this paper does not claim} It does not claim that foundation
models are unsuitable for tail-risk forecasting, nor that they are ready for it.
Correctly configured, the six zero-shot foundation-model series in this panel are
neither catastrophically miscalibrated nor superior: their raw 1\% violation
rates run from \nRawPiTimesFM{} to \nRawPiLagLlama, which places them alongside
the GARCH-family benchmarks on the same assets and windows, and a per-asset
CAViaR \citep{engle2004caviar} fitted the ordinary way outperforms all of them.
It does not claim that implementation failure explains tail miscalibration in
general: five failure modes are identified in one pipeline, and their prevalence
in the literature is not identified by this study. And it does not claim that the
ten structural checks are sufficient, or that more than three of them are
necessary conditions in the strict sense; the other seven are plausibility bands.
What can be said for all of them is that they are cheap, that each states a
property a practitioner would endorse before seeing any data, and that the
alternative --- coverage --- provably cannot do this job.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}

This paper relates to three strands: tail miscalibration in probabilistic forecasters, regulatory backtesting of tail-risk forecasts, and post-hoc calibration under temporal dependence.

Tail miscalibration is well-documented across forecaster classes. Parametric volatility models can systematically under- or over-estimate extreme quantiles under heavy-tailed innovations or regime shifts \citep{christoffersen1998,mcneil2000estimation,nolde2017elicitability}. Recent time series foundation models (TSFMs) --- including Chronos \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}, TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, Moirai \citep{woo2024moirai,liu2025moirai}, and Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2024lagllama} --- achieve competitive zero-shot forecasting performance but exhibit a similar pattern: \citet{goel2024tsfmvar} show that pre-trained quantiles require fine-tuning for acceptable Value-at-Risk (VaR) coverage, and \citet{pele2025llmvar} identify tail compression in instruction-tuned models, with similar miscalibration documented for volatility forecasting \citep{goel2025volatility,rahimikia2025revisiting}. These findings, spanning parametric and foundation-model regimes alike, motivate post-hoc calibration but do not address the identification constraint imposed by extreme-tail sparsity, and they stop short of a taxonomy of when post-hoc correction adjusts an informative base versus when it acts as an empirical fallback.

Regulatory backtesting procedures \citep{kupiec1995,christoffersen1998,basel1996,acerbi2014back,fissler2016higher,nolde2017elicitability} detect violations of nominal coverage, exceedance dependence, and tail-loss inconsistency, but do not prescribe forecast corrections. Their limits at a $1\%$ level are established. \citet{escanciano2012pitfalls} prove the unconditional coverage test \emph{inconsistent} against Historical Simulation and Filtered Historical Simulation, the mechanism being that those estimators are unconditionally calibrated by construction, and their empirical application finds the regulatory multiplication factors set at the $3.00$ floor for portfolios their own consistent test rejects --- a capital multiplier at its minimum on a model a backtest has rejected, reported in 2012.

A second line treats the poverty of the single-level exceedance indicator as a premise to design around rather than a result to state. \citet{gordy2020spectral} weight exceedance events by a function of the modelled probability level, reading the probability integral transform rather than the binary indicator, and say what that requires: the regulator must observe more than the exception indicator at one level. \citet{kratz2018multinomial} make the same move with a different instrument, taking exceptions at four or more levels and showing the multinomial test more powerful than the binomial one at a single level. Both establish what is available to a supervisor who holds the predictive distribution.

\citet{cont2010robustness} supply the framing this paper's first layer rests on. They define a risk measurement procedure as the pair of an estimation step and a risk measure, and show that the same risk measure exhibits different sensitivities depending on the estimation procedure used, so that the procedure is part of the measured object rather than an implementation detail beneath it. The extension here is narrow and is stated as such: in a sample-based forecaster the estimation step is a \emph{documented software default} rather than a modelling choice, it is not reported, and it is invisible to every diagnostic the domain runs.

Post-hoc calibration adjusts the output of a fixed forecaster without retraining \citep{gneiting2013combining,taillardat2016calibrated}. These procedures sit within the broader probabilistic-forecasting literature on calibration--sharpness trade-offs \citep{gneiting2007probabilistic,gneiting2014probabilistic}, where calibration restoration is balanced against sharpness loss. Model-based alternatives, such as CAViaR \citep{engle2004caviar} and conditional-density frameworks such as the Generalised Additive Model for Location, Scale and Shape \citep[GAMLSS;][]{rigby2005generalized}, estimate tail behaviour directly but impose structure on the conditional distribution. Conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic}, applied to quantile regression by \citet{romano2019conformalized} as conformalized quantile regression (CQR), provides a model-free alternative. Standard guarantees rely on exchangeability, which is violated in financial time series by volatility clustering, serial dependence, and structural change; extensions to dependent or non-exchangeable data include weighted conformal methods \citep{barber2023conformal}, blocking schemes \citep{chernozhukov2018exact}, and adaptive procedures \citep{zaffran2022conformal,xu2024conformal,angelopoulos2024pid}.

The TSFM literature receives closer attention above because this class is recent and has not yet been the subject of a systematic 1\% tail audit; the parametric volatility literature is mature and enters this paper through standard benchmarks rather than fresh review.

The object this paper proves is identified has already appeared in that
literature, as a weight. \citet{escanciano2012pitfalls}'s Lemma~1 derives the
optimal weight for a weighted backtest,
$w^\star(I_{t-1}) = F_{I_{t-1}}(m_\alpha(I_{t-1},\theta_0))$ --- the conditional
distribution function of the return evaluated at the reported quantile --- which
is exactly the realised tail probability $u_t$ of equation~\eqref{eq:u_process} below.
The same functional carries opposite uses in the two papers: they weight by it to
\emph{recover} power inside the exceedance class, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:identification} shows that it \emph{exhausts} what the
exceedance path carries. Their result and this one hold against different
alternative sets --- theirs agree with the null on $\E[u_t]$, these on the whole
process $\{u_t\}$ --- so a consistent weighted backtest and a non-identification
result coexist, and the second says where the class ends rather than that the
first is wrong.

What differs is where the paper looks. \citet{goel2024tsfmvar} and
\citet{goel2025volatility} reduce TSFM tail miscalibration by fine-tuning;
\citet{gibbs2021adaptive} deliver adaptive coverage under nonstationarity by
online updates of the miscoverage rate; \citet{tamingtailrisk2026} extend
conformal risk control to nonstationary portfolio VaR through regime-similarity
weighting. Each treats the forecast series as given and improves what is done to
it. The question here is whether the series is what it is claimed to be, which
turns on the code converting a predictive distribution into a threshold and which
no procedure downstream of that conversion can answer. It is also a question
posed on a poorer information set than the spectral and multinomial backtests
require: a consumer of a third-party quantile series holds the thresholds and the
realised returns, and cannot obtain a probability integral transform from a
forecaster that never published a distribution.

A reporting gap follows directly. For a sample-based forecaster the generation configuration is part of the estimator, in the same sense that a bandwidth is part of a kernel estimator: it determines the object whose quantile is reported. It is not, at present, part of what such studies report. In the recent tail-risk and volatility applications of sample-based foundation models surveyed here, none of \texttt{num\_samples}, \texttt{top\_k}, \texttt{top\_p}, temperature or seed appears in the text \citep[e.g.][]{rahimikia2025revisiting}. The prevalence of misconfiguration does not follow from that and this design does not identify it; what follows is narrower and sufficient here, that the convention which would make a configuration of this kind visible to a reader does not exist.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:framework}

The audit has two layers: a base model $M$ producing one-step-ahead probabilistic forecasts (Section~\ref{sec:layer1}), and a one-parameter conformal shift that recalibrates the predicted lower quantile (Section~\ref{sec:layer2}). Two implementations of the second layer are developed in Section~\ref{sec:layer2}: a single-split estimator, which admits asymptotic coverage under $\beta$-mixing dependence, and a rolling variant.

\paragraph*{Notation}
Let $r_t$ denote the daily log return and $\hat q_t^{lo} = (\widehat F_t^M)^{-1}(\alpha)$ the predicted lower $\alpha$-quantile from base model~$M$; we fix $\alpha = 0.01$. VaR is reported as a positive loss threshold:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_raw}
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}.
\end{equation}
The nonconformity score is
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:score}
	S_t = \hat q_t^{lo} - r_t,
\end{equation}
and the conformal shift is denoted $q_V$, with single-split and rolling variants
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_cp_roll}
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{cp}} = \VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} + q_V^{\mathrm{stat}},
	\qquad
	\VaR_t^{\mathrm{roll}} = \VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} + q_V^{\mathrm{roll}}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer~1: Probabilistic Forecasting}
\label{sec:layer1}

\subsubsection{Base Model Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The first layer produces one-step-ahead probabilistic forecasts of returns from a fixed base model~$M$.

For sample-based forecasters (Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini, Moirai~1.1, and Lag-Llama), the forecast at time~$t$ consists of $N = 1{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws $\{\hat r_t^{i;M}\}_{i=1}^N$, yielding the empirical predictive CDF
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:ecdf_sample}
	\widehat F_t^M(x)
	= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\mathbf{1}\{\hat r_t^{i;M}\leq x\}.
\end{equation}
The raw VaR forecast is the empirical lower $\alpha$-quantile, which is
equation~\eqref{eq:var_raw} evaluated at $\widehat F_t^M$; we write
$\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}}$ throughout and do not introduce a second symbol for it.

For quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 and Moirai~2.0), the predictive object is a finite grid of conditional quantiles $\{\hat q_t(u_j)\}_{j=1}^{J}$, with $J = 9$ deciles. Since the audit operates at the 1\% lower-tail level, we obtain $\hat q_t(0.01)$ by fitting a three-parameter Student-$t$ distribution to the native predicted quantiles:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:tail_completion}
	(\hat\nu_t, \hat\mu_t, \hat\sigma_t)
	=
	\arg\min_{\nu, \mu, \sigma}
	\sum_{j=1}^{J}
	\left[
	\hat q_t(u_j)
	-
	\left\{
	\mu + \sigma\, t_{\nu}^{-1}(u_j)
	\right\}
	\right]^2,
	\qquad \nu > 2,\ \sigma > 0,
\end{equation}
and set
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:var_grid}
	\hat q_t(0.01)
	=
	\hat\mu_t + \hat\sigma_t\, t_{\hat\nu_t}^{-1}(0.01).
\end{equation}
The Student-$t$ closure is a parsimonious heavy-tailed default in financial VaR modelling \citep{bollerslev1987,hansen1994autoregressive,mcneil2000estimation}. It is not, however, an innocuous choice: Supplement~S.10 shows that the closure rule governs the tail behaviour of a grid model far more than the model does, so this decision is reported as part of the forecaster rather than as an implementation detail.

For classical benchmarks (GJR-GARCH, GARCH-Normal, EWMA, Historical Simulation), VaR is computed from the model-implied conditional distribution or, for Historical Simulation, the empirical 250-day rolling distribution. EWMA is the exception to the rolling-window convention and is stated as such wherever it appears: its conditional variance is the RiskMetrics recursion at $\lambda = 0.94$ \citep{riskmetrics1996}, which is not truncated at 250 observations (Supplement~S.8, Table~S.19).

Expected Shortfall is reported separately at $\alpha = 0.025$ following the Basel III/FRTB convention \citep{bcbs2019frtb} and is computed from the same predictive object as VaR. Because ES is not elicitable as a standalone functional \citep{gneiting2011making}, the conformal construction is developed for VaR only; ES computation details and the heuristic ES shift are in Supplement~S.1.

\subsubsection{Diagnosing Miscalibration}
\label{sec:diag}

We apply the standard regulatory backtests --- Kupiec~\citep{kupiec1995} for unconditional coverage, Christoffersen~\citep{christoffersen1998} for joint coverage and serial independence, the dynamic quantile regression of \citet{engle2004caviar}, which tests whether the centred exceedance indicator is orthogonal to its own lags and to the reported quantile, and the Basel Traffic Light \citep{basel1996} for zone classification --- and define the violation indicator
\begin{equation}
	V_t = \mathbf{1}\bigl(r_t < -\widehat{\VaR}_t^{\alpha;M}\bigr).
\end{equation}

Tail-loss consistency is assessed through the $Z_2$ statistic \citep{acerbi2014back}, computed with the time-averaged ES denominator $Z_2^{\mathrm{mod}}$, which avoids division by near-zero daily ES on quiet days (Supplement~S.3). The statistic requires ES to enter as a positive magnitude, a sign convention that the stored lower-tail column does not satisfy and that Supplement~S.1 states explicitly. Beyond coverage, the Fissler--Ziegel scoring rule \citep{fissler2016higher} ranks $(\VaR, \ES)$ jointly; full construction is in Supplement~S.1. The conformal shift $\qVstat$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:layer2} complements these tests by quantifying miscalibration on a continuous, signed scale; the ratio $\bar R$ built from it in Section~\ref{sec:R_meaning} is non-negative by construction and carries magnitude only.

Beyond the per-asset backtests, four panel-level inferential procedures support the empirical audit: a diagnostic regression of the Quantile Score improvement on the conformal threshold, a cross-sectional association between the threshold and asset characteristics, a Diebold--Mariano pairwise forecast comparison with Driscoll--Kraay panel-HAC variance, and a panel-pooled Kupiec test with cluster-robust inference. Specifications are in Supplement~S.3; results enter the empirical sections at first use.

\subsection{Layer~2: Conformal Recalibration}
\label{sec:layer2}

\subsubsection{One-Sided Conformal VaR Correction}
\label{sec:cqr_var}

Conformal prediction provides a model-free framework for post-hoc calibration of predictive distributions \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,romano2019conformalized}. We adapt it to one-sided tail risk: forecast outputs are split chronologically into calibration and test sets with fraction $f_c = 0.70$ (robustness in Supplement~S.4).

The nonconformity score $S_t$ from equation~\eqref{eq:score} is positive when the realised loss exceeds the predicted quantile. The conformal shift is the finite-sample split-conformal quantile of the calibration scores: writing $S_{(1)} \leq \cdots \leq S_{(n)}$ for the order statistics of $\{S_t\}_{t\in\mathcal D_n}$ over the calibration sample $\mathcal D_n$ of size $n$,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qV}
	\qVstat
	= S_{(k)},
	\qquad
	k = \left\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
\end{equation}
with $\qVstat = S_{(n)}$ when $k > n$, which occurs only for $\alpha < 1/(n+1)$. This is the conformal convention rather than the plain empirical quantile $Q_{1-\alpha}(\{S_t\})$, and the distinction is not cosmetic: the two differ by one order statistic, an $O(1/n)$ gap that can nonetheless change the sign of $\qVstat$ on assets whose calibration scores straddle zero near the $(1-\alpha)$ level. Every result in this paper that enters a headline table uses equation~\eqref{eq:qV}, and the rolling variant substitutes the trailing window of size $w$ for $\mathcal D_n$ and $k = \lceil (w+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$. Six auxiliary artefacts compute the shift as $Q_{k/n}$, the empirical quantile at the level $k/n$ rather than the order statistic itself; on this panel the two differ by a median $\nSeqLevelGapMedian$ in relative terms and change the violation count of \nSeqLevelChanged{} of the \nSeqPairs{} pairs. That such a divergence existed at all has a cause worth naming, because it is the most productive single source of defect in this project: the convention was stated in a docstring and implemented in no function, so every producer rebuilt it, and four rebuilt it differently. A definition that lives in prose is not a definition that a pipeline can be held to. The shift is now defined once, in code, and every site in the repository that takes a quantile of nonconformity scores is enumerated with its convention by an audit that fails the build when an undeclared one appears. A further property of equation~\eqref{eq:qV} is worth stating where the reader meets it rather than where it bites: $k > n-1$ whenever $n < 2/\alpha - 1$, so at $\alpha = 0.01$ the shift is the \emph{sample maximum} for every calibration sample of 198 observations or fewer. A short calibration window therefore does not give a noisy estimate of the conformal quantile; it gives an extreme-value statistic with no stable variance. The bound is $n \le 198$ at $\alpha = 0.01$ and $n \le 78,\,38,\,18$ at $\alpha = 0.025,\,0.05,\,0.10$. The consequence for window choice is measured in Section~\ref{sec:cost}: the three trailing windows a reader would naturally compare at $\alpha = 0.01$, $w \in \{125, 250, 500\}$, are not three points on a variance curve, because $k/w$ depends on $w$ and the three therefore target different effective levels --- $\nWSweepShortLevel$, $\nWSweepMidLevel$ and $\nWSweepLongLevel$ against a nominal $0.99$ --- with $w = 125$ the extreme case at which the shift \emph{is} the window maximum. The recalibrated lower quantile and VaR are then given by equation~\eqref{eq:var_cp_roll} with $q_V^{\mathrm{stat}} = \qVstat$. A positive $\qVstat$ shifts the predicted quantile downward, increasing reported VaR and correcting underestimation of tail risk; a negative value indicates over-conservatism.

\begin{remark}[The effective level of the conformal shift]
	\label{rem:effective_level}
	Equation~\eqref{eq:qV} returns the $k$-th smallest of $n$ scores with
	$k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$, capped at $S_{(n)}$ when $k > n$. The
	estimator therefore targets the \emph{effective level} $\min(k,n)/n$ and not
	$1-\alpha$, and the difference is an overshoot
	\[
		0 \;<\; \frac{\min(k,n)}{n} - (1-\alpha) \;\le\; \alpha ,
	\]
	with equality at the upper bound for every $n \le 2/\alpha - 1$ --- exactly
	the regime in which $k$ reaches $n$ and the shift is the sample maximum,
	$n \le \nLevelBound$ at $\alpha = 0.01$. Away from that regime the overshoot
	is $\Theta(1/n)$, while the sampling standard deviation of the empirical
	level is $\Theta(n^{-1/2})$.

	The two therefore trade places as $n$ grows, and not once: the overshoot is a
	sawtooth in $n$, dropping whenever the ceiling increments and rising between,
	against a smooth standard deviation. At $\alpha = 0.01$ it exceeds the
	sampling standard deviation on $\nLevelDomOneLo \le n \le \nLevelDomOneHi$ and
	again on $\nLevelDomTwoLo \le n \le \nLevelDomTwoHi$, and the first of those
	runs ends precisely at the degeneracy boundary.

	Two consequences follow, and both are used below. A comparison of the
	estimator across sample sizes confounds the size with the level being
	targeted, because the second is a function of the first. And the confound is
	largest where the estimator is degenerate, so the two defects of a short
	calibration window are one boundary apart rather than independent. Section~\ref{sec:window}
	measures the confound on the rolling window and Supplement~S.4.1 measures it
	on the Monte Carlo at $n_{\mathrm{cal}} = \nSupNCal$.
\end{remark}

The construction makes no parametric assumption on returns; the coverage guarantee under temporal dependence is developed in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

The one-sided design is dictated by the regulatory loss function, not by a symmetry consideration. Basel III/FRTB capital and the Yellow/Red zone triggers are functions of loss-side exceedances alone (Section~\ref{sec:economic}): a day on which the realised return falls below the reported VaR is counted, a day on which it comfortably exceeds it is not. A correction applied to the upper tail would therefore alter no capital charge and no zone assignment, while widening the reported interval and degrading sharpness. We accordingly shift only $\hat q_t^{lo}$ and leave the remainder of the predictive distribution untouched; two-sided and distributional alternatives are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}. Operational safeguards, including the non-negativity constraint $\qVstat \geq 0$, are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\begin{remark}[Variance scaling under tail sparsity, in the forecaster and in the correction]
	\label{rem:tail_sparsity}
	Tail calibration relies on events $\{r_t \leq \hat q_t^{lo}\}$, whose expected number is $\E[N_\alpha] = \alpha T$. The constraint binds twice, and the two layers are worth separating. An \emph{estimator} fitted to that tail subset inherits variance of order $k/N_\alpha$ for $k$ parameters, whatever it is estimating; a \emph{correction} estimated from the same subset inherits it again. By contrast, $\qVstat$ is a single order statistic of the full calibration sample --- the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th, by equation~\eqref{eq:qV}, and not a quantity estimated from the $N_\alpha$ tail observations alone. A one-parameter correction therefore enjoys a favourable bias--variance trade-off under tail sparsity, and the correction magnitude is interpretable as a diagnostic precisely because the construction adds no further parameters. Values of $\qVstat$ that dominate the scale of $\hat q_t^{lo}$ signal the high-$\bar R$ range.

	The forecaster layer is visible directly. Gradient-boosted quantile regression on \nMLAssets{} assets and \nMLDates{} dates moves its realised exceedance rate by a factor of \nMLGbmTailSpan{} across the leaf-size grid, from the second grid point upward, while the centre of the same predictive law moves by \nMLGbmCentreSpread{} estimated standard deviations; at the library default of $\texttt{min\_data\_in\_leaf} = \nMLGbmLeafDefault$ the rate is $\nMLGbmPiDefault$. A quantile random forest on the same assets and dates spans a factor of \nMLQrfTailSpan. The parameter that resolves the leaf is a tail-sample-size parameter, so this is the remark's own mechanism and not a configuration accident: three documented control knobs each move the tail past the same threshold, and a second granularity parameter does not move it at all (Supplement~S.13).
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Validity under Temporal Dependence}
\label{sec:validity}

Standard conformal coverage guarantees require exchangeability, which volatility
clustering and serial dependence in financial returns violate.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} replaces that assumption with geometric $\beta$-mixing
and states an asymptotic coverage result for the single-split estimator, a
corollary fixing the mixing rate for GARCH data-generating processes, and two
bounds for objects adjacent to the rolling variant. The construction here makes
no parametric assumption on returns; what it requires of the dependence
structure, and the one condition the contiguous split does not meet, are set out
there.

\subsubsection{Rolling Conformal Calibration}
\label{sec:rolling_method}

Financial time series exhibit time-varying volatility and structural change, motivating a locally adaptive conformal correction. In the rolling implementation, the conformal threshold is recomputed at each time $t$ from a trailing window of size $w = 250$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:qV_roll}
	\qVroll
	=
	S_{(k_w)},
	\qquad
	k_w = \left\lceil (w+1)(1-\alpha) \right\rceil,
\end{equation}
where $S_{(1)}\le\cdots\le S_{(w)}$ are the order statistics of the trailing-window scores, so the rolling shift uses the same finite-sample conformal convention as equation~\eqref{eq:qV} and not the plain empirical quantile. The recalibrated lower quantile and VaR follow from equation~\eqref{eq:var_cp_roll} with $q_V^{\mathrm{roll}}(t) = \qVroll$.

The rolling variant is an operational heuristic, not a construction with finite-sample conformal validity: Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var} covers the single-split estimator only, and recomputing the threshold from a trailing window violates the single-split assumption.\footnote{Complementary results from the online learning literature \citep{zinkevich2003online} and the weighted conformal framework \citep{barber2023conformal,angelopoulos2024pid} bound time-average miscoverage and finite-window deviation under drift, but cover related objects --- exact OGD updates and exponentially weighted quantiles, respectively --- not the uniform-weight rolling quantile used here. Propositions~\ref{prop:ogd_regret} and~\ref{prop:rolling_drift} state what is available for them.} Empirical support comes from the Monte Carlo study of Section~\ref{sec:mc} and from the drift diagnostic $\hat\delta_w(t)$ (Supplement~S.9.6), which flags windows where local recalibration departs from the global one.

Remark~\ref{rem:rolling_status} states what the rolling estimator is entitled to claim.

\subsubsection{Alternative Recalibration Baselines}
\label{sec:alternatives}

We compare the one-parameter conformal shift against alternatives that vary on three axes: parameter count, correction structure (location only, location--scale, monotone, fully nonparametric), and target object (correction, conditional distribution, or tail). We compare against ten baselines: five single-shot post-hoc corrections, adaptive conformal inference, two distributional or nonlinear methods (GAMLSS-SST and gradient-boosted quantile regression), and two dedicated VaR models (EVT-POT and filtered historical simulation). Full specifications are in Supplement~S.2; Table~S.3 of the supplement provides a compact reference. The comparison tests Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} on the correction layer: at $\alpha = 0.01$, additional parameters in the \emph{correction} increase variance faster than they reduce bias. The remark's forecaster layer is tested separately, by the leaf-resolution response recorded there. Empirical results are in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}.

\subsection{Data and Models}
\label{sec:data_models}

\subsubsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis covers 24 financial assets across five classes: 11 equity indices, 3 government bond exchange-traded funds (ETFs), 4 commodities, 2 cryptocurrencies, and 4 foreign exchange (FX) pairs. Daily log-returns are computed from adjusted closing prices obtained from Yahoo Finance over January 2000--March 2026. Price series were retrieved on 13 March 2026. Cryptocurrency histories begin in 2014 for Bitcoin and 2017 for Ethereum.

Returns are computed on each asset's native trading calendar without cross-asset alignment, and no further data cleaning is applied: missing days are dropped rather than forward-filled, and asynchronous closes across classes are retained. The evaluation therefore reflects deployment conditions rather than a synchronised research panel.

Each asset's return series is partitioned chronologically: the single-split estimator uses the first 70\% as calibration and the remaining 30\% as the out-of-sample test window ($f_c = 0.70$, Section~\ref{sec:cqr_var}); the rolling estimator recomputes the threshold at each $t$ from a trailing window of size $w = 250$ (Section~\ref{sec:rolling_method}) and is evaluated on the same 30\% test window. The full asset list, per-asset sample sizes, and per-asset test windows are in Supplement~S.7.

\subsubsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We evaluate six TSFM forecasters in strict zero-shot mode (no updating or fine-tuning) against four classical benchmarks estimated per asset. Fine-tuning TSFMs is left to future work.

The TSFM set spans two predictive interfaces. Sample-based forecasters (Chronos-Small, Chronos-Mini \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2} with tokenised sequence models; Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2024lagllama} with a LLaMA-inspired architecture and Student-$t$ heads; Moirai~1.1 \citep{woo2024moirai}) return $N = 1{,}000$ Monte Carlo draws from the predictive distribution. Quantile-grid forecasters (TimesFM~2.5 \citep{das2024timesfm}; Moirai~2.0 \citep{liu2025moirai}) return a fixed nine-decile grid, from which a 1\% quantile must be supplied by a tail-completion step. Each Chronos checkpoint enters the panel \emph{twice}: once as the shipped series, sampled under the checkpoint's own default configuration, and once read analytically from the same weights by the analytic estimator of Supplement~S.14.4. The pair is the paper's controlled comparison, and it holds the model fixed while varying only the code that turns its output into a threshold.

The classical benchmarks --- GJR-GARCH \citep{glosten1993relation}, GARCH-Normal \citep{bollerslev1986generalized}, EWMA \citep{riskmetrics1996} and Historical Simulation --- represent standard volatility and tail-risk models; estimation details are in Supplement~S.8 and in Section~\ref{sec:exp_design}.

\subsubsection{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:exp_design}

\textbf{Two panels, named once.} The paper reports two, and mixing them is the
easiest way to produce a number nobody can reconcile, so they are defined here
and tagged wherever they appear.

\emph{The main panel} is \nMainForecasters{} forecasters $\times$ \nMainAssets{}
assets $=$ \nMainPairs{} pairs at $\alpha = 0.01$. This is Table~\ref{tab:master}.
It includes CAViaR-AS, CAViaR-SAV and GAS-$t$, which are estimated directly from
returns by a separate routine and produce no stored forecast series.

\emph{The sequence panel} is \nSeqForecasters{} forecasters $\times$
\nMainAssets{} assets $=$ \nSeqPairs{} pairs, evaluated at four levels. It is the
subset with stored per-date series, and every analysis that needs one runs on it:
the Christoffersen degeneracy rates by level, the Diebold--Mariano comparisons,
the wild-cluster bootstrap, the panel-pooled coverage tests, the deterioration
counts behind the indication rule, and Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}.

The two agree wherever they overlap --- the sequence panel's static Basel zones
reproduce Table~\ref{tab:master} on all \nSeqPairs{} shared pairs --- and every
figure in the text is generated from the artefacts by
\texttt{scripts/paper\_numbers.py}, which fails the build if a value in the
manuscript drifts from the value in the data.

\textbf{The comparison is deliberately asymmetric, and we state that here rather than as a qualification later.} The classical and dynamic benchmarks are estimated per asset and re-estimated at every step; the foundation models are deployed strictly zero-shot, with no fine-tuning and no per-asset adaptation. These are not equivalent adaptation regimes, and any Quantile Score comparison between the two groups reflects that difference as well as any difference in model quality. We retain the asymmetry because it is the operationally relevant contrast --- a zero-shot model is what a practitioner can deploy without an estimation pipeline per instrument --- but no conclusion in this paper rests on a TSFM-versus-GARCH score ranking. The audit's findings are within-group: the interface contrast is between two releases of the same family under identical deployment, and the recalibration results are computed separately for every forecaster.

All forecasts are one-step-ahead. TSFMs use a fixed context length of 512 observations, the default for all six pretrained checkpoints (Supplement~S.8). The GARCH-family benchmarks and Historical Simulation are estimated on rolling windows of 250 observations, following the Basel convention \citep{basel1996}, with parameters re-estimated at each step. EWMA carries no estimation window: its variance is the RiskMetrics recursion at the conventional $\lambda = 0.94$, so its memory is geometrically decaying rather than truncated. That distinction is immaterial numerically --- the weight beyond lag $k$ is $\lambda^k$, which is $\nLitLambdaTail$ at $k = 250$ --- but it is material for the finite-context condition of Lemma~\ref{lem:score_mixing}, and Remark~\ref{rem:finite_context} treats it explicitly rather than folding EWMA into the 250-observation convention.

A warm-up period (512 observations for TSFMs, 250 for the windowed classical benchmarks) precedes the calibration--test split. For full-history series (2000--2026), the test sample is approximately 1{,}500 observations per asset, yielding roughly 36{,}600 panel observations per model and 366 expected violations at $\alpha = 0.01$.

\section{The Audit}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Five implementation failure modes}
\label{sec:audit_trail}

The forecast-production pipeline was audited step by step, from the raw price
series to the stored threshold, before any forecast entered the evaluation. It
isolates five ways in which the reduction from a predictive object to a
Value-at-Risk number can fail while leaving a plausible series behind, and all
five were found in this pipeline. The enumeration, the diagnostic for each, and
the realised exceedance rate with and without the defect are in
Supplement~S.15; the two that matter for what follows are the support
truncation of Supplement~S.14 and the inverted sign convention, whose
$\hat\pi$ at $\alpha = 0.01$ was $\nRawPiTimesFMDefective$ and
$\nRawPiMoiraiTwoDefective$ before correction. What the enumeration is for is
stated once: each mode is invisible to a coverage backtest applied after the
reduction, which is the ordering argument of Section~\ref{sec:gate} arriving
from the implementation side.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Raw Diagnostics and the Ordering by $\bar R$}{Raw Diagnostics and the Ordering by R}}
\label{sec:results_raw}

Table~\ref{tab:master} reports raw and conformally corrected diagnostics across
\nMainForecasters{} forecasters and \nMainAssets{} assets, ordered by $\bar R$. Its most useful feature is the
narrowness of the range it spans. Once the two series sampled at the checkpoint default are set
aside, raw 1\% violation rates run from \nMainRawPiMin{} for CAViaR-AS to \nMainRawPiMax{} for GAS-$t$
--- a factor of three across dynamic-quantile models, score-driven models,
GARCH-family benchmarks, historical simulation and six zero-shot foundation-model
series. Including the two truncated series the range is a factor of 38, and that
factor is a property of a sampling parameter rather than of any forecaster.

The panel is uniformly under-covered at the 1\% level and nothing in it is well
calibrated there. No forecaster passes unconditional coverage on more than
\nMainBestKupiec{} of \nMainAssets{} assets, the best being a per-asset CAViaR
fitted the ordinary way \citep{engle2004caviar}; the six foundation-model series
pass on between 0 and 13. Across the whole table \nMainKupiecRawPasses{} of
\nMainPairs{} pairs pass raw, and \nMainKupiecCorPasses{} pass after correction,
so recalibration does not deliver a uniformly passing panel either. A reader
looking for a foundation model that is competitive at the extreme tail will not
find one here; a reader looking for one that fails categorically will not find
that either, once its reduction step is configured correctly.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{What the ratio $R$ measures}{What the ratio R measures}}
\label{sec:R_meaning}

The ratio
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:replacement_ratio}
	R = \frac{|\qVstat|}{|\VaR_{\mathrm{raw}}|}
\end{equation}
measures how large the conformal correction is relative to the threshold it
adjusts. It is a ratio of absolute values, hence non-negative by construction: it
carries magnitude only, and the sign that carries the diagnosis belongs to
$\qVstat$ and is reported separately (Section~\ref{sec:practitioner} and the last
column of Table~\ref{tab:master}). In this panel only the two truncated Chronos
series exceed one, at $\bar R = \nMainRTruncOne$ and $\nMainRTruncTwo$;
everything else lies between $\nMainRMin$ and $\nMainRMax$.

$R$ should not be read as an instrument carrying information the standard
backtests do not. Among the well-specified forecasters it is a rank-preserving
transform of the raw violation rate --- Spearman $\nSpearmanRPi$ across the
\nSpearmanRPiN{} well-specified forecasters --- and a detection rule built on the
violation rate alone reproduces every sensitivity and specificity figure it
delivers. It is a scale-interpretable restatement of a quantity already printed
in the adjacent column, useful for saying \emph{how much} of a forecast survives
correction, and not an independent test. In the language of
\citet{gneiting2023regression}, $\qVstat$ estimates the unconditional
miscalibration component: an identity between estimators rather than a finding.
What is empirical is its size --- on well-specified series a constant shift
addresses a median of \nuMCBSharePct\% of total miscalibration, so most of what
is wrong with these forecasts is conditional and a scalar correction cannot reach
it. The full ordering by $\bar R$ and the structure at both ends of its range are
in Supplement~S.12.

\subsubsection{The quantile-grid interface}
\label{sec:interface_failure}

It is tempting to read the panel as a comparison of predictive interfaces --- a
coarse quantile grid against a sampled predictive law --- and to ask which of the
two can deliver a backtestable 1\% VaR. The panel does not support such a claim
in either direction.
The best raw foundation-model series at the 1\% level is a quantile-grid model,
TimesFM~2.5 at $\nRawPiTimesFM$; the sample-based Moirai~1.1 is at
$\nRawPiMoiraiOne$, the analytic Chronos series at $\nRawPiChronosSmallA$ and
$\nRawPiChronosMiniA$, the quantile-grid Moirai~2.0 at $\nRawPiMoiraiTwo$, and
the sample-based Lag-Llama at $\nRawPiLagLlama$. The two interfaces do not
separate.

What can be said is narrower, and is a statement about specification rather than
about models. A nine-decile grid whose lowest point is the 10\% quantile does
not contain a 1\% quantile, so a tail-completion step must supply it and becomes
part of the forecaster. That step is consequential: across three assets the same
grids give $\bar R$ between $\nLitClosureRMin$ and $\nLitClosureRMax$ depending on whether the closure is
Student-$t$, Gaussian or linear, and on BTC the choice moves both models from
the Basel yellow zone to the red one (Supplement~S.10). A figure reported as
``TimesFM~2.5 at the 1\% level'' therefore identifies the closure as much as the
model, and is not interpretable without it.

The panel offers no controlled comparison of interfaces, and a shared family
name does not supply one. The two Moirai
releases are the natural candidate, and they run at $\nRawPiMoiraiOne$ and
$\nRawPiMoiraiTwo$, a gap of \nLitMoiraiGapPP{} percentage points. But Moirai~1.1 is a
masked-encoder model with multi-patch inputs and a mixture output, while
Moirai~2.0 replaces all three with a decoder-only architecture, a single patch
and a quantile loss, trained on a different corpus
\citep{woo2024moirai,liu2025moirai}. Output format, architecture and pretraining
data change together, so a gap of any size between them would be unattributable.
What differs between two releases is a primary-source question, and it settles
before a comparison is drawn. The one genuinely controlled contrast
available here holds the weights, contexts and dates fixed and varies only the
code that reads the predictive law --- the two Chronos configurations of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.\footnote{The \nGateSeries{} series with forecast files in the replication
archive have been through the scripted validation gate of
Section~\ref{sec:gate} and Supplement~S.11. The three dynamic-quantile
forecasters of Table~\ref{tab:master} --- CAViaR-AS, CAViaR-SAV and GAS-t ---
are estimated per asset by a separate pipeline that stores no series, so the
gate has no object to run on and they are ungated rather than passed. \nGateBlocked{} do not pass it: the two Chronos series
sampled at the checkpoint default, which fail five checks each, and their
analytic counterparts, which fail the coverage band on one asset apiece, and
Chronos-Mini-A the $\alpha$-response band on one asset besides. The
first two are retained in Table~\ref{tab:master} deliberately, as the measured
consequence of that configuration rather than as candidate forecasters; a blocked series is
reported with its diagnosis, not silently dropped.}

\section{What the Standard Diagnostics Cannot Diagnose}
\label{sec:blindspot}

Section~\ref{sec:audit_trail} lists five ways in which a Value-at-Risk series can
be produced incorrectly. This section asks what the standard tail diagnostics
report when they are applied to such a series, and the answer is not that they
are blind to miscalibration --- Kupiec's test rejects most of this panel. It is
narrower: a coverage test reports \emph{that} a series miscovers, never
\emph{why}. It cannot separate a model that is genuinely poor in the tail from a
series carrying any of the failure modes of Table~S.31, all of
which present as the same rejection. At $\alpha = 0.01$ these tests also have
their least power exactly where the regulatory framework places them, and a
correction applied downstream of them removes the evidence they would need. An
implementation failure can therefore pass through a pipeline untouched.

The first two subsections state that claim as a result rather than as an
observation, and the rest of the section measures it on the panel.

\subsection{What the exceedance sequence identifies}
\label{sec:blindspot_identification}

Write $\mathcal G_{t-1}$ for the information available when the forecast for date
$t$ is made, let $\hat q^{lo}_t$ be the reported lower $\alpha$-quantile, which is
$\mathcal G_{t-1}$-measurable, and let $F_t$ denote the conditional law of $r_t$
given $\mathcal G_{t-1}$. Define the \emph{realised} tail probability the reported
threshold cuts off,
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:u_process}
	u_t \;:=\; F_t\!\left(\hat q^{lo}_t\right)
	\;=\; \P\!\left(r_t < \hat q^{lo}_t \mid \mathcal G_{t-1}\right),
\end{equation}
which is itself $\mathcal G_{t-1}$-measurable, and let
$V_t = \mathbf 1\{r_t < \hat q^{lo}_t\}$ be the exceedance indicator of
Section~\ref{sec:diag}.

\begin{proposition}[What a backtest can see]
	\label{prop:identification}
	For any $T$ and any $v_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^T$,
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:path_likelihood}
		\P\left(V_{1:T}=v_{1:T}\right)
		=
		\E\left[\prod_{t=1}^{T} u_t^{\,v_t}\,(1-u_t)^{1-v_t}\right].
	\end{equation}
	The law of the exceedance sequence therefore depends on the joint law of
	$(\{\hat q^{lo}_t\},\{r_t\})$ only through the law of the process
	$\{u_t\}$. Consequently, if two models induce the same law for $\{u_t\}$,
	then every test $\phi$ measurable with respect to $\sigma(V_1,\dots,V_T)$
	satisfies $\E_P[\phi]=\E_Q[\phi]$: its power against the alternative equals
	its size. The same holds for any test of a conditional-moment restriction
	$\E[(V_t-\alpha)Z_{t-1}]=0$ with $Z_{t-1}$ predictable, since both models
	satisfy the restriction exactly.
\end{proposition}

The proof is one application of iterated conditioning and is in Supplement~S.9.7.
Statistics that read the \emph{magnitude} of an exceedance rather than its
occurrence lie outside the class, and on this panel they do separate: the
Acerbi--Sz\'ekely $Z_2$ statistic of Section~\ref{sec:diag} passes \nMainZTwoPassTrunc{} of
\nMainAssets{} assets for each truncated Chronos series against
\nMainZTwoPassAnalytic{} of
\nMainAssets{} for each analytic counterpart, the sharpest separation any
diagnostic in this paper achieves (Supplement~S.1). That is the boundary of
Proposition~\ref{prop:identification}. It is not a general escape: the construction of
Section~\ref{sec:blindspot_pair} can be matched on mean expected shortfall as
well, after which a size-$5\%$ $Z_2$ test retains almost no power
(Supplement~S.9.8).
The class named in the proposition contains every instrument this paper uses:
Kupiec's unconditional coverage test is a function of $\sum_t V_t$; both
Christoffersen components are functions of the transition counts of $V$; the
Basel traffic light is a function of $\sum_t V_t$; and the dynamic quantile test
of \citet{engle2004caviar} regresses $V_t-\alpha$ on predictable instruments,
which is the moment-restriction case. None of them can distinguish two models
agreeing on $\{u_t\}$, at any sample size.

That is a statement about what a rejection means, not a claim that these tests
are badly designed. A rejection says $u_t\neq\alpha$; it does not say whether the
cause lies in the model, in the reduction step, or in the returns.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{How large the resulting blind spot is}{How large the resulting blind spot is}}
\label{sec:blindspot_pair}

Proposition~\ref{prop:identification} is only useful if the set of
indistinguishable alternatives contains objects a risk manager would care to
separate. It does. Let $\delta$ denote the \emph{truncation depth}: the
probability mass discarded from each tail of a predictive law before its
$\alpha$-quantile is read. It is distinct from the drift diagnostic
$\hat\delta_w(t)$ of Supplement~S.9.6, which shares no more than the letter.

\begin{proposition}[The depth of the blind spot]
	\label{prop:blindspot_depth}
	Fix $\alpha = 0.01$ and a correctly specified forecaster whose returns are
	standardised Student-$t_5$, so that its reported quantile is
	$\nGapQTrueStd$ in units of conditional scale. If the alternative model's
	return law is required only to be symmetric and unimodal with the same
	variance and the same mean absolute deviation, then for every
	$\delta \le \delta^\star = \nGapDeltaUni$ there exists a truncated
	forecaster and a return law under which the exceedance sequence has exactly
	the same law as under the correctly specified pair, while the reported
	Value-at-Risk is understated by up to \nGapUndUni\%. For
	$\delta > \delta^\star$ no such unimodal law exists.
\end{proposition}

The construction is explicit and is given in Supplement~S.9.8 as a linear
programme: the constraints --- coverage, variance, mean absolute deviation, and
the reported quantile --- are all linear in the probabilities of a discrete
symmetric law, so feasibility is decided by the programme. The resulting pair
is matched on the predictive standard deviation, on point-forecast accuracy in
both mean absolute error and root mean squared error, and on the median; it
differs only in the reported threshold and in the shape of the predictive law
away from it.

$\delta^\star$ belongs to the restriction placed on the return law and is not a
constant. Dropping unimodality raises it to $\nGapDeltaFree$, an understatement
of \nGapUndFree\%; requiring in addition a fourth moment no larger than the
honest model's lowers it to $\nGapDeltaMoment$; confining the alternative to a
GARCH class with standardised Student-$t$ innovations leaves only
\nGapUndGarchT\%. The discretisation
converges from above, so each figure is an upper bound and the bias runs toward
overstating the blind spot. Supplement~S.9.8 gives the full table.

Two consequences run through the rest of the paper. Section~\ref{sec:gate}
measures how much of this depth structural checks remove. And the Chronos
configuration of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} sits \emph{outside} the blind
region: the tail probability it cuts off shows up as a realised exceedance rate
of $\nPiSmallDefaultOne$, well beyond $\nGapDeltaFree$, which is why
unconditional coverage rejects it on every asset
--- while rejecting \nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\% of the panel besides. Detection is not
diagnosis, and the two separate exactly where Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot_depth}
says they should.

\subsection{The independence test is frequently degenerate}
\label{sec:blindspot_cc}

The independence component of \citet{christoffersen1998} conditions on the
transition counts of the exceedance indicator. When no two violations fall on
consecutive days the table is degenerate: $\hat\pi_{11}$ is estimated at the
boundary of the parameter space, the likelihood ratio can be defined by
continuity but its $\chi^2_1$ calibration is not valid there, and the test has
essentially no power against the alternative it is meant to detect. Standard
implementations differ in what they do about this --- ours returns a missing
value, others return a $p$-value computed from a statistic whose asymptotics do
not hold. Implementations differ in what they return in that state --- a missing
value here, a $p$-value computed from a statistic whose asymptotics fail
elsewhere --- and the substantive point does not depend on the choice: a
degenerate table carries no evidence either way. At $\alpha = 0.01$ this is not a
corner case. Across the \nSeqPairs{} model--asset pairs of the sequence panel the
table is degenerate on \nSeqCCUndefRawOne\% of the raw series and on
\nSeqCCUndefCorOne\% of the same pairs after recalibration. The share falls
monotonically with the nominal level --- \nSeqCCUndefRawTwoFive\%,
\nSeqCCUndefRawFive\% and \nSeqCCUndefRawTen\% raw at $\alpha = 0.025$, $0.05$
and $0.10$ --- so this is a property of the tail probability rather than of this
panel.

Two things follow. First, recalibration makes the problem worse rather than
better, because a correction that restores coverage also spaces violations out,
and the tests are usually run on the corrected series. Second, and more
seriously, a degenerate table is an absence of evidence, but the natural
implementation returns ``no rejection'', which reads as a pass. A test with
essentially no power on half of its applications, reported as passing on all of
them, is not evidence of independence. Where the table \emph{is} informative the
test rejects on \nSeqCCRejRawOne\% of raw pairs and \nSeqCCRejCorOne\% of
recalibrated ones, so the non-degenerate cases are not reassuring either.

\subsection{Unconditional coverage rejects almost everything}
\label{sec:blindspot_kupiec}

The test of \citet{kupiec1995} is always defined, and at $\alpha = 0.01$ it
rejects \nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\% of the \nSeqPairs{} pairs, against
\nSeqKupiecRejRawTen\% at $\alpha = 0.10$. No forecaster in the panel passes on
more than \nMainBestKupiec{} of \nMainAssets{} assets at the 1\% level. A test
that rejects the majority of defensible models carries little information about
any one of them --- and, decisively for the question of this paper, it does not
separate a series carrying an implementation failure from one that is merely
imperfect. Miscoverage is detected; its source is not identified.

The separation was measured. The exercise is run on the
series as produced with each failure mode present, since that is the state in
which a diagnostic would have to identify one. Treating the five series affected
by an identified failure mode as positives and the eight unaffected series as
negatives, Kupiec's majority-fail rule attains sensitivity 5/5 at
specificity 2/8, a Fisher exact $p$ of $\nFisherKupiec$: it flags 11 of 13 forecasters,
which is not discrimination. The independence test attains 0/5. A severe cut on
the violation rate --- rejecting $\hat\pi > 0.10$ or $\hat\pi < 0.005$ ---
attains 5/5 at 8/8, $p = \nFisherSevere$. The finding concerns a \emph{threshold} rather
than a statistic: what separates an implementation failure from ordinary
miscalibration is severity, while Kupiec's threshold is calibrated to sample
size. The six Kupiec flags falling on unaffected forecasters were each followed
to a verdict, as specified in advance; none corresponds to an implementation
failure, so the 2/8 figure stands as measured rather than as an artefact of
unexamined positives. Two qualifications belong with it: thirteen forecasters is
a small sample for a discrimination exercise, and the positives are the failure
modes this audit found, so the exercise measures separation on identified cases
and not sensitivity to failures of a kind not represented here.

\subsection{The most elaborate of the three discriminates least}
\label{sec:blindspot_dq}

The dynamic quantile regression of \citet{engle2004caviar} nests both of the
preceding tests: it regresses the centred exceedance indicator on its own lags
and on the reported quantile, so a failure of unconditional coverage, a failure
of independence, and any dependence of exceedances on the threshold level all
appear as departures from the null. If sophistication helped, it would help here.

It does not. On the \nSeqDQCells{} cells of the sequence panel the test rejects
\nSeqDQRejRaw\% of raw series at the 5\% level and \nSeqDQRejCor\% after
correction --- a higher raw rejection rate than Kupiec's
\nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\%, and one that falls on the well-specified series as
heavily as on the others. The two truncated Chronos series are rejected on all
\nMainAssets{} assets, as they should be; so are GARCH-N, EWMA and GJR-GARCH on
\nMainDQRejBenchmark{} of \nMainAssets{}, and GJR-GARCH-$t$ on
\nMainDQRejGjrT. A test that rejects four series in
five, including every benchmark a practitioner would consider defensible,
separates nothing.

This is Proposition~\ref{prop:identification} showing through. Adding the
reported quantile to the instrument set does not enlarge what the exceedance
sequence identifies, because the quantile is predictable and the moment
restriction it tests is one both a correct and a truncated forecaster can satisfy
exactly. What the extra structure buys is power against \emph{some} alternatives,
at the cost of rejecting the ones that are merely imperfect --- which at
$\alpha = 0.01$ is most of them.

\subsection{Recalibration conceals what it corrects}
\label{sec:blindspot_conformal}

Split-conformal recalibration targets marginal coverage regardless of the
forecaster it is applied to --- exactly in finite samples when the scores are
exchangeable \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution}, and asymptotically
under the dependence assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:dependent_conformal_var},
which the contiguous split used here does not satisfy exactly
(Section~\ref{sec:theory_gap}). What matters for this section is what both
versions share: the guarantee is indifferent to the forecaster it is applied to.
In this panel \nMainGreen{} of \nMainPairs{} pairs reach the Basel green zone
after correction. Applied to the Chronos series truncated at
$\texttt{top\_k} = 50$ --- a series whose predictive dispersion is
\nDispDefaultSmall{} of realised volatility and whose exceedance rate is nearly
invariant to the nominal level --- the correction returns $\hat\pi =
\nTruncCorPi$ and places \nTruncCorGreen{} of \nMainAssets{} assets in the green
zone. It does the same for a series whose quantile has been stored under the
opposite sign convention.

Coverage-based backtests downstream of that step therefore lose most of their
power to separate: after correction the violation rates of every forecaster
cluster within a fraction of a percentage point of nominal, the truncated series
among them, and the green-zone counts of Table~\ref{tab:master} range over a
narrow band that no longer orders the forecasters. This is not a failure of the
method: a correction that guarantees coverage cannot
diagnose the forecaster it corrects, precisely because the guarantee holds
whatever the forecaster is. The practical implication is that a pipeline which
validates \emph{after} recalibration has, by construction, no test of the
forecaster at all.

\section{Why Structural Validation Precedes Correction}
\label{sec:gate}
\label{sec:precede}

The ordering is forced by two facts already established. The correction restores
marginal coverage whatever the forecaster (Section~\ref{sec:recal}), so a
pipeline that validates after correcting has no test of the forecaster; and a
coverage test identifies only the realised tail probability
$u_t = F_t(\hat q^{lo}_t)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:identification}), so validating
before correcting does not help either, if the validation is itself a coverage
test. What is left is a check that reads the series rather than its exceedances,
and it has to run first.

\paragraph{What is new here, and what is not} That exceedance-based backtests are
weak at $\alpha = 0.01$ is established, and this paper claims no priority over
it. \citet{escanciano2012pitfalls} prove the unconditional test inconsistent
against Historical Simulation and Filtered Historical Simulation, and their
Lemma~1 then exhibits a weighted backtest that is consistent --- a result
recovering inside the exceedance class the power the unconditional test loses.
\citet{gordy2020spectral} and \citet{kratz2018multinomial} treat the poverty of
the single-level indicator as a premise to design around, the first by reading
the probability integral transform and the second by reading exceptions at four
levels, and Gordy and McNeil state the requirement plainly: what is essential is
that the regulator observes more than the exception indicator at one level.

Two things separate the present argument from that literature and neither is the
observation of weakness.

The first is the characterisation, and it is what lets the two results stand
together. \citet{escanciano2012pitfalls}'s alternatives agree with the null on
the \emph{mean} of $u_t$; the alternatives of
Proposition~\ref{prop:identification} agree with it on the whole process
$\{u_t\}$. Against the first, a weighted backtest recovers power, and their
Lemma~1 constructs one. Against the second, \emph{no} statistic measurable with
respect to the exceedance sequence separates them, that weighted backtest
included. The two propositions are about different alternative sets and neither
contradicts the other; what the second adds is where the exceedance class ends.

The second is the information set, and it is not a weaker claim than theirs but a
claim about a different one. \citet{gordy2020spectral} and
\citet{kratz2018multinomial} establish what a supervisor can do who holds the
predictive distribution: read the probability integral transform, or take
exceptions at four levels, and recover power the single-level indicator cannot
carry. That is correct and it is not available here. A firm consuming a
third-party quantile series --- a vendor model, a pretrained checkpoint, a
counterparty's reported thresholds --- holds the thresholds and the realised
returns and nothing else, and cannot obtain a PIT value from a forecaster that
never published a distribution. The structural checks are built for that
position: they read the series and the returns, which is what such a user has.
The two literatures are answering the same question on two information sets, and
the second is the one most consumers of a forecast are in.

This section measures how much of the blind spot of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot_depth} a structural check removes. Measured on
the understatement it permits, which is the quantity
Table~\ref{tab:gate_residual} reports, the answer is a little over a third.

A forecast series can be checked against properties it must have \emph{to be a
Value-at-Risk series at all}, before it is calibrated, evaluated or compared with
anything. The checks are stated on the lower quantile $\hat q^{lo}_\alpha$, which
is negative and is what the forecast files store, and not on the positive
threshold $\VaR_t^{\mathrm{raw}} = -\hat q_t^{lo}$ of
equation~\eqref{eq:var_raw}; the two conventions differ by a sign, and a column
named for a risk measure while holding a quantile is precisely the ambiguity
that the first failure mode of Table~S.31 exploits.

Ten such checks are stated in Supplement~S.11 and implemented in
\texttt{scripts/promotion\_gate.py}: a lower quantile whose median is not
negative, quantiles that are not ordered across $\alpha$, an exceedance rate that
does not respond to the nominal level, a predictive dispersion far from realised
volatility, a support of a handful of distinct values.

The ten are not of equal standing. Two are invariants that follow from what a
quantile forecast is and carry no tunable constant; one is an invariant tested
through a proxy with a threshold; the remaining seven are plausibility bands
with chosen cutoffs, stated in Table~\ref{tab:gate_compact}. Seven of the ten need nothing but the forecast
series and the returns. Tail reach needs the model's sampled paths and is
inapplicable to grid and parametric forecasters; $\alpha$-responsiveness and
coverage plausibility need an evaluation window, so the gate is not entirely
upstream of the data a backtest consumes. What separates them from a backtest is the
question they ask: not whether a coverage rate is statistically distinguishable
from nominal, but whether the series responds to the level it was asked for at
all. No $p$-value is computed, no benchmark is required, and none of the ten
needs access to the hardware that produced the forecasts.

\label{sec:gate_residual}

The construction of Section~\ref{sec:blindspot_pair} makes this measurable. For
a given restriction on the return law, let $\delta^\star$ be the largest
truncation depth admitting an alternative model that the available diagnostics
cannot distinguish from a correctly specified one, and read off the

The two halves of the result point in opposite directions. Structural validation
does not close the identification gap, so the checks are not a complete

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Where the band edge can sit, and how much margin it has}{Where the band edge can sit, and how much margin it has}}
\label{sec:gate_band}

The scale band --- $\operatorname{median}(\hat q^{lo}_{0.01}/\hat\sigma_t)$
within $[\nGapBandLower, \nGapBandNow]$ --- is the check that binds, and its
edges were chosen by hand. On these data the choice does not matter, and the
separation that makes it not matter is the quantity worth reporting.

Evaluated on all \nGapCellsTotal{} series--asset cells, the two populations are
disjoint by a wide interval. The worst well-specified cell sits at
$\nGapCellGoodWorst$ and the best truncated cell at $\nGapCellTruncBest$, a gap
of $\nGapGapWidth$ in units of estimated scale. \emph{Any} edge strictly inside
that interval blocks all \nGapCellsTrunc{} truncated cells and no well-specified
one, so the gate's verdict on this panel is invariant to where the edge is placed
within the gap. The verdict therefore does not rest on the choice at all.

The safety margin is a different quantity from the gap, and the smaller of the
two. It is the distance from the edge to the nearest well-specified cell, and at
$\nGapBandNow$ it is $\nGapMarginUpper$ --- an order of magnitude smaller than
the gap. Tightening the edge to $\nTightBand$ reduces the residual understatement from
\nGapUndGateNow\% to \nTightUnd\%, and leaves a margin of $\nTightMargin$: essentially none. At the current edge of $\nGapBandNow$ the
gap exceeds the margin by a factor of eleven; at $\nTightBand$ it exceeds it
by more than two hundred. Robustness read off the gap rather than the margin
therefore overstates the check by one order of magnitude at the edge as
specified, and by two at the tightened one.

The margin measures the check against the panel that produced it, and on that
panel the two populations are far apart: eleven series whose reported quantile
sits between two and three estimated standard deviations below zero, and two
whose truncation has driven it to a fifth of that, with nothing between. A
separation established on a sample containing only gross failures and correct
specifications is a weak thing to report, because the discriminating power is
in-sample by construction. The band therefore has one test on this evidence
that is not: a family it was never shown.

The band edges were fixed before that family was run --- the declaration is dated
\nMLBandDeclared{} and the estimator panel \nMLPanelRun{} --- so what follows is
out of sample rather than a re-reading of the cells the edges were drawn around.
Gradient-boosted quantile regression and a quantile random forest were run on
\nMLAssets{} assets across \nMLLeafSettings{} leaf resolutions, \nMLCells{} cells
at \nMLDates{} dates each, and they land on opposite sides of the question. The
forest places every cell inside the band, between $\nMLQrfRatioLo$ and
$\nMLQrfRatioHi$. The boosted estimator places \nMLGbmBlocked{} of its
\nMLGbmCells{} cells between $\nGapCellGoodWorst$ and $\nGapCellTruncBest$ ---
the interval the sequence panel leaves empty --- and the check blocks all
\nMLGbmBlocked. Of those, \nMLBlockedUnder{} are on cells exceeding at
\nMLUnderThreshold{} times nominal or more, and no cell the check passes exceeds
at that rate; the remaining \nMLBlockedNotUnder{} return three violations where
two were expected, which \nMLDates{} dates at $\alpha = 0.01$ cannot separate
from correct coverage.

So the intermediate range is no longer empty and no longer untested. On the one
family available that occupies it, the check discriminates rather than misfiring:
it blocks under-covering cells, passes the rest, and misses none. That is the
strongest statement this paper makes about the gate, and it is the only one
measured on series the gate was not built around.

The edge's false-positive region is then bounded in closed form rather than left
open. A correctly specified forecaster is blocked at $\nGapBandNow$ only if its
standardised innovation law puts its own $1\%$ quantile above that point, which
requires a tail lighter than a triangular law's $\nMLQuantTriangular$: Normal
innovations sit at $\nMLQuantNormal$ and standardised $t_5$ at $\nMLQuantTFive$,
and only a bounded law as light as the uniform, at $\nMLQuantUniform$, is caught.
The illustrative forecaster reporting $-\nMLWorkedExampleSigma\hat\sigma_t$ is
not in that class. It is under-conservative by a quarter measured on the
threshold and by a factor of \nMLWorkedNormal{} measured on coverage --- it
exceeds at \nMLWorkedNormal{} times nominal under Normal innovations,
\nMLWorkedTFive{} under standardised $t_5$ and \nMLWorkedTThree{} under $t_3$ ---
so blocking it is a correct verdict. The two units differ by a factor of nearly
three, and the limitation is the coverage one.

\paragraph{One of our own checks cannot fail, and that is the paper's argument
turned on itself} The lower edge $\nGapBandLower$ blocks
\nGapCellsLowerBlocks{} of \nGapCellsTotal{} cells. Nothing in the panel comes
near it: the most negative cell anywhere is $\nGapCellGoodBest$, a margin of
$\nGapMarginLower$, five times the margin at the upper edge. \emph{A check that
blocks nothing on any series it has been shown cannot fail informatively, so a
series passing it has not been tested --- which is precisely what
Section~\ref{sec:blindspot_cc} says about a Christoffersen statistic computed on
a degenerate transition table, and it is here in one of our own ten checks.} The
two cases have the same structure: an absence of evidence returned in the form
that reads as a pass, and a pass rate reported as though it were an outcome.

Adjusting the edge downward until it binds would manufacture the outcome, so the
band stands as specified and its consequence is recorded instead. On this panel
the scale check is one-sided; the band is asymmetric in risk; and whether a lower
edge is needed at all cannot be determined from these data, because nothing in
them is over-conservative enough to test one. The block count is nine checks
exercised and one that was not. The \nMLCells{} cells of the second estimator
family do not settle it either: the most negative of them reaches
$\nMLRatioMostNegative$, a margin of \nMLLowerEdgeMargin{} from the edge, and
\nMLBelowLowerEdge{} of them fall below it. That family is measured on
\nMLAssets{} assets rather than \nMainAssets, so it can only have shown the edge
binding and cannot show that it does not.

\paragraph{What the gate cannot see} The checks operate on a stored forecast
series and the returns. They can establish that a series has the properties a
Value-at-Risk series must have, and they cannot establish that the estimator
which produced it is the estimator the paper says produced it. A mismatch between
a declared estimator and an implemented one leaves no trace in the series: both
the plain empirical quantile and the conformal order statistic of
equation~\eqref{eq:qV} yield a well-formed series that passes all ten checks, and
only reading the code distinguishes them. Structural validation and code
provenance are complements, and neither is inferable from the other.

\paragraph{Nor can it validate prose} The same limit applies one level up.
Converting this manuscript's numeric claims to generated macros surfaced two
assertions that no artefact produces: a pair of figures comparing a modified and
a canonical test statistic, whose verification script computes neither of them,
and a pair of raw violation rates whose producer emits only corrected ones. Both
were in the version submitted for review and survived it. Seventy-nine numeric claims sat outside the reach of the script that
checks the manuscript's arithmetic, and a literal that is never emitted cannot be
checked by a tool that checks emitted values --- the structure of
Section~\ref{sec:blindspot_cc}, one level up from the forecasts. A gate that
validates series does not validate the sentences written about them, and the
argument of this paper applies to the paper.

\paragraph{Two checks are out of scope for order-statistic estimators} Support cardinality and tail reach
are inapplicable to order-statistic estimators, which may legitimately take few
distinct values and tie across adjacent $\alpha$ as a window slides; a discrete
predictive support may tie for the same reason. The gate records the estimator class of each
series in a manifest and declares the check inapplicable rather than returning a
verdict on it.

That scoping has a consequence worth stating rather than leaving to the
manifest: on this panel tail reach returns \emph{inapplicable} for all
\nGateSeries{} series, because none of the shipped forecast files stores sampled
paths. It contributes no verdict to any of the blocks reported below, and the
gate that operates here is nine checks wide, not ten. A check that cannot return
a verdict on the population it is run against carries no evidence when that
population passes --- the same reading this paper applies to the lower band edge
of Section~\ref{sec:blindspot}, and it is recorded for the same reason.

\section{What Recalibration Restores}
\label{sec:recal}

\subsection{Static Recalibration}
\label{sec:recalib}

Marginal coverage is restored across the panel. \nMainGreen{} of \nMainPairs{}
model--asset pairs reach the Basel Green Zone (\nMainGreenPct\%), and
unconditional coverage passes rise from \nMainKupiecRawPasses{} pairs to
\nMainKupiecCorPasses. Corrected violation rates cluster within a thousandth of
$\hat\pi = 0.010$ for every forecaster in Table~\ref{tab:master}, which is what
the guarantee promises, and it is the same fact that makes the corrected column
useless for ranking: a column on which every entry is at nominal separates
nothing. The restoration is indifferent to the forecaster, which is the result,
and indifference is not a property one can order by.

The ordering by $\bar R$ records the amount of correction each pair required.
For \nLitMainROkN{} of the \nMainForecasters{} forecasters it is between
$\nLitMainRMinAll$ and $\nLitMainRMaxOK$ --- the correction moves the threshold
by at most a third of its own size --- while the two series sampled at the
checkpoint default sit at $\nMainRTruncOne$ and $\nMainRTruncTwo$, where the
corrected forecast is an empirical fallback retaining nothing of the base
model's tail estimate. Green-zone compliance after correction therefore carries
no information about whether the corrected forecast retains any of the base
model's: the truncated series reach it as comfortably as the rest. The ordering
has structure at both ends and none in the middle, and the two largest
consecutive ratios fall in places that are not boundaries between kinds of
forecaster --- a factor of 84 near the \emph{bottom} of the range, separating the
dynamic-quantile forecasters whose raw coverage is already nominal, and a factor
of 48 at the top, separating Lag-Llama from the two truncated Chronos series.

\paragraph{What $\qVstat$ measures} $\qVstat$ is a measurement in the units of
the forecast, not a test statistic. It is an out-of-sample estimate of the
constant displacement minimising the quantile score --- the argmin of the
unconditional miscalibration term in the \citet{gneiting2023regression}
decomposition --- and it is that argmin, not the score reduction achieved there.
The two are distinct objects: the reduction is approximately
$\tfrac12 f \qVstat^2$ with $f$ the residual density at the $\alpha$-quantile,
so the map from one to the other runs through a factor that is specific to the
pair. On the window it is estimated on, $\qVstat$ is that displacement to a rank
correlation of \nMCBRhoCal{}; the residual is one order statistic and an
interpolation convention. Across the window boundary it is \nMCBRhoTest{} on the
\nMCBWellSpec{} well-specified pairs.

That gap is a statement about the estimator, not about the quantity it estimates,
and its size is predicted rather than merely named. The standard deviation of a
sample $\alpha$-quantile is $\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/n}\,/\,f$, so the same
density that converts a displacement into a score reduction also sets how
precisely the displacement itself can be estimated. Across the \nMCBDensityPairs{}
pairs on which $f$ is defined it spans a factor of \nMCBDensitySpan{} between its
5th and 95th percentiles, \nMCBDensityLo{} to \nMCBDensityHi, and the estimation
error tracks it: regressing $\log\lvert\qVstat - \delta^\star\rvert$ on $\log f$
gives a slope of $\nMCBGapSlope$ with standard error \nMCBGapSlopeSE, which is
\nMCBGapSlopeSigma{} standard errors from the theoretical $-1$, and the median
gap is \nMCBGapMagRatio{} times the predicted standard deviation with no fitted
constant. The collapse from \nMCBRhoCal{} to \nMCBRhoTest{} is sampling noise of
the size a sparse residual tail implies.

It does not follow, and is not true, that the density explains why $\qVstat$ and
the score reduction order the panel differently. Supplying each pair's own $f$
moves that rank correlation from \nMCBRhoPlain{} to \nMCBRhoConverted, which is
nothing: the conversion factor operates within a window and carries no
information across one. What the measurement licenses is one sentence:
\textbf{$\qVstat$ does not support an out-of-sample ordering between
forecasters.} The ordering by $\bar R$ in Table~\ref{tab:master} is a description
of how much correction each pair required on its own calibration window, and it
is presented as one.

\paragraph{Conditional coverage, and where the test is unavailable} Conditional
coverage is a different matter, and the reason is partly that the test cannot be
run. The Christoffersen independence statistic is degenerate when no two
exceedances fall on consecutive days, because the transition probability is then
estimated at the boundary of the parameter space and the $\chi^2_1$ calibration
of the likelihood ratio does not hold. After correction at $\alpha = 0.01$ that
is the case for \nMainCCUndefined{} of the \nMainPairs{} pairs. On the
\nMainCCDefined{} pairs where the test is informative, \nMainCCPass{} pass, or
\nMainCCPassPct\%; counting a degenerate table as a pass would report
\nMainCCAsPassPct\% from the same evidence. At the $1\%$ level, on samples of
the length regulators use, the standard independence test has no evidence to
work with on more than half the panel, and Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} takes
that as a result rather than a caveat. Green Zone recovery is restored
unconditional coverage and nothing further; the structural reasons a scalar
shift cannot reshape conditional dynamics are in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\section{What It Costs, and When to Apply It}
\label{sec:cost}

\subsection{When the Correction Helps, and What It Costs}
\label{sec:when_to_apply}

Aggregate improvements conceal deteriorations, so the full distribution of
$\Delta\mathrm{QS} = \mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{raw}} -
\mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{corrected}}$ over model--asset pairs is reported rather than
its mean.

At $\alpha = 0.01$ the correction makes \nDegradedStatic{} of \nSeqPairs{} pairs
worse under the single-split estimator and \nDegradedRoll{} of \nSeqPairs{} worse
under the rolling estimator. The deteriorations are not scattered: they
concentrate on pairs whose raw forecast was already well calibrated, where the
correction has nothing to remove and adds the estimation noise of $\qVstat$. Of
the \nWellCalStaticN{} pairs whose raw forecast passes Kupiec on the test set,
the single-split correction degrades \nWellCalStaticWorse{} (mean
$\Delta\mathrm{QS} = \nWellCalStaticMeanPct\%$ of $\mathrm{QS}_{\mathrm{raw}}$,
Wilcoxon $p = \nWellCalStaticWilcoxon$) and the rolling variant degrades
\nWellCalRollWorse, with mean $\nWellCalRollMeanPct\%$ and
$p \approx \nWellCalRollWilcoxon$. The rolling estimator re-estimates the shift on a 250-day
window and is correspondingly noisier, which is why its deterioration rate is
roughly double at every level. The pattern holds at
$\alpha \in \{0.025, 0.05, 0.10\}$, where it applies to a larger share of the
panel because more raw forecasts are already acceptable at those levels.

Whether a deterioration matters depends on whether it buys anything. Of the
\nDegradedRoll{} pairs the rolling estimator degrades, \nDegradedRollZoneUp{}
move from Yellow to Green --- a trade a risk manager can accept --- while
\nDegradedRollNoChange{} obtain no zone improvement: \nDegradedRollAlreadyGreen{}
were already Green, \nDegradedRollSameNotGreen{} was not and stays where it is,
and \nDegradedRollZoneDown{} move from Green to Yellow and worsen on both axes. On \nDegradedRollNoChangePct\% of the degradations the correction is
therefore not a trade-off but the wrong intervention.

This suggests a deployment rule that requires no classification: apply the
correction when the raw forecast fails a backtest (Basel zone worse than Green,
or Kupiec rejected), and not otherwise. \emph{Which} backtest matters, and the
distinction is not cosmetic.

Gated on the evaluation window, the rule looks excellent: at $\alpha = 0.01$ it
applies to \nGateRollOracleApplied{} of \nSeqPairs{} pairs, skips
\nGateRollOracleSkipped, avoids \nGateRollOracleAvoided{} of the
\nDegradedRoll{} rolling deteriorations --- \nGateRollOracleAvoidedPct\% of the
damage --- and retains \emph{all} \nGateRollOracleUpgrades{} Basel zone
upgrades. But that rule reads the window it is then scored on. It is an oracle,
and it stands as an upper bound on what gating can buy, not as a rule.

The deployable rule gates on the calibration window, which is the information
available on the day the decision is taken. It is materially weaker. At
$\alpha = 0.01$ it applies to \nGateRollApplied{} pairs and skips only
\nGateRollSkipped; it avoids \nGateRollAvoided{} deteriorations,
\nGateRollAvoidedPct\% of the damage rather than half; and it costs
\nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of the \nGateRollUpgrades{} zone upgrades, so the claim
that gating is free on the regulatory axis does not survive contact with the
constraint.

That cost is stated in the zone because the zone is what the rule is keyed on,
and it is worth pricing in something else, because Proposition~\ref{prop:zone_inclusion}
says the zone resolves a sign. Measured on the two quantities that are not
functions of the violation count, the \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} upgrades given up
divide: all \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} would have moved closer to nominal, and
\nGateRollUpgradesLostWorse{} of them would have paid for that with a
\emph{worse} quantile score. The net cost of the rule is therefore
\nGateRollUpgradesLostNet{} pairs, not \nGateRollUpgradesLost, on the only
measure here that does not read the exceedance count. The rule is better than
its own accounting in zones made it look, and the discrepancy is not an
accident of this panel: a benefit counted in a sign cannot distinguish a pair
that gains from one that pays for the gain elsewhere. Under the single split the deployable rule avoids
\nGateStaticAvoided{} of the \nDegradedStatic{} deteriorations and costs
\nGateStaticUpgradesLost{} of \nGateStaticUpgrades{} upgrades.

The deployable summary is therefore weaker than an in-sample evaluation of the
same rule would support, and still useful: a calibration-window backtest carries enough
signal to avoid about a quarter of the damage at a cost of about a tenth of the
zone upgrades, and the gap between that and the oracle is a measure of how
poorly the calibration window predicts the test window at $\alpha = 0.01$ ---
which is the tail-sparsity constraint of Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} showing
up in a decision problem rather than in an estimator. The raw rejection rate
falls steadily with the level --- \nSeqKupiecRejRawOne\%,
\nSeqKupiecRejRawTwoFive\%, \nSeqKupiecRejRawFive\% and
\nSeqKupiecRejRawTen\% at the four levels --- so at $\alpha = 0.10$ a clear
majority of raw forecasts \emph{pass} and the rule skips most of the panel. It
binds at every level, on a growing share of pairs as $\alpha$ falls; what is
specific to $\alpha = 0.01$ is not how often it fires but the size of the damage
it avoids when it does. The decision problem is therefore sharpest in the extreme
tail, which is where the identification constraint of
Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity} operates.

\subsection{The trailing window, and why the three settings are not a curve}
\label{sec:window}

Section~\ref{sec:cqr_var} leaves this section a debt: what the shift does at
$w = 125$. The answer is arithmetic before it is empirical. Equation~\eqref{eq:qV}
sets $k = \lceil (w+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$, and $k \ge w$ whenever
$w < 2/\alpha - 1$, so at $\alpha = 0.01$ every window of \nWSweepBound{}
observations or fewer makes the shift the \emph{maximum} of its window. At the
three settings a reader would compare, $k$ is \nWSweepShortK, \nWSweepMidK{} and
\nWSweepLongK: the maximum, the second largest, and the fifth largest.

The consequence is not that the shortest window is noisier. It is
Remark~\ref{rem:effective_level}: the three do not estimate the same quantity.
Their effective levels are $\nWSweepShortLevel$, $\nWSweepMidLevel$ and
$\nWSweepLongLevel$ against a nominal $0.99$, so the overshoot falls from
$\nWSweepShortOvershoot$ to $\nWSweepLongOvershoot$ across the sweep, a factor of
five, and at $w = 125$ it sits at the remark's upper bound of
$\nLevelMaxOvershoot$ because the estimator there is the window maximum.

Over the \nWSweepCells{} cells of the sequence panel, the shift and the corrected
rate both move with the overshoot rather than against the window length: the
median shift falls from $\nWSweepShortShift$ to $\nWSweepLongShift$ and the
median corrected rate rises from $\nWSweepShortPi$ to $\nWSweepLongPi$, toward
nominal from below. The shift at $w = 125$ is the larger of the two on
\nWSweepShiftLargerShort{} of \nWSweepCells{} cells.

Dispersion confirms that no variance curve is being read. Halving a window
multiplies the standard deviation of an interior order statistic by
$\sqrt 2 = \nWSweepSqrtTwo$; the observed ratios are $\nWSweepSdRatioShort$ from
$w = 250$ to $w = 125$ and $\nWSweepSdRatioLong$ from $w = 500$ to $w = 250$.
Written as $\mathrm{sd} \propto w^{-b}$, the implied exponent is
$\nWSweepExpShort$ over the first step and $\nWSweepExpLong$ over the second. A
variance curve returns one exponent, and $0.5$ at that; this returns neither, at
either end.

The ablation requested of $w \in \{125, 250, 500\}$ is therefore answered by
saying that its three points are not comparable as a curve, and the degenerate
estimator at $w = 125$ is the extreme case of a confound present at all three.
No window recommendation follows, and the reason is worth stating in the form a
practitioner would meet it. Choosing the window by corrected coverage selects
$w = 500$, whose rate sits closest to nominal here. But $w = 500$ is the setting
with the smallest overshoot, not the setting whose estimator is best calibrated,
and the corrected rate moves with the overshoot. A reader who selects on that
criterion is optimising the confound rather than the property: the ranking it
produces is a ranking of effective levels, and it would come out the same on a
panel where every window estimated equally well. Separating the two would require
holding $k/w$ fixed as $w$ varies, which equation~\eqref{eq:qV} does not permit,
so the sweep is reported and no setting is recommended from it.

\subsection{Static versus rolling recalibration}
\label{sec:static_vs_rolling}

The rolling variant re-estimates the shift on a trailing 250-day window, so
$R_t = |\qVroll|/|\widehat{\VaR}^{\mathrm{raw}}_t|$ becomes a monitored series
rather than a single number. It improves unconditional coverage substantially and
conditional adequacy not at all: Basel green rises from 89\% to 99\% of the
\nSeqPairs{} pairs, while Christoffersen pass rates are unchanged within noise,
which is a structural limitation of scalar recalibration rather than a tuning
question (Section~\ref{sec:failure}). The per-forecaster comparison, the
class-level breakdown and the single exception --- Historical Simulation, whose
raw threshold is an order statistic of the same trailing window --- are in
Supplement~S.12.
\subsection{Multiple levels, and the configuration contrast}
\label{sec:multiquantile}

Evaluated at four levels the panel separates the two Chronos configurations more
sharply than the 1\% table does, and it does so in the rejection counts rather
than the violation rates: corrected rates sit near nominal at every level for
every series, which is what the guarantee delivers and why the corrected column
cannot rank anything. Read analytically, Chronos-Small is rejected on \nMainKupRejCorSmallAnalyticOne,
\nMainKupRejCorSmallAnalyticTwoFive, \nMainKupRejCorSmallAnalyticFive{} and
\nMainKupRejCorSmallAnalyticTen{} of \nMainAssets{} assets across $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.10\}$ --- all
four counts on the \emph{corrected} series --- while the same weights sampled at
the checkpoint default give \nMainKupRejCorSmallDefaultOne, \nMainKupRejCorSmallDefaultTwoFive,
\nMainKupRejCorSmallDefaultFive{} and \nMainKupRejCorSmallDefaultTen. Recalibration at one level cannot
repair a predictive distribution that is too narrow at all of them
(Table~S.13).

The comparison that belongs in the body rather than a robustness appendix is the
configuration one, because it is the only contrast in this paper that holds the
model fixed and varies only the reduction step. On identical weights, contexts
and dates, reading the Chronos predictive distribution analytically beats
sampling it at the checkpoint default: mean corrected quantile score
$\nLitQSAnalyticSmall \times 10^{-4}$ against $\nLitQSDefaultSmall \times
10^{-4}$ for Chronos-Small and $\nLitQSAnalyticMini \times 10^{-4}$ against
$\nLitQSDefaultMini \times 10^{-4}$ for Chronos-Mini, both at $p < 0.001$. These figures are computed on the dates the
two configurations share, so they differ slightly from the per-asset averages of
Table~\ref{tab:master}, which run over each series' full test window. They are
the \emph{corrected} series, so the difference survives a recalibration that has
already restored coverage for both.
Cross-family score rankings are reported in Supplement~S.12 and no conclusion
here rests on them.

At the panel level the two cluster-robust procedures in this paper disagree, and
the disagreement is the informative part. The pooled unconditional test rejects
four forecasters at the 5\% level --- EWMA, Moirai~2.0, Chronos-Small read
analytically and GJR-GARCH, at pooled rates of $\nPoolPiEWMA$,
$\nPoolPiMoiraiTwo$, $\nPoolPiSmallAnalytic$ and $\nPoolPiGJR$ against a
nominal $0.01$. Allowing dependence within an asset through a Driscoll--Kraay
cluster statistic leaves one rejection, EWMA at $p = \nPoolClusterEWMA$; a
Rademacher wild-cluster bootstrap over the same \nMainAssets{} clusters with
$B = 999$ replications leaves none, EWMA coming closest at
$p = \nPoolBootEWMA$. Neither
procedure is the wrong one. With \nMainAssets{} clusters the bootstrap
distribution is built from \nMainAssets{} sign flips, its resolution near the
5\% boundary is coarse, and a forecaster sitting at $\nPoolClusterEWMA$ under
one and $\nPoolBootEWMA$ under the other has not been shown to be either adequately or
inadequately calibrated. What the comparison establishes is narrower than either
verdict: the pooled rejections are not robust to allowing within-asset
dependence, and should not be read as evidence of miscalibration
(Table~S.14).

\subsection{Alternatives to the one-parameter shift}
\label{sec:baselines}

Ten alternatives, a five-process Monte Carlo study and five robustness checks are
in Supplement~S.2, S.4 and S.12. One result from them bears on the argument here:
performance across the alternatives is governed less by flexibility than by
whether the recalibration layer respects the tail-sparsity constraint of
Remark~\ref{rem:tail_sparsity}, and the one-parameter shift has the best quantile
score of the nine alternatives that report one and of the raw baseline.
Isotonic regression is the tenth and reports none: its step-function estimate is
unstable or undefined at the 1\% tail for most pairs, which Table~S.25
records rather than fills in.
\subsection{Capital arithmetic, and what it now shows}
\label{sec:economic}

Under the Basel~II/pre-FRTB internal-models framework the traffic-light zone sets
a capital multiplier $k \in [3,4]$. With $W$ the widening of the corrected
threshold relative to an uncorrected benchmark, the capital ratio between a
corrected Green-zone forecast and an uncorrected Yellow-zone one is
$(k_{\mathrm{Green}}/k_{\mathrm{Yellow}})\,W$, so correction pays whenever
$W < k_{\mathrm{Yellow}}/k_{\mathrm{Green}} \approx 1.22$. Under FRTB the binding
measure is ES at 97.5\% and this arithmetic describes the backtesting channel
rather than the capital channel; we report it as illustrative.

Nothing in this panel comes close to the break-even widening. Relative to
GJR-GARCH the corrected thresholds run from $W = \nLitWMin$ for the analytic Chronos series to $W = \nLitWMax$ for Lag-Llama, with TimesFM~2.5 at $\nWidthRatioTimesFM$ and
Moirai~2.0 at $\nWidthRatioMoiraiTwo$. Correction is therefore capital-cheap
across the board, and the backtesting channel rather than the sharpness cost is
what governs the arithmetic here.

The identification result of Section~\ref{sec:blindspot} bears on this
arithmetic directly, and the statement does not depend on foundation models or on
any forecaster in this panel. Two models can report thresholds nearly a factor of
two apart --- one at $\nPairVaRHonest\sigma_t$, the other at
$\nPairVaRAlt\sigma_t$, both stated in units of the true conditional scale
rather than an estimate of it --- while exceeding on
the same days at the same rate, passing Kupiec, Christoffersen and
the dynamic quantile test with the same verdicts, and sitting in the same Basel
zone. The second holds \nPairCapitalPct\% of the capital of the first, is fully compliant
on the traffic light, and is not distinguishable from it by any test of a
predictable moment restriction on the exceedance sequence. A capital requirement
is a function of a reported threshold and a violation count; the pair shows that
those two numbers do not determine the threshold's adequacy. What separates them
is the reported quantile measured against an estimated scale --- the structural
check of Section~\ref{sec:gate}, applied before any of this arithmetic is run.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discuss}
\label{sec:failure}

\subsection{What a scalar shift cannot do}

A scalar shift restores unconditional violation frequency but cannot reshape the
conditional dynamics of the violation process; the rolling variant captures time
variation in the magnitude of miscalibration but remains a location adjustment.
State-dependent recalibration --- one-sided adaptive conformal inference
\citep{gibbs2021adaptive}, the PID framework of \citet{angelopoulos2024pid} ---
lies outside the present framework. Identification is limited in a second sense:
the positive cross-sectional association between $\qVstat$ and asset-level
volatility (Supplement~S.5.1) is consistent with pre-training domain mismatch,
but the zero-shot design does not permit causal attribution. The audit locates
\emph{where} tail signal is missing, not \emph{why}.

Operationally, small calibration sets ($n_{\mathrm{cal}} < 200$) produce unstable
corrections, very high score persistence weakens the mixing bound, and structural
breaks cause temporary under-protection when the calibration window predates the
break. The trailing-window design produces a measurable lag: around the COVID-19
shock \nLagImmediate{} of the \nLagForecasters{} forecasters reach their
post-shock maximum within the acute window, \nLagMidN{} peak \nLagMidDays{}
calendar days later, and the \nLagLateN{} GJR-GARCH variants take
\nLagLateDays{} days. Mitigations and the drift diagnostic that flags the regime in real
time are in Supplement~S.9.6.

\subsection{What the structural gate cannot do}
\label{sec:gate_limits}

The gate's limit is a bound rather than an anecdote. Its scale band blocks a
correctly specified forecaster only if that forecaster's standardised innovation
law places its own $1\%$ quantile above the upper edge $\nGapBandNow$, which
requires a tail lighter than a triangular law's $\nMLQuantTriangular$; Normal
innovations sit at $\nMLQuantNormal$ and standardised $t_5$ at $\nMLQuantTFive$,
and only a bounded law as light as the uniform, at $\nMLQuantUniform$, is caught.
For daily returns that region is empty in practice, so the band's false-positive
exposure is bounded rather than unknown.

What that bound does not cover is the range between the two populations of this
panel, and there the evidence is one estimator family and \nMLAssets{} assets:
of the \nMLGbmCells{} gradient-boosted cells, the gate blocks \nMLGbmBlocked, of
which \nMLBlockedUnder{} exceed at \nMLUnderThreshold{} times nominal or more,
and it passes no cell exceeding at that rate (Section~\ref{sec:gate_residual}).
That is one family on \nMLAssets{} of \nMainAssets{} assets, measured at
\nMLDates{} dates where a coverage ratio moves in steps of \nMLPiGrid, and it is
the only out-of-sample evidence the gate has. A second family sitting in the same
range and passing while under-covering would overturn it, and nothing here
excludes one.

\subsection{Three absences of verdict, reported as verdicts}
\label{sec:absences}

Three checks in this paper return no evidence and return it in the form that
reads as a pass, and they are one failure rather than three: the Christoffersen
independence test is degenerate on \nMainCCUndefined{} of the \nMainPairs{} pairs
after correction, tail reach is inapplicable on all \nGateSeries{} gated series
because no shipped forecast file stores sampled paths, and the lower edge of the
gate's own scale band blocks \nGapCellsLowerBlocks{} of \nGapCellsTotal{} cells.
A statistic that cannot be computed, a check with no object, and a bound nothing
in the panel approaches all report the same thing --- absence of evidence --- in
a format a reader counts as evidence of adequacy. The third is one of this
paper's own ten checks, which is what makes the sentence worth writing: the gate
is not exempt from the argument it is built on, and the block count in
Section~\ref{sec:gate} is nine checks exercised and one that was not.

\subsection{What the verification found, and where}
\label{sec:harness_limits}

The estimator this paper offers as a remedy carried a defect of the kind the
paper is about. The analytic Chronos quantiles paired each bin's probability with
the next bin up, a uniform one-bin translation of the support, and the validation
that accompanied them could not have seen it: a translation leaves the predictive
standard deviation exactly invariant, and the violation rates were compared on
\nLitAnalyticDates{} dates where the rate moves in steps of \nLitAnalyticGrid.
It was found by rebuilding the estimator from its own description rather than by
re-running it, and the corrected figures are throughout.

Two further defects sat in the verification apparatus itself, and both were found
the same way. Of the defects this project's computation protocol records,
\nHarnessDefectsInstrument{} of \nHarnessDefectsTotal{} were in a check and
\nHarnessDefectsObject{} in the quantity being checked. That ratio is not an
aside. A validation instrument is an estimator like any other, it fails in the
same way a coverage test does --- by returning a pass that is an absence of
evidence --- and it fails more often than the objects it validates. This is the
paper's own argument measured on the paper: it is why a coverage test cannot
carry the validation of a forecast series by itself, and why each check in the
replication material carries a negative control that must be seen to fail before
the check may report anything.

One finding ran the other way, and it is the reason verification is worth the
cost rather than a tax on it. The effective-level overshoot of
Remark~\ref{rem:effective_level} was not derived and then confirmed. It appeared
twice, from opposite directions --- once in a Monte Carlo control written to
check that a harness returned zero on a correctly specified forecaster, where it
did not, and once in a window sweep run to answer a referee's request for a
variance curve, where the curve turned out not to exist. Neither exercise was
looking for it. The overshoot is a property of equation~\eqref{eq:qV} that was
visible in the algebra all along and was noticed only when two unrelated checks
returned the same anomaly, which is verification producing a result rather than
repairing one.

\section{Implications for Deployment}
\label{sec:practitioner}

\emph{Structural validation precedes recalibration.} A recalibration decision
taken on a series that has not been structurally checked is a decision about an
artefact, and the correction will make it look correct
(Section~\ref{sec:gate}).

\emph{The correction has an indication.} The shift is warranted when the raw
forecast fails a backtest \emph{on the calibration window}, and not otherwise. At
$\alpha = 0.01$ that rule skips \nGateRollSkipped{} of \nSeqPairs{} pairs, avoids
\nGateRollAvoided{} of the \nDegradedRoll{} degradations the rolling estimator
would otherwise cause, and costs \nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of
\nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades. It is not free on the regulatory axis,
and a version that appears to be free is one evaluated on the window it used to
decide.

\emph{The operative criterion is the estimation constraint, not the model class.}
With adequate per-asset history, the GJR-GARCH family with conformal correction
attains the best corrected quantile score in this panel, and the two variants
within it are not distinguishable: their mean corrected scores differ by
\nPairQSGapPct\%, the ordering reverses between per-asset and pooled aggregation,
and a Diebold--Mariano test on the corrected loss differentials with a
Driscoll--Kraay panel-HAC variance --- cross-sectional mean per date, Bartlett
kernel at \nPairDMLags{} lags --- returns $t = \nPairDMt$, $p = \nPairDMp$ over
\nMainAssets{} assets. The sign of that statistic is itself a function of the
weighting: equal weight on dates favours one variant and equal weight on
observations the other, which is the same reversal the per-asset and pooled
averages show, arriving a third time. The family is therefore the identified unit, not a winner
within it. Where per-asset estimation is infeasible --- scale, short history,
latency --- a correctly configured zero-shot foundation model with the same
correction is competitive with the GARCH family at the 1\% level, which is an
operational option rather than a claim of superiority.

\emph{The signed shift carries the diagnosis; the ratio does not.} $\qVstat$ is
negative when the base forecast is over-conservative and the correction tightens
it, releasing capital, and positive in the reverse case. $\bar R$ cannot carry
that information, being a ratio of absolute values, which is why the two are
reported separately throughout. In this panel negative values are rare and belong
to no single forecaster --- three assets for TimesFM~2.5, two for Moirai~2.0, one
each for Moirai~1.1 and GJR-GARCH-$t$ --- while GJR-GARCH is positive on all
\nMainAssets{} assets (\nGJRnegqV{} negative). A forecaster that is
systematically over-conservative across an entire asset universe warrants
inspection at the level of its quantile map before the pattern is read as a
modelling choice: the Student-$t$ failure mode of
Table~S.31 presents exactly that way.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}

A Value-at-Risk forecast is produced by code, and the step in which a predictive
distribution becomes a threshold determines what the resulting series is. In this
panel that step --- not the architecture of the model, nor the format in which it
exposes its distribution --- is what separates a forecaster that is roughly
usable at the 1\% level from one that violates on four days in ten. The clearest
instance is a documented sampling default in a public checkpoint, correct for the
task it was written for and silently wrong for this one, and the mechanism is
established by controlled experiment rather than inferred: dispersion recovers
monotonically as the truncation is relaxed, one thousand draws under the default
contain exactly fifty distinct values, and the parameters a careful analyst would
vary move nothing.

The result that generalises beyond this panel is an identification result. A
coverage backtest reports that a series miscovers and cannot report why, and the
sources it cannot distinguish --- a poor tail model, a truncated predictive
support, an inverted sign convention, a misalignment, a stale vintage --- call
for different remedies. At $\alpha = 0.01$ the instruments are additionally at
their weakest: the independence test is degenerate on a third of raw pairs and on
more than half of recalibrated ones, where its asymptotics do not hold, while
unconditional coverage rejects seven pairs in ten and so carries little
information about any one of them. Post-hoc recalibration does not repair this
and makes it harder to see, because a guarantee that holds regardless of the
forecaster cannot diagnose the forecaster: applied to a truncated series it
returns $\hat\pi = \nTruncCorPi$ and \nTruncCorGreen{} of \nMainAssets{} Basel
green zones. Validation that runs after a correction is not validation of the
model.

We therefore propose checking forecast series structurally, against properties
they must have to be Value-at-Risk series at all, before they are calibrated or
evaluated. Ten such checks are cheap, and seven of them need nothing but the
forecast series and the returns: tail reach needs the model's sampled paths, and
$\alpha$-responsiveness and coverage plausibility need an evaluation window, so
the gate is not entirely upstream of the data a backtest consumes. Together they
block \nGateBlocked{} of the \nGateSeries{} series here. Two are invariants, one is an invariant tested through a proxy, and seven
are plausibility bands with stated rather than derived thresholds; all ten were
specified after the failure modes they address had been identified, so their
performance on those failure modes is in-sample by construction. The argument for
them is not their measured sensitivity but that the alternative provably cannot
do this job.

On recalibration itself the claims are narrow. The conformal shift restores
marginal coverage under $\beta$-mixing dependence --- asymptotically, with an
explicit remainder, and under a gap-separation condition that the contiguous
split used here does not satisfy (Section~\ref{sec:theory_gap}) --- and that
is all it does. It is an intervention with an indication rather than a default
step: under the rolling estimator, \nWellCalRollWorse{} of the \nWellCalRollN{}
pairs that pass Kupiec on the test window are degraded by correcting them, and
gating the correction on a calibration-window backtest avoids
\nGateRollAvoided{} of the \nDegradedRoll{} deteriorations at the cost of
\nGateRollUpgradesLost{} of \nGateRollUpgrades{} Basel zone upgrades --- half the
benefit that the same rule appears to deliver when it is allowed to read the
window it is scored on. The shift reaches only part of the problem: in the
decomposition of \citet{gneiting2023regression} a constant correction addresses a
median of \nuMCBSharePct\% of total miscalibration on well-specified series, the
remainder being conditional, and it does not restore violation independence. The
magnitude $\bar R$ is retained as a scale-interpretable statement of how much of
a forecast survives correction, not as an independent diagnostic; among
well-specified forecasters it is a rank-preserving transform of the violation
rate.

What the panel says about foundation models is deflationary. Configured
correctly, the zero-shot series here are neither catastrophically miscalibrated
nor superior: they sit alongside the GARCH-family benchmarks at the 1\% level, a
per-asset CAViaR fitted the ordinary way outperforms all of them, and the
residual miscalibration of the best of them is concentrated in the tail and
largely gone by $\alpha = 0.10$. Whether fine-tuning closes that tail gap remains
open. What this paper does close, in our view, is the question of whether a
tail-risk evaluation can be trusted without a structural check of the series it
evaluates.

\section*{Data and Code Availability}

The full replication pipeline --- data retrieval, foundation-model inference, conformal calibration, backtesting, robustness analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, and figure generation --- together with machine-readable result matrices for all 384 model--asset pairs ($\qVstat$, $\hat\pi$, Basel Traffic Light, Kupiec $p$, Christoffersen $p$, Quantile Score), is available via the Quantlet repository \quantlet{https://github.com/QuantLet/Conformal_Oracle/} at \url{https://github.com/QuantLet/Conformal_Oracle}.

Return data are retrieved from Yahoo Finance. All foundation model checkpoints are open-weight and hosted on HuggingFace; no proprietary API access is required.

A Python package implementing the framework, \texttt{conformal-oracle}, is available on PyPI (\url{https://pypi.org/project/conformal-oracle/}) and installable via \texttt{pip install conformal-oracle}. Every rolling-mode result reported here was produced by the Quantlet replication pipeline, which computes the conformal correction as the finite-sample split-conformal quantile, the $\lceil(w+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th order statistic of the trailing-window scores. The shift is defined once, in \texttt{cfp\_config.conformal\_quantile}, and \texttt{scripts/audit\_qv\_convention.py} enumerates every site in the repository that takes a quantile of nonconformity scores, records the convention each uses, and fails the build when an undeclared one appears. Through version~0.3.1 of the \texttt{conformal-oracle} package the block-bootstrap confidence interval for $\qVstat$ computed its replicates with the plain empirical quantile while the point estimate used the order statistic; this is corrected and recorded in the package changelog.

Each series admitted to the analysis carries a manifest entry recording its model, asset, path, vintage and estimator class, and each passes --- or is recorded as blocked by --- the structural gate of Section~\ref{sec:gate} before entering any table. The numbers asserted in the text are emitted as macros by \texttt{scripts/paper\_numbers.py}, which recomputes each of them from the artefacts and fails the build when a value in the manuscript differs from the value in the data.

The verification apparatus was itself the more frequent site of defect. Of the \nHarnessDefectsTotal{} defects the project's computation protocol records, \nHarnessDefectsInstrument{} were in a check and \nHarnessDefectsObject{} in the quantity being checked: a literal screen that stripped every hand-authored table before reading it, a claim audit that read one generated number as evidence for both sides of a ratio, a manifest comparing against the frozen submission rather than the working tree, and a consistency check that regenerated the artefact it was verifying. The ratio is not incidental to the paper's subject. A validation instrument is an estimator like any other and fails in the same way the coverage test does --- by returning a pass that is an absence of evidence --- which is why every check in this repository carries a negative control that must be seen to fail before the check may report anything, and why the controls are planted where each check reads worst rather than where it reads typically.
